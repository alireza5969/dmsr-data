# Literature-based historical assessment of record-level reproducible screening in systematic reviews

## Executive conclusion — recommended starting year: **2021**

If your objective is to select a single, scientifically defensible year from which to **evaluate systematic reviews for availability of record-level screening information**, I recommend **2021**.

Importantly, I am **not** concluding that record-level screening datasets became common in 2021. The evidence suggests they did not. Rather, **2021 is the earliest defensible boundary at which the methodological, reporting, technological, and open-science environment had matured enough that preservation and sharing of underlying review materials could reasonably be evaluated as a reproducibility practice**.

I would therefore define your study period as:

> **Systematic reviews published from January 1, 2021 onward.**

The strongest reason *not* to choose an earlier date is empirical. A study of **530 systematic reviews published in 2015–2016** found that **none** completely reported a reproducible search-and-selection process and only **8/530 (1.5%)** provided a list of records excluded at title/abstract screening with reasons. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0895435617307424?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The strongest reason to choose 2021 is the convergence that occurred around that time: PRISMA 2020 explicitly introduced reporting of the availability of **data, analytic code, and other materials**; substantially expanded description of screening procedures and automation; PRISMA-S formalized reproducible search reporting; repositories and screening systems were mature; and systematic-review reproducibility itself had become an explicit methodological research topic. [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/372/bmj.n71.long?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

However, there is an important qualification: **neither PRISMA 2020 nor PRISMA-S actually requires publication of a complete title/abstract-level screening dataset.** Thus 2021 should be described as the beginning of the *modern reproducibility era for systematic reviews*, rather than the year in which complete screening logs became a universal reporting expectation.

# Why 2021?

There are really three historical eras.

**Before ~2010:** reporting standards increasingly required reviewers to explain *how many* studies were identified, screened, excluded, and included. They did not generally require publication of the underlying screening records.

**~2010–2020:** PRISMA became widespread; online supplements expanded; screening software began automatically recording record-level decisions; repositories and open-science infrastructure emerged. Nevertheless, actual publication of title/abstract screening decisions remained exceptional.

**From 2021:** reproducibility and sharing of review materials became explicitly integrated into mainstream systematic-review reporting guidance. PRISMA 2020 added item 27 concerning public availability of data, analytic code, and other materials and greatly expanded reporting about study selection. PRISMA-S simultaneously strengthened the expectation that the identification side of the process should be reproducible. [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/372/bmj.n71.long?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That combination makes **2021 much more defensible than 2009, 2014, 2016, or 2018** as the beginning of a study assessing *record-level reproducibility*.

# The critical distinction: PRISMA reporting ≠ reproducible screening

This is central to the validity of your study.

A **PRISMA flow diagram** tells you that, for example, 4,812 records were screened and 4,500 were excluded. It does not tell you *which* 4,500 records they were.

A **full-text exclusion table** might tell you that Smith 2017 was excluded for wrong population and Jones 2019 for wrong comparator. This permits auditing those particular decisions but says nothing about the thousands of records rejected at title/abstract screening.

Even current Cochrane guidance illustrates this distinction particularly clearly: the “Characteristics of excluded studies” table should contain studies readers could plausibly expect to have been included, but **should not contain every report identified by the search**. [Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

PRISMA 2020 similarly requires authors to cite studies that “might appear to meet the inclusion criteria” but were excluded and explain why. It does **not** require publication of every title/abstract-screened record. [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/372/bmj.n160?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Therefore:

**PRISMA compliance should not satisfy your eligibility criterion.**

# Empirical evidence of adoption

The quantitative evidence is much stronger for *conventional reporting* than for *record-level screening disclosure*.

The 2004-versus-2014 comparison by Page et al. provides excellent evidence of the former. Review-flow reporting increased from **42% to 78%**, and reporting reasons for full-text exclusion increased from **48% to 70%**. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1002028) This is compelling evidence that PRISMA-era selection reporting improved.

But it is not evidence that screening became reproducible.

Faggion et al. provide the more relevant observation: among 530 reviews from 2015–16, only **1.5%** reported title/abstract excluded articles with reasons, and none completely met their 12 criteria for reproducible search and selection. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0895435617307424?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Another methodological evaluation of influential periodontology reviews similarly found that **none provided a detailed list of articles excluded during title/abstract screening**, although about one-quarter provided excluded-article information after full-text assessment. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31141722/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is exceptionally important for your cutoff decision: **record-level title/abstract disclosure was still decidedly nonstandard in the middle of the 2010s.**

Even broader data sharing remained poor much later. Nguyen et al.'s cross-sectional meta-research found only **20/300 (7%)** reviews from 2020 publicly shared underlying meta-analysis data or analytic code. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9679891/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) A separate study of 300 intervention systematic reviews found only **29%** contained a data-availability statement, and only 12 statements identified downloadable data that could actually be verified. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0895435622000646?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So there is **no credible evidence of a historical moment before 2021 when complete screening datasets became routine**.

Indeed, a 2026 methodological paper still characterizes documentation of retrieved records and screening decisions as a persistent reproducibility problem and recommends explicitly preserving and exporting stage-specific record-level decisions. [BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/16/9/e123460?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That means your criterion is appropriately viewed as an **advanced transparency/open-science practice**, rather than ordinary PRISMA reporting.

# Why technology matters—but does not determine the cutoff

Screening software changed what was technically possible considerably earlier than reporting standards changed what was expected.

Cohen et al. were already using annotated record-level datasets in 2006 for automated classification research. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16357352/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Wallace et al. developed machine-learning approaches using labeled citation records in 2010. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2824679/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) EPPI-Reviewer 4 launched in 2010 and supported reference management, screening, coding, collaboration, and data export. [EPPI Centre](https://eppi.ioe.ac.uk/cms/er/Help/ER-4-Information?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Abstrackr followed in the early 2010s. [Abstrackr](https://www.abstrackr.com/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Rayyan's major publication appeared in 2016. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/5139140/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

These systems created an important transition:

**screening decisions changed from transient human judgments into structured digital data.**

That is historically significant.

But there is a second transition:

**structured screening data → publicly shared screening data.**

That transition occurred much later and remains incomplete.

Therefore, I would **not use the launch date of Covidence, Rayyan, EPPI-Reviewer, DistillerSR, Abstrackr, or another platform as your cutoff**. Software made reproducibility *possible*; it did not make public disclosure *standard*.

# Recommended operational definition

I recommend that you avoid the phrase **“reproducible screening process”** as a simple yes/no concept unless you define it very precisely. It can otherwise be confused with dual screening, reporting screening methods, or reproducibility of reviewers' judgments.

A stronger name would be:

### **Record-level reproducible study selection**

A systematic review should qualify only when the publication, supplementary materials, or a persistently accessible linked repository provides sufficient information to map **individual screened records to their final screening disposition**.

I would operationalize the primary criterion as:

> **Record-level reproducible study selection is present when sufficient information is publicly available to identify the individual records subjected to screening and determine, for each record, its disposition in the study-selection process, such that an independent researcher can reconstruct the record-level transition from the screened set to the included set.**

I would then distinguish four levels:

| Level | Available information | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| **0 — Aggregate only** | PRISMA counts, e.g. 5,000 screened/4,800 excluded | **Not reproducible** |
| **1 — Partial record-level transparency** | Included studies plus identified full-text exclusions/reasons | **Not reproducible for complete screening** |
| **2 — Record-level final decisions** | All screened unique records identifiable, with final include/exclude/uncertain or equivalent disposition | **Meets your core criterion** |
| **3 — Full screening audit trail** | Level 2 plus screening stage, individual reviewer decisions, disagreements/adjudication and exclusion reasons where recorded | **Fully auditable screening** |

For your **primary eligibility criterion**, I recommend **Level ≥2**.

That is important because requiring reasons for exclusion for *every title/abstract* would make your criterion substantially stricter than necessary. Many legitimate screening processes do not assign a formal exclusion reason at title/abstract stage.

Thus the minimum necessary fields should effectively be:

**record identifier/citation + screening disposition**.

Useful but nonessential enhancements are screening stage, exclusion reason, reviewer-specific votes, uncertainty/maybe status, conflict status, adjudicated decision, deduplication provenance, and source database.

For full-text exclusions, however, a reason should normally be expected because this has been a longstanding methodological reporting expectation and is explicit in PRISMA 2020. [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/372/bmj.n160?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

# An important methodological refinement

I would distinguish **reconstructability** from **independent reproducibility**.

Suppose a supplementary CSV contains:

> PMID 12345 — exclude  
> PMID 23456 — include  
> PMID 34567 — exclude

You can reconstruct what the original reviewers did.

But you cannot necessarily *reproduce their judgments* unless eligibility criteria are sufficiently explicit.

Therefore your study might measure two dimensions separately:

**Record-level reconstructability:** Can I identify the records and original decisions?

**Decision reproducibility:** Could an independent reviewer apply the reported eligibility criteria to those records and plausibly recreate the decisions?

The former is objectively measurable from publication materials and is, in my view, the better primary endpoint for your meta-research study.

The latter involves judgment and should probably be secondary.

This distinction is consistent with newer methodological thinking emphasizing that transparency is necessary for reproducibility but does not guarantee identical selection decisions. [BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/16/9/e123460?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

# Sensitivity analysis

I strongly recommend prespecifying **2021 as the primary cutoff**, with **2018 and 2023** as sensitivity analyses.

Using **2018** would test the earlier “emergence” period: AMSTAR 2 had just appeared, screening reproducibility had become an explicit methodological topic, Rayyan/EPPI/Abstrackr and similar systems existed, and open-science infrastructure was available. But you should expect a large number of reviews to fail simply because the practice was not yet normative.

Using **2021** answers the most defensible question:

> Once modern systematic-review reproducibility guidance and infrastructure were in place, how often did published reviews actually provide reconstructable record-level screening?

Using **2023** gives authors roughly two publication years after PRISMA 2020/PRISMA-S to adapt their practices. It is therefore the strongest conservative analysis if reviewers argue that publications appearing during 2021–22 may have been designed or conducted before PRISMA 2020.

If prevalence rises markedly from 2018 → 2021 → 2023, that itself would be a valuable finding.

If prevalence remains low across all three periods, your study would demonstrate something arguably more important: **the infrastructure and rhetoric of reproducible evidence synthesis developed considerably faster than actual sharing of screening data.**

# Why 2021 is not an arbitrary PRISMA date

The argument for 2021 is stronger when several independent developments are considered together.

PRISMA 2020 added an explicit item about availability of review data, analytic code, and other materials. [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/372/bmj.n71.long?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Its explanation and elaboration substantially expanded reporting requirements around who screened each record/report, independent screening, disagreement resolution, and automation. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8005925/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

PRISMA-S simultaneously formalized reproducible reporting of searches. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8270366/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

And the REPRISE project, published in 2021, provides especially strong conceptual support for **your exact notion**: its investigators proposed obtaining from original review authors a file containing all screening decisions, explicitly giving as examples an Excel or EndNote file listing **all titles and abstracts screened** and citations of full-text reports with the eligibility decision for each. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13643-021-01670-0?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is remarkably close to your proposed eligibility criterion.

Consequently, **2021 is not merely “the new PRISMA year.” It is the point at which several streams—reporting guidance, search reproducibility, open materials, screening technology, and explicit research into systematic-review replication—converged.**

## Recommended wording for your protocol

If I were writing the eligibility justification in your Methods section, I would use something close to this:

> **We restricted eligibility to systematic reviews published from 2021 onward. This cutoff was selected a priori to correspond to the emergence of contemporary expectations regarding reproducibility and availability of systematic-review materials, including PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA-S. Earlier reporting standards, including QUOROM and PRISMA 2009, substantially improved reporting of aggregate study-selection flow and reasons for exclusion but did not establish an expectation that the complete set of screened records and corresponding record-level decisions be made available. We therefore considered 2021 the earliest defensible point at which availability of record-level screening information could be evaluated as a contemporary reproducibility practice rather than an historically exceptional reporting practice.**

That is, in my assessment, substantially more defensible than saying **“we selected 2009 because PRISMA was introduced”** or choosing the launch year of a particular screening platform.

**Bottom line: use 2021 as the primary cutoff, define eligibility as Level ≥2 record-level reconstructability, and prespecify 2018 and 2023 as temporal sensitivity analyses.** The evidence does **not** support claiming that complete screening-data publication became routine in 2021; it supports 2021 as the earliest reasonable point to begin *expecting and measuring it as a modern reproducibility practice*.
