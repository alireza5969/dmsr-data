Efficacy and Safety of Bevacizumab in Pretreated Metastatic Breast Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Zhijun Li[a]

Fengzhu Guo[a][b]

Abstract

Background

The use of bevacizumab in patients with previously treated metastatic breast cancer (MBC) is controversial. This meta-analysis was carried out to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the regimen including bevacizumab among patients with pretreated MBC.

Methods

We systematically searched PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase databases for randomized controlled trials evaluating bevacizumab combined with chemotherapy for previously treated MBC patients. The primary endpoints were progression-free survival (PFS) and objective response rate (ORR). The secondary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and toxicity. The risk of bias was assessed by the Cochrane Collaboration's tool. The pooled hazard ratio (HR) and risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the identified studies. This study was performed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist.

Results

Four studies involving 1,640 individuals were included. Pooling results showed that the PFS of bevacizumab-containing groups (HR 0.82; 95% CI 0.73–0.93, p = 0.002) was significantly better than that of the control groups, especially when bevacizumab was administered as the second-line treatment for patients with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative MBC (HR 0.77; 95% CI 0.66–0.88, p = 0.0002). The ORR in the bevacizumab-containing group was superior to that in the control group, both in the general (RR 1.45; 95% CI 1.18–1.78, p = 0.0004) and HER2-negative groups (RR 1.30; 95% CI 1.03–1.63, p = 0.03). However, no significant effect on OS was demonstrated for the addition of bevacizumab to the second-line treatment for HER2-negative MBC (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.79–1.10, p = 0.39). Comparatively, proteinuria was more common in the bevacizumab-containing group. In addition, the application of bevacizumab tended to result in therapy discontinuation due to treatment-related toxicity.

Conclusions

Bevacizumab-containing chemotherapy, in light of its favorable effects on clinical outcomes, could be a preferred therapeutic option for patients with MBC, for whom the disease must be rapidly relieved. Further studies are warranted for exploring the advantageous patients with the receipt of bevacizumab in multiline treatment.

Introduction

Breast cancer has the leading cause of cancer incidence among female malignancies, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases worldwide in 2020 [1]. Approximately 20–30% of early breast cancer patients will experience recurrence and metastasis, and 3–10% of breast cancer patients have distant metastasis at the initial diagnosis [2, 3, 4]. Metastatic breast cancer (MBC) is not curable, and its therapeutic goals are prolonging survival and maintaining the quality of life. There is no standard regimen for advanced breast cancer patients who progress after first-line treatment. Currently, multidisciplinary treatment has been advocated for cancer patients to achieve more precise treatment. Practical strategies for breast cancer treatment include radiation therapy, surgery, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy. The median overall survival (OS) varies from 1 to 5 years with aggressive treatment across different molecular subtypes of MBC [5, 6, 7].

Bevacizumab is a humanized anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monoclonal antibody that slows the growth of new blood vessels by inhibiting VEGF-A, thereby controlling tumor growth [8, 9]. Three prior randomized phase 3 trials (RIBBON-1, E2100, and AVADO) showed that bevacizumab combined with standard chemotherapy was effective in the initial treatment of HER2-negative MBC, with significantly longer progression-free survival (PFS) in the combination group than in the chemotherapy alone group [10, 11, 12]. In February 2008, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved bevacizumab plus paclitaxel for first-line treatment in HER2-negative MBC. However, 2 years later, the FDA withdrew the indication for bevacizumab in MBC because long-term follow-up showed no benefit in OS and increased rates of serious adverse events (AEs). Subsequently, bevacizumab has been examined in neoadjuvant therapy, adjuvant therapy, and after first-line treatment for breast cancer to expand its indications. However, conflicting outcomes exist [13, 14, 15, 16].

Therefore, we performed this systematic review and meta-analysis to explore the efficiency and safety of the bevacizumab-containing regimen in pretreated patients with MBC. To our knowledge, this is the first relevant meta-analysis to be conducted. This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist (available in the online suppl. material at www.karger.com/doi/10.1159/000525882).

Methods

Literature Search

We searched PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating bevacizumab combined with chemotherapy for patients with previously treated MBC. The PICOS principle was used, and the keywords “bevacizumab” and “metastatic breast cancer” were searched. We used the Medical Subject Heading terms and the text words, for example, “Bevacizumab”[Mesh] OR Avastin[tw] AND “Neoplasm Metastasis”[Mesh] OR “Neoplasm Metastases”[tw] OR Metastases[tw] OR Metastasis[tw] AND “Breast Neoplasms”[Mesh] OR “Breast Neoplasm”[tw] OR “Breast Tumor*”[tw] OR “Breast Cancer”[tw] OR “Mammary Cancer*”[tw] OR “Breast Malignant Neoplasm*”[tw] OR “Breast Malignant Tumor*”[tw] OR “Human Mammary Carcinoma*”[tw] OR “Breast Carcinoma*”[tw]. Only human studies were allowed, and the latest search was performed on October 15, 2021. There was no language restriction.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) RCTs, (2) breast cancer diagnosed by pathology or imaging with distant metastasis, (3) patients were assigned to either chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab or chemotherapy alone, and (4) MBC received at least one prior therapy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a retrospective study, cohort study, and other non-RCT research, (2) single-arm design, (3) patients receiving first-line therapy, and (4) original data were unavailable.

Data Extraction

NoteExpress 3.5 (http://www.inoteexpress.com/aegean/) was used to check for duplicate literature. Two authors (Z.L. and F.G.) independently screened and selected the remaining literature according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Then, the following data were extracted: trial name; trial phase; first author; year of publication; country; sample size; regimens; toxicities; hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the PFS, OS, and objective response rate (ORR).

The risk of bias in the study was assessed by the Cochrane Collaboration's tool, which includes the following items: allocation concealment, random sequence generation, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, blinding, and other biases. We independently assessed trial quality (Z.L. and F.G.), and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. When necessary, a third reviewer (J.W.) was consulted to reach a consensus.

Statistical Analysis

The RevMan Version 5.4 software (https://community.cochrane.org/help/tools-and-software) was used to perform this systematic assessment. The primary endpoints were PFS and ORR. The secondary endpoints were OS and toxicity. Forest plots presented the pooled clinical outcomes, and dichotomous variables were compared by calculating the risk ratio (RR) and 95% CI. Heterogeneity was assessed visually using forest plots and the I2 statistic. An I2 > 50% indicated statistically significant heterogeneity, and in such cases, random-effects models were used for analysis. Otherwise, fixed-effects models were applied. A funnel plot was generated to evaluate publication bias. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The filtering procedure is shown in Figure 1. Four RCTs were identified, including phase 3 clinical trials [13, 17, 18, 19]. Based on the same trial, von Minckwitz et al. [18] and Vrdoljak et al. [19] performed research on the same population at different stages. The former mainly reported PFS and toxicities, and the latter primarily reported the final OS. Table 1 shows the basic characteristics of the included studies, and Figure 2 illustrates the assessment of Cochrane's risk of bias.

Summary of Enrolled Studies

One thousand six hundred forty individuals, including 938 and 702 participants, were enrolled in the intervention and control groups. A study conducted by Miller et al. [13] was the first randomized phase 3 study of antiangiogenic therapy in MBC patients. It was designed to evaluate the benefits and risks of capecitabine with bevacizumab versus capecitabine alone in MBC patients prior treated with taxane and anthracycline. RIBBON-2 was a multicenter, phase 3 trial, and it compared the benefits and risks of standard chemotherapy regimens with or without bevacizumab as second-line therapy in HER2-negative MBC patients. TANIA was designed to explore the benefits of second-line bevacizumab in MBC patients who had disease progression on first-line bevacizumab plus chemotherapy. The above three studies are all phase 3 trials. The RIBBON-2 and TANIA studies enrolled MBC patients with HER2-negative status, while the AVF2119g study enrolled MBC patients regardless of HER2 status. Bevacizumab was administered at 10 or 15 mg/kg every 2 or 3 weeks, respectively.

Progression-Free Survival

The AVF2119g study showed no significant difference in PFS between the two groups, while the RIBBON-2 and TANIA studies showed a statistically significant difference in PFS. The I2 statistic indicated nonsignificant heterogeneity (I2 = 45%), so we used the fixed-effects model for pooled study analysis. The results showed that the PFS (HR 0.82; 95% CI 0.73–0.93, Z = 3.17, p = 0.002) of the bevacizumab-containing groups was significantly better than that of the control groups (Fig. 3a), especially in the second-line treatment subgroup of HER2-negative MBC (Fig. 3b) (HR 0.77; 95% CI 0.66–0.88, Z = 3.67, p = 0.0002).

Overall Survival

The results of the three studies showed that there was no significant difference in OS between these two groups. Since there were no complete data on OS in the AVF2119g study, we performed a pooled analysis of the other two trials, in which all participants had HER2-negative MBC with second-line treatment. The I2 statistic indicated a lack of heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), so the fixed-effects model was applied. The results showed that whether bevacizumab was added to the second-line treatment had no effect on OS for HER2-negative MBC (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.79–1.10, Z = 0.86, p = 0.39) (Fig. 4).

Objective Response Rate

In the RIBBON-2 study, compared with the control group, the ORR of the bevacizumab-containing group improved by 10%, but it was not statistically significant. Consistently, the TANIA study showed that the bevacizumab-containing group had a similar ORR as the control group. However, the AVF2119g study confirmed a significant difference in ORR between the two groups. Using a fixed-effects model (I2 = 45%), the results showed a significantly higher ORR in the bevacizumab-containing group than in the control group, both in the general population (RR 1.45; 95% CI 1.18–1.78, Z = 3.52, p = 0.0004) (Fig. 5a) and the HER2-negative group (RR 1.30; 95% CI 1.03–1.63, Z = 2.24, p = 0.03) (Fig. 5b).

Safety

The pooled RRs of bevacizumab-associated grade 3/4 adverse effects are summarized in Table 2. Compared with the control group, the probability of proteinuria in bevacizumab-containing groups was significantly higher (RR 9.83; 95% CI 2.77–34.81, Z = 3.54, p = 0.0004), while the incidence of hypertension (RR 10.07; 95% CI 0.77–131.53, Z = 1.76, p = 0.08), bleeding (RR 1.3; 95% CI 0.41–4.10, Z = 0.45, p = 0.65), and thromboembolic events (RR 1.10; 95% CI 0.61–1.97, Z = 0.32, p = 0.75) in the two groups were similar. The application of bevacizumab tended to result in therapy discontinuation due to treatment-related toxicity, but the difference was not significant (HR 1.59; 95% CI 0.85–2.68, Z = 1.75, p = 0.08). Significant heterogeneity existed in this endpoint between these two groups, with I2 = 68%, so a random-effects model was applied (Fig. 6).

Publication Bias

Funnel plots of PFS, OS, and ORR are shown in Figure 7. In this meta-analysis, we found no obvious publication bias among the included studies.

Discussion

Currently, most studies of bevacizumab on MBC are aimed at first-line treatment [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Three classic phase 3 studies have shown that compared with chemotherapy alone, bevacizumab combined with chemotherapy as first-line treatment can significantly prolong the PFS of MBC patients but fails to improve the OS [10, 11, 12]. Based on the above studies, the FDA has successively approved and withdrawn the indications of bevacizumab for breast cancer. The status of bevacizumab in the first-line treatment of MBC has not yet been confirmed. For patients with progression after first-line treatment, the efficacy of bevacizumab has also been explored, with controversial results [26, 27, 28]. Therefore, a comprehensive study is warranted to summarize these results. This is the first meta-analysis to investigate the efficacy and safety of bevacizumab-containing regimens in patients with previously treated MBC.

Since there were few clinical trials for the application of bevacizumab in previously treated MBC patients, only four pieces of literature of 3 studies were included for analysis in this study. One of the studies reported the outcome through two pieces of literature. However, these three studies were of high quality, and they were all phase 3 randomized controlled clinical trials with high reliability. The control group was chemotherapy in the three studies, and the intervention group was chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab. As bevacizumab was used as the second-line or multiline treatment for MBC, no standard treatment regimens exist. Therefore, the chemotherapy regimens in these three studies were different, among which capecitabine accounted for a high proportion, followed by taxanes, gemcitabine, and anthracycline. In this study, the PFS, OS, and ORR of the three studies were pooled, and a subgroup analysis was performed on HER2-negative MBC receiving second-line treatment.

RIBBON-2 was a multicenter, phase 3 trial that compared the benefits and risks of standard chemotherapy regimens with or without bevacizumab as second-line therapy in HER2-negative MBC patients [17]. Chemotherapy drugs included capecitabine, taxanes, gemcitabine, and vinorelbine. The control group was chemotherapy combined with placebo, and the intervention group was chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab. The results showed that the PFS of the bevacizumab-containing group was significantly better than that of the control group, and the median PFS increased from 5.1 to 7.2 months (p = 0.0072). Although not statistically significant, the ORR increased from 29.6% to 39.5%, while OS was comparable in these two groups. The most common grade 3/4 bevacizumab-related AEs were proteinuria and hypertension. An increased number of AEs led to study discontinuation in the bevacizumab-containing arm compared with the control arm (13.3% vs. 7.2%). TANIA was designed to explore the benefits of second-line bevacizumab in MBC patients who had disease progression on first-line bevacizumab plus chemotherapy [18, 19]. Chemotherapy drugs included taxanes, anthracyclines, capecitabine, gemcitabine, vinorelbine, and cyclophosphamide. The control group was chemotherapy alone, and the intervention group was bevacizumab plus chemotherapy. The results showed that PFS was longer for those patients treated with chemotherapy plus bevacizumab than for those treated with chemotherapy alone (6.3 months vs. 4.2 months, HR = 0.75, p = 0.0068). Additionally, ORR and OS showed no significant differences between the two groups. The most common bevacizumab-related grade 3/4 AEs were hypertension and proteinuria. AVF2119g was a randomized phase 3 study designed to evaluate the benefits and risks of capecitabine with bevacizumab versus capecitabine alone in MBC patients prior treated with taxane and anthracycline [13]. The control group received capecitabine alone, and the intervention group received capecitabine combined with bevacizumab. Combination therapy significantly increased the response rates (19.8% vs. 9.1%; p = 0.001). However, it did not prolong PFS or OS. The most common bevacizumab-related grade 3/4 AE was hypertension.

Survival outcomes of bevacizumab-containing regimens were evaluated by ORR, PFS, and OS. Pooled results showed that the PFS of bevacizumab-containing groups was significantly better than the control groups, especially when administered as the second-line treatment for patients with HER2-negative MBC. In addition, compared with chemotherapy alone, the addition of bevacizumab further increased the ORR in both the general population and the HER2-negative group. Regarding OS, since there were no complete data on OS in the AVF2119g study, we performed a pooled analysis of the other two studies, in which all participants had HER2-negative MBC with second-line treatment. However, no significant effect on OS was demonstrated in association with the addition of bevacizumab to the second-line treatment for HER2-negative MBC. This finding is consistent with a previous meta-analysis [29], which included MBC patients with both first-line and multiline treatments. For pretreated patients with MBC, bevacizumab inhibited disease progression and improved the response rate but failed to improve long-term survival. Similarly, due to the benefits outweighing the risks, the European Medicines Agency has already granted full approval for the use of bevacizumab as first-line treatment for MBC, which remains in place today.

Safety management is of great importance in the systemic treatment of MBC. Pooled analysis showed that the probability of proteinuria in bevacizumab-containing groups was significantly higher compared with the control group. In a previous study, the vast majority of patients with proteinuria following bevacizumab treatment showed renal thrombotic microangiopathy on renal biopsy. Importantly, proteinuria resolved following drug discontinuation, and no patient developed severe renal failure requiring dialysis [30]. In contrast, the incidences of hypertension, bleeding, and thromboembolic events in the two groups were similar. In addition, the application of bevacizumab tended to result in therapy discontinuation due to treatment-related toxicity but did not reach a significant difference. The profile and frequencies of the bevacizumab-related AEs in this study were similar to those reported in other studies [31, 32, 33]. For example, Vaklavas et al. [33] assessed the toxicity of bevacizumab. They indicated that the most common bevacizumab-related adverse effects included hypertension (32%), hemorrhagic and thrombotic events (16%), proteinuria, headache, rash, and dyspnea [33]. Therefore, while using bevacizumab, renal function and urine routine tests should be closely monitored, and renal biopsy should be performed if necessary to detect proteinuria and renal insufficiency as early as possible and intervene in time. Additionally, blood pressure and coagulation function are tested to better control AEs, improve the tolerance and compliance of patients, and reduce dosage reduction and discontinuation caused by AEs to ensure sufficient dose intensity and enhance the treatment effect.

This meta-analysis has some limitations. First, only three randomized, controlled, phase 3 studies were analyzed, with a relatively small sample size. Since most of the current studies are about the use of bevacizumab as first-line treatment, few relevant studies have examined the value of bevacizumab for patients with previously treated MBC. Second, although the control group was chemotherapy and the intervention group was combination treatment with bevacizumab, the chemotherapy drugs in each study were different because there was no standard treatment plan after first-line treatment, so the results may be less reliable. Third, only two studies had complete OS data, and the other study only had simple data of median OS, so the results could not be pooled. Fourth, we cannot completely rule out the influence of publication bias.

Conclusions

Bevacizumab-containing chemotherapy, in light of its favorable effects on clinical outcomes, could be a preferred therapeutic option for patients with MBC, for whom the disease must be rapidly relieved. Further studies are warranted for exploring the advantageous patients with the receipt of bevacizumab in multiline treatment.

## Introduction

Breast cancer has the leading cause of cancer incidence among female malignancies, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases worldwide in 2020 [1]. Approximately 20–30% of early breast cancer patients will experience recurrence and metastasis, and 3–10% of breast cancer patients have distant metastasis at the initial diagnosis [2, 3, 4]. Metastatic breast cancer (MBC) is not curable, and its therapeutic goals are prolonging survival and maintaining the quality of life. There is no standard regimen for advanced breast cancer patients who progress after first-line treatment. Currently, multidisciplinary treatment has been advocated for cancer patients to achieve more precise treatment. Practical strategies for breast cancer treatment include radiation therapy, surgery, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy. The median overall survival (OS) varies from 1 to 5 years with aggressive treatment across different molecular subtypes of MBC [5, 6, 7].

Bevacizumab is a humanized anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monoclonal antibody that slows the growth of new blood vessels by inhibiting VEGF-A, thereby controlling tumor growth [8, 9]. Three prior randomized phase 3 trials (RIBBON-1, E2100, and AVADO) showed that bevacizumab combined with standard chemotherapy was effective in the initial treatment of HER2-negative MBC, with significantly longer progression-free survival (PFS) in the combination group than in the chemotherapy alone group [10, 11, 12]. In February 2008, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved bevacizumab plus paclitaxel for first-line treatment in HER2-negative MBC. However, 2 years later, the FDA withdrew the indication for bevacizumab in MBC because long-term follow-up showed no benefit in OS and increased rates of serious adverse events (AEs). Subsequently, bevacizumab has been examined in neoadjuvant therapy, adjuvant therapy, and after first-line treatment for breast cancer to expand its indications. However, conflicting outcomes exist [13, 14, 15, 16].

Therefore, we performed this systematic review and meta-analysis to explore the efficiency and safety of the bevacizumab-containing regimen in pretreated patients with MBC. To our knowledge, this is the first relevant meta-analysis to be conducted. This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist (available in the online suppl. material at www.karger.com/doi/10.1159/000525882).
## Methods

### Literature Search

We searched PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating bevacizumab combined with chemotherapy for patients with previously treated MBC. The PICOS principle was used, and the keywords “bevacizumab” and “metastatic breast cancer” were searched. We used the Medical Subject Heading terms and the text words, for example, “Bevacizumab”[Mesh] OR Avastin[tw] AND “Neoplasm Metastasis”[Mesh] OR “Neoplasm Metastases”[tw] OR Metastases[tw] OR Metastasis[tw] AND “Breast Neoplasms”[Mesh] OR “Breast Neoplasm”[tw] OR “Breast Tumor*”[tw] OR “Breast Cancer”[tw] OR “Mammary Cancer*”[tw] OR “Breast Malignant Neoplasm*”[tw] OR “Breast Malignant Tumor*”[tw] OR “Human Mammary Carcinoma*”[tw] OR “Breast Carcinoma*”[tw]. Only human studies were allowed, and the latest search was performed on October 15, 2021. There was no language restriction.

### Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) RCTs, (2) breast cancer diagnosed by pathology or imaging with distant metastasis, (3) patients were assigned to either chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab or chemotherapy alone, and (4) MBC received at least one prior therapy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a retrospective study, cohort study, and other non-RCT research, (2) single-arm design, (3) patients receiving first-line therapy, and (4) original data were unavailable.

### Data Extraction

NoteExpress 3.5 (http://www.inoteexpress.com/aegean/) was used to check for duplicate literature. Two authors (Z.L. and F.G.) independently screened and selected the remaining literature according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Then, the following data were extracted: trial name; trial phase; first author; year of publication; country; sample size; regimens; toxicities; hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the PFS, OS, and objective response rate (ORR).

The risk of bias in the study was assessed by the Cochrane Collaboration's tool, which includes the following items: allocation concealment, random sequence generation, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, blinding, and other biases. We independently assessed trial quality (Z.L. and F.G.), and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. When necessary, a third reviewer (J.W.) was consulted to reach a consensus.

### Statistical Analysis

The RevMan Version 5.4 software (https://community.cochrane.org/help/tools-and-software) was used to perform this systematic assessment. The primary endpoints were PFS and ORR. The secondary endpoints were OS and toxicity. Forest plots presented the pooled clinical outcomes, and dichotomous variables were compared by calculating the risk ratio (RR) and 95% CI. Heterogeneity was assessed visually using forest plots and the I2 statistic. An I2 > 50% indicated statistically significant heterogeneity, and in such cases, random-effects models were used for analysis. Otherwise, fixed-effects models were applied. A funnel plot was generated to evaluate publication bias. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
