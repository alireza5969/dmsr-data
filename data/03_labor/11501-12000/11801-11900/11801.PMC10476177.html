Behavioral interventions to extend sleep duration: A systematic review and meta-analysis

SUMMARY

The goal of this study was to examine the effects of behavioral sleep extension interventions on sleep duration outcomes in children and adults ≥ age 12. We conducted a systematic literature review, article extraction and meta-analysis. Our search yielded 42 studies from 14 countries. The majority of studies (n = 19) enrolled adults, 10 studies enrolled college students, 13 enrolled children (high school or middle school aged). Results from the meta-analysis demonstrated behavioral sleep extension resulted in a significantly higher sleep duration as compared to control group or baseline, with pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) that was similar for both two-arm 0.80 (95 % CI 0.28 to 1.31; p < 0.01; I2 = 99.2%) and one-arm studies 0.75 (95% CI 0.39 to 1.11; p < 0.01; I2 = 86%), and there was significant heterogeneity among both study types. Subgroup analyses revealed that studies with direct interventions on sleep duration (i.e., specified the sleep schedule) had larger effects compared to indirect methods (coaching, educational approaches) and a greater number of curriculum components was associated with smaller effects. Results of this review demonstrate that sleep extension studies are effective at extending sleep in a variety of populations but improving the description of intervention methods and use of more rigorous study designs will improve the quality and reproducibility of this area of research.

Introduction

Short sleep duration, defined as <6.5 h sleep duration on weeknights is perhaps the most common sleep problem in the U.S. adult population. According to data from the National Health Interview Survey, 70.1 million U.S. adults (29.2 %) sleep <6 h per 24 h period [1], which is far below the consensus recommendation of “at least 7 h” needed for health and performance among adults [2]. According to the 2017 Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance, only 25 % of high school students get the recommended eight or more hours of sleep per night [3,4]. Over the past 20 yrs, experimental and epidemiologic studies have documented the wide-ranging consequences of insufficient sleep, including increased risk for obesity and type two diabetes, all-cause mortality, suicidal ideation and decreased academic and cognitive performance [5–7]. These studies suggest there is a large unmet public health need for interventions to increase sleep duration and that addressing short sleep duration has the potential for multiple benefits for health and functioning.

A small but growing number of studies have begun to investigate interventions to extend sleep duration among individuals with short sleep duration, or among populations who are at risk for short sleep duration, such as college students. Although there is a large literature focused on treatment of insomnia and other sleep disorders, such as sleep apnea, it is only recently that studies have turned their attention to interventions for individuals who have short sleep duration. These types of interventions, focused on increasing the duration of sleep rather than addressing sleep complaints or insomnia, are typically referred to as sleep extension interventions. These studies have primarily addressed the question of proof of concept, i.e. “does extending sleep improve x outcome”. Consequently, less attention has been paid to the behavioral methods of how to extend sleep and which populations respond more or less favorably to sleep extension. Consistent with the National Institute of Health (NIH) Stage Model of intervention development [8], a variety of studies are needed to adapt promising interventions to maximum potency before these interventions can progress from basic to translational studies. This includes studies of mechanism of action and feasibility before moving to more real-world settings. Therefore, effective sleep extension interventions are needed to meet this public health need and to understand the effective components of sleep extension.

The purpose of this systematic review is to characterize the current practice of sleep extension interventions and then to conduct a meta-analysis to quantify the extent of sleep duration change associated with sleep extension interventions, including examining the role of characteristics of the populations studied, intervention components and outcome measurements. Following the review, we will provide suggestions for future research and clinical practice in this area. Greater understanding of the delivery of behavioral sleep extension interventions is needed to facilitate reproducibility and translation [9,10]. This study will address critically important barriers to communication and reproducibility of study design that are needed to transition this area of research from tightly controlled to real-world intervention studies.

Methods

Methods overview

We completed a systematic review and meta-analysis [11,12]. We used the PRISMA-P checklist for our protocol [13,14] and a detailed review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42019147590) prior to data extraction.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

The included studies were all prospective behavioral sleep interventions that reported a goal to increase sleep duration, with an intervention duration of at least 1 wk. We used a broad definition of sleep extension, including studies that used direct manipulation of sleep schedules (i.e., specified a sleep schedule to participants) as well indirect interventions (e.g., coaching, educational and/or sleep hygiene interventions if the goal was to influence sleep duration). Additional inclusion criteria included: participant age ≥12, randomized or non-randomized trials, randomized clinical trials (RCT), pragmatic clinical trials, controlled clinical trials, clinical trials, controlled singled group studies and quasi-experimental trials, enrolling at least 10 participants, published in English, inclusion of a quantitative outcome measure for sleep (self-report or objective), peer-reviewed publication, and availability of full text paper. Studies that focused on behavioral or pharmacologic intervention for sleep disorders, such as insomnia or sleep apnea, were excluded. When we suspected articles were derived from the same dataset, we contacted the corresponding author to determine whether each article was from a unique dataset.

Search strategy

An information specialist (MMM) conducted a search of the electronic databases: Medline(Ovid) 1946–2021, Embase (embase.com) 1974–2021, CINAHL Complete (Ebscohost) 1937–2021, CENTRAL (wiley.com) 1898–2021, Cochrane Library (wiley.com) 1898–2021, PsycINFO (Ebscohost) 1872–2021, SportDiscus (Ebscohost) 1800–2021. We conducted the initial search in 2019 and updated the search in January 2021. The search strategy is listed in Appendix 1. We also utilized bibliographies of articles selected for review to search for additional relevant literature. We searched grey literature to ensure the capture of all relevant articles, including: the annual meeting of the Associated Professional Sleep Societies and Google Scholar (Phrase searching: Sleep extension, extended bedtimes, sleep regularity). We used EndNote (Clarivate Analytics) to manage and remove duplicate citations.

Data collection and analysis

We used Covidence (Covidence.org) an online systematic reviewing platform for screening and study selection. Two co-authors screened and reviewed each study (KB, JD, SR and JL). A 3rd reviewer was used to resolve the disagreement if consensus was not reached (KB or JD). Data extraction was collected and managed using REDCap hosted at the University of Utah [15]. Variables extracted included study location, age category, inclusion/exclusion criteria, sample size, study design, length of intervention, number of intervention sessions and direct or indirect intervention. Studies were rated as direct interventions if the methods indicated participants followed a required sleep schedule. Studies were indirect if they used education or coaching for the sleep extension intervention. Reviewers extracted behavior change techniques (BCTs) using a standardized taxonomy [10] for 16 categories (See Table 1 for descriptions) and calculated a total BCT score for each study. Reviewers rated whether the article reported coaching from staff or interventionists (human), other coaching (e.g., text messages) or no coaching. Reviewers recorded the types of curriculum components that made up the intervention content (sleep hygiene, sleep education, stimulus control, bedtime advancement, electronics, no curriculum). Finally, reviewers extracted the sleep outcome measures collected (objective versus self-report) as well as the values for baseline and change scores for sleep duration. Two reviewers (KB and JD) assessed the included studies for sources of systematic bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias two tool [16] to classify five domains of bias and the overall risk of bias for randomized studies and the Risk of Bias In Non-Randomized studies (ROBINS-I) [17] to rate each study as ‘low’, ‘high’ or ‘some concerns’ (i.e., potential risk for bias). Disagreement for risk of bias between the two reviewers was resolved through discussion and consensus.

Measurement of treatment effect

Data were coded and stored into a Microsoft Excel version 16.39. All statistical analyses were performed using meta, metafor, and dmetar packages in R Studio version 1.2.1335. We utilized meta-analytic techniques for standardized means and sampling variances of two-arm pre-post control designs [18], and for one-arm repeated measure designs [19]. Furthermore, we calculated the effect sizes for each study using Hedge’s g to examine the sleep intervention’s standardized clinical effect. We present pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) values for both the fixed and random effects models. Due to data heterogeneity, we have mainly applied random effects models instead of fixed effects models to assure that data from all studies reporting prevalence data are represented in the pooled estimate, and to avoid influence by a handful of large studies. However, we present the results of the fixed effects model as well for comparison. The variation in effect size according to the random effects model is made up from both the random error within and the random error between studies. This fundamental difference between the two models is illustrated by their functional form [20].

Significance level α was set as 0.05 and we used a 2-tailed confidence interval to examine statistical significance. For all studies, the mean and standard deviation of sleep duration was converted into hours. Because we could not obtain raw pre – post correlation value from each study, we used conservative pre-post correlations of r = 0.5 throughout analyses.

Missing data

For studies with missing data, we made at least two attempts to contact the original study authors via email. When possible, we calculated missing statistics (e.g., correlation coefficients or standard deviations) from other available statistics (e.g., standard error or CI).

Assessment of reporting biases

We examined statistical heterogeneity among studies using I2 and between-study variance τ2 using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method for tau. We evaluated publication bias using the funnel plot and Egger test of the intercept [21].

Data synthesis

First, we conducted a narrative analysis to characterize the included studies. Then, we summarized quantitative effects of the interventions on sleep duration outcomes using random-effects models conducted in R. Subgroup analysis and investigation of heterogeneity were used in parallel to identify recorded sources of heterogeneity between studies [22,23].

Sensitivity analyses

We defined a study with an extreme effect size relative to other studies when a study’s 95 % confidence interval does not overlap with the estimated 95% confidence interval of the pooled effect. Finally, we performed sensitivity analysis after excluding observed extreme studies by giving them a weight of zero. We completed sensitivity analyses for relevant measures including to assess the impact of missing data and types of bias. For example, Greenland and O’Rourke [24] argue that results from higher quality studies should be emphasized and have laid out methods for incorporating quality scores in analysis and point to the special care needed with using quality scores as bias predictors [24].

Subgroup analysis

We examined predefined subgroups according to prespecified criteria including characteristics of the population (age, athlete vs. non-athlete, baseline sleep duration), characteristics of the study design and intervention (direct versus indirect, number of BCTs, coaching type, number of curriculum components) and outcome measurement type (self-report versus objective). For each of these subgroup analyses, we recalculated the overall effect and produced results independently. We set a minimum sample size per level (three studies) to maintain the robustness of association with effect size. Similar to the main analyses, we conducted separate analyses for the two and one-arm studies. Analyses were conducted using a random effect model using REML that stratified the outcome by prespecified criteria (2 or more levels). For analyses of continuous measures, we utilized meta-regression using REML and Knapp-Hartung adjustment. For continuous variables in the two designs, we performed a meta-regression on the interaction between two groups to evaluate the relationship between control groups and experimental groups.

Rating the quality of evidence

We reviewed the risk of bias for randomized studies using the Revised tool for Risk of Bias (ROB two tool) [16] and observational studies using the Risk of Bias In Non Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [17]. Then, we used GRADE guidelines [25] to synthesize and rate the quality of evidence for sleep duration and provide recommendations for use in patient care and for future research.

Results

Search results

PRISMA diagram presents the number of studies identified, reviewed and included (Fig. 1). The search identified 9316 studies, and after removal of duplicates, 5753 studies were screened. We completed 265 full text reviews and 41 studies met our entry criteria. We identified one study that was not included in our search, and therefore the total articles selected was 42 studies. Two-arm studies are listed in Table 2 (n = 26) [26–49] and one-arm studies are listed in Table 3 (n = 16) [50–65].

Study characteristics

The most prevalent study location was the USA (n = 17) [27–29,32,33,35,36,38,42,44,51–53,55,58,62,64–67]. Several studies were also conducted in Australia (n = 4) [31,41,43,56,63], New Zealand (n = 3) [37,47,48,50,61], and one study conducted in Belgium [54], Brazil [30], Canada [45], Finland [57], Iran [39], Japan [46], Mexico [40], The Netherlands [68], Taiwan [49], Thailand [59] and the UK [26], respectively. Over half of the included studies were two-arm designs 26) and of those studies, 15 were randomized [26,27,30,32,34–37,39,40,43,45,46,67] and three were randomized crossover designs [29,59,66]. Age groups include 18 studies among adults [26,31–33,36,38,42,44–48,54,59,62,63], 10 studies among college students [27,35,49,52,53,55,58,60,64,65], 14 youth samples (high school and middle school) [29,30,34,37,39–41,43,51,56,57,61,66,67]. Other populations included eight studies conducted among athletes [31,48,50,52,56,58,63,64], three studies among perinatal women [38,45,47], two studies in hypertension [28,36] and one study conducted among the following: adolescents diagnosed with ADHD [29], college students with binge drinking [35], adults with overweight or obesity [62], adults with prediabetes [33] and women with depressive symptoms [32].

Behavior change techniques (BCTs)

The average total BCTs per study was 1.8 (sd = 1.1, range 0–6, Fig. 2). The most commonly reported BCT was “shaping knowledge (n = 30) [26–28,30,31,34–39,41,43,45–50,52,54–57,59,61–64,67]. Other commonly used techniques were goals and planning (n = 23) [26,28,29,32–34,36,39–42,48,51,52,54,55,57,59,62–64,66,67] and feedback/monitoring (n = 14) [28,32,35,46,49,50,53e55,57,62, 63,66,67]. Use of social support was rare (n = 4) [39,43,45,66] as were natural consequences [39], comparison [39], reward [67] and antecedents [39]. No studies reported using associations, repetition, regulation, identity, scheduled consequences or covert learning. We also extracted the specific sleep goals from the studies (listed in Tables 2 and 3). The most common goal was extending time in bed by 1 h [36,57,59]. Pediatric studies tended to extend time in bed by 1.5 h [26,51,63,66] or adjusted time in bed by gradually advancing their bedtime by 5 m per night [29,34,40]. Some studies reported discussing goals but did not report a specific time in bed goal [33,39,41,52,55,67]. Other studies extended time in bed to 8.5 h [29,62], at least 10 h [48] or extended habitual time in bed by 3 h [42]. One study asked participants to “sleep as much as possible. 1–1.5 [60].

Sessions/coaching

Number of intervention sessions ranged from 0 to 14 (m = 2.2, sd = 2.8). There were 20 studies that did not report any coaching or interventionist [27,30–32,35,37,38,42,44,47,49,53,54,56,58,60–62,64,65], 21 studies reported presence of coaching delivered by study personnel or interventionist [26,29,39,41,43,45,48,50,51,57,59,61–63,66,67].

Curriculum components

Studies reported an average of two educational components (sd = 1, range 0–4). A total of 25 studies reported using sleep hygiene in their intervention, and included either a detailed description of the components or a citation to published recommendations [26–28,30,31,34–36,39,40,43,45,46,48,50,52,54,55,57,59,61,64–67]. In addition, six studies only reported having a sleep hygiene intervention but did not provide a citation or description [38,49,53,56,62,63]. A total of 21 studies provided “sleep education” such as information about the importance of sleep [26–28,30,33,35,37,39,41,43,45–47,49,50,52,55,57,59,61,63,66,67], 10 studies reported bedtime advancement techniques [28–30,32,36,41,48,62,66,68], seven studies provided advice regarding electronic use [27,31,35,50,55,59,66,68], and five studies included stimulus control instructions [35,38,41,46,49]. Finally, five studies did not describe an educational curriculum [42,44,51,58,60].

Meta-analysis results

The meta-analysis results were derived from 42 studies and include a total of 5668 participants with pre-score measurement and only 5203 (91.8 %) with post-score measurements due to dropout of participants. We present the results of analyses of the two-arm and one-arm studies separately in the analyses below.

Publication bias (Supplemental Fig. 1.)

Among the two-arm pre-post control designs, there was evidence of publication bias; some of the studies were excluded from the 95 % CI of the contour-enhanced funnel plot and Egger’s test of intercept indicated significant asymmetry (intercept, −7.62; 95% CI, −14.26 to −0.98; p = 0.03). Similarly, among the one-arm repeated measures design, these analyses also indicated possible publication bias. Both the funnel plot and Egger’s test of intercept indicated significant asymmetry (intercept, 4.05; 95% CI, 2.43 to 5.67; p < 0.01).

Overall results of effect size (Fig. 2.)

The overall pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) of two-arm studies (26 studies) was 1.52 (95% CI 1.44 to 1.59; p < 0.01) hours when using the fixed effects model and 0.80 (95% CI 0.28 to 1.31; p < 0.01; I2 = 99.2%) hours when using the random-effects model (Fig. 3.). In addition, the overall pooled SMD of single-group repeated measure designs (16 studies) was 0.33 (95% CI 0.25 to 0.42; p < 0.01) hours when using the fixed effects model and [48,49,66,67] hours when using the random-effects model (Fig. 3). Considerable heterogeneity between studies was identified for both the two-arm [39] and one-arm designs [51] using a Baujat plot. Nevertheless, exclusion of the influential studies had minimal effect on the pooled SMD. In the two-arm studies, the exclusion decreased the pooled SMD to 0.61 (95% CI 0.2 to 1.01; p < 0.01; I2 = 91%) and in the one-arm studies 0.66 (95% CI 0.33 to 1.00; p < 0.01; I2 = 82%). Therefore, since the estimated I2 values were considerably high, we kept both studies in the final analyses.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted for participant characteristics (age, athlete versus non-athlete and mean baseline sleep duration), study design/intervention (BCTs, curriculum components, direct versus indirect intervention and coaching) and outcome measurement (objective versus self-report measurement.

Participant characteristics

Age. Studies were grouped by age category (age <18/youth, college student, and adult). As shown in Supplemental Fig. 1., Among the pooled SMD of two-arm studies, both adult and youth subgroups demonstrated larger effect sizes compared with college student samples (adult = 0.88,95% CI 0.17 to 1.59; I2 = 88%, youth 0.97, 95% CI 0.06 to 1.88; I2 = 99%, and college −0.14, 95% CI −0.88 to 0.59; I2 = 88%). Among the single-group designs, adult subgroup’s pooled SMD were larger 1.29 (95% CI 0.21 to 2.37; I2 = 81%) compared to both college and youth subgroup’s pooled SMD, 0.55 (95% CI 0.04 to 1.07; I2 = 82%) and 0.71 (95% CI 0.04 to 1.37; I2 = 90%), respectively. For both the two-arm and one-arm designs, the test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant ( p = 0:16) and (p = 0:48) respectively.

Athletes vs. non-athletes

We compared studies of athletes to studies of non-athlete participants in the one-arm studies only. The pooled SMD of the non-athlete and athlete groups were similar; non-athlete = 0.77 (95% CI 0.23 to 1.32; I2 = 89%) and athlete = 0.76 (95% CI 0.27 to 1.25; I2 = 79%). The test for subgroup differences was not significant (p = 0:97). We did not perform sub-analyses with studies employed two-arm designs since a limited number of studies were conducted with this design among athletes.

Mean baseline sleep duration

We examined mean baseline sleep duration as a continuous variable. The mean baseline sleep duration values were similar for both two-arm 7.01 (SD = 0.79) and one-arm 6.9 (SD = 0.97) studies, although please note that there were differences between studies in how baseline was considered. A substantial heterogeneity between studies were identified for both the two-arm and one-arm studies (I2= 99%; I2 = 90%) respectively. Mean baseline sleep duration of both control and intervention groups was not significantly associated with pooled SMD in the two-arm studies with coefficients 0.59(95% CI −1.4 to 3.06; p = 0:45) and −0.53(95% CI −3.07 to 1.74; p = 0:57) respectively. However, among the one-arm studies, mean baseline sleep duration of intervention group was significantly associated with a smaller SMD with coefficient −0.48 (95% CI −0.91 to −0.03; p < 0:05).

Characteristics of the study design/intervention

Direct vs. indirect

We compared studies of direct intervention to studies of indirect intervention. The direct subgroup demonstrated larger effect sizes 1.63 (95% CI 0.67 to 2.59; I2 = 92%) compared to the indirect subgroup 0.40 (95% CI −0.16 to 0.96; I2 = 99%) in two-arm studies. Similarly, in one-arm studies, the direct subgroup demonstrated larger effect sizes 1.22(95% CI 0.44 to 1.91; I2 = 90%) compared to the indirect subgroup 0.28 (95% CI 0.39 to 1.11; I2 = 45%). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant with both study designs (p < 0:05).

Coaching types

We rated coaching in the intervention as none, human, and the other types of coaching (e.g., text message). Subgroup analyses on coaching type were performed on the two-arm studies only; due to insufficient number of studies in the one-arm group. The no coaching subgroup demonstrated larger effect sizes 0.98(95% CI 0.09 to 1.88; I2 = 91%) compared with human and other coaching subgroup 0.68 (95% CI −0.15 to 1.52; I2 = 100%) and 0.71 (95% CI −0.23 to 1.64; I2 = 91%) respectively. However differences in coaching types were not statistically significant (p = 0:87).

Number of curriculum components

Number of curriculum components was analyzed as a continuous variable. The tests for residual heterogeneity were significant for both the two-arm and one-arm studies (I2 = 98%; I2 = 66:6%) respectively. The regression coefficient for the two-arm was −0.58 (95% CI −0.9 to −0.06; p < 0.05) and one-arm was −0.34(95% CI −0.67 to −0.01; p < 0.05). Therefore, in both types of study design, the meta-regression coefficients indicated higher curriculum components were negatively associated with SMD and statistically significant with both types of study design.

Characteristics of the outcome

We compared studies of objective outcome measurement to studies of self-report outcome measurement. The objective subgroup demonstrated larger effect sizes 0.81 (95% CI 0.29 to 1.33; I2 = 92%) compared to self-report subgroup 0.70 (95% CI −0.58 to 1.97; I2 = 100%) in two-arm studies. Similarly, in one-arm studies, the self-report subgroup demonstrated larger effect sizes 0.91 (95% CI −0.17 to 1.99; I2= 91%) compared to objective subgroup 0.72 (95% CI 0.33 to 1.11; I2 = 85%). For both the two-arms and one-arm designs, the test for subgroup differences were not statistically significant (p = 0:87; 0:75).

Rating the quality of evidence

Risk of bias (Fig. 4)

Risk of bias ratings indicated that the majority of studies had some concerns for bias (79%). The majority lacked detail on randomization or sequence allocation. In sleep extension, participant blinding is not likely to be possible. However, outcomes assessors (e.g., scoring actigraphy) were not blinded in nearly half of all the studies. Therefore, most studies were rated as some concern of bias.

GRADE ratings (Table 4)

Using the GRADE PRO system for rating evidence, we rated the 15 randomized studies. Results demonstrated there is moderate evidence for the use of sleep extension to increase sleep duration. The evidence was rated as moderate due to large effect size but there were concerns noted due to inconsistency in the effect sizes and potential bias that was identified in the study rating.

Discussion

The goals of this study were to review the growing area of sleep extension interventions in order to develop a greater understanding of the components of effective sleep extension interventions. Results of the descriptive review demonstrated sleep extension has been conducted in many samples of adults, college students and children. There were a variety of studies in unique populations (e.g., ADHD, hypertension) but not enough studies in one area to analyze. Results of the meta-analysis indicated that the pooled effect size for sleep duration change was approximately 45 m, on average. Although there was significant heterogeneity between studies, the majority of studies indicated that sleep extension was feasible, using a variety of behavior change methods and in a variety of populations. This study provides a benchmark regarding the range of effect sizes seen in sleep extension studies for adults, college-aged samples and adolescent/high school aged children.

One contributor to the heterogeneity in our results is that we used a broad definition of sleep extension, and included studies with direct intervention (i.e., directly scheduled a longer period in bed) and studies of indirect intervention (using techniques such as coaching or educational interventions). Results demonstrated that studies rated as direct intervention had a significantly larger effect size (1:23 h greater in the two-arm studies, 0:94 h different for one-arm studies). Coaching did not increase effect sizes. This may be a consequence of most indirect studies using coaching and education to persuade behavior change. In addition, studies with more curriculum components had lower effect sizes. This large difference in effects also highlights differences in the objectives of the studies. Direct sleep extension interventions were often used in efficacy studies, to determine the effects of the changes in sleep on an outcome. On the other hand, indirect interventions tended to be closer to effectiveness studies, focused on behavior change in real world-settings (e.g., schools), Therefore, these studies are addressing somewhat different questions. There were also studies that contained some direct (specified a time in bed) and some indirect techniques (provided education and/or coaching), including studies from our own lab in that category [26,28]. Based on these results, extending time in bed by direct intervention has the largest potential to change sleep, at least in the short term.

In other subgroup analyses, we found that studies with a higher number of BCTs had a larger effect size. This suggests that studies that had more behavioral tools (or at least provided greater description of their components) changed behaviors indeed were more able to produce a change, perhaps due to greater direction or precision in the use of behavioral techniques such as goal setting and self-monitoring. On the other hand, we found that studies with a larger number of curriculum components had a smaller effect size. This may be because studies that had greater educational components more likely to be school based or other educationally focused/indirect interventions. The differences we observed based on age group were not statistically significant but revealed some interesting patterns. The effects for college age participants were lower than in adults or youth participants, possibly because of the unique social and educational challenges of this age group. In addition, studies using objective measures found larger effect sizes than self-report, which may suggest that participants are more motivated to change if they are aware they are being encouraged and monitored [69].

The risk of bias assessment also provided important information about details needed to improve the rigor of sleep extension studies. Most studies lacked important methodological details that suggested those studies were at risk for biased results. For example, details regarding randomization, sequence allocation, and blinding of assessors are critical to the validity of randomized studies. For pilot studies, lack of resources may play a role (e.g., not enough staff to have blinded assessors). However, other sources of potential bias are more easily addressed (e.g., describing the details of randomization in the paper). Therefore, this review highlights an opportunity to increase the rigor of extension trials.

Limitations to this review

Our study evaluated a variety of aspects of sleep extension interventions but there are other areas that would be important to examine in future studies, such as the effects based on sex or race/ethnicity. Another limitation to this review is that sleep extension interventions are still in an early development phase, mainly in NIH stage 1 (refinement, pilot testing) and some early stage II (efficacy) trials. Only a few of the studies we reviewed were conducted in real-world settings (stage III). Few studies included follow-up beyond 30 days. The studies in this review generally had small sample sizes and although the effect size was large, it varied widely between studies (significant heterogeneity). In addition, we would consider our review of study BCTs to be a broad overview, due to rating the large domains rather than the detailed categories, which means our ratings likely underestimated what actually happened in the interventions. Even rating whether or not participants received coaching was difficult at times, because it was unknown whether research staff who reviewed logs, for example, engaged in problem solving or other coaching behaviors. For a more detailed analysis, having the study intervention manuals would provide more information regarding what was actually done by the interventionists and staff. Although the “active ingredients” of BCTs, such as goal setting, self-monitoring, etc., have been part of behavioral interventions for decades, it is only recently that a well-accepted taxonomy of these techniques has been available and gaining traction in intervention design [9,10]. Therefore, the results of this review should serve to encourage sleep extension researchers to increase their training in BCT components in the planning of sleep extension studies as well as their precision of reporting BCTs in their published study protocols. This method of communication about study design has the potential to significantly improve the precision of behavioral intervention development and refinement for sleep extension research.

Conclusion and future directions

In conclusion, this review identified 42 sleep extension studies that on average extended sleep, on average by approximately 45 m. Although direct methods lead to a larger effect size, it is unknown whether indirect methods would produce more sustained effects over time. Further research is needed to identify and refine the effective elements of sleep extension and evaluate the effects of sleep extension on emotional behavioral and physical health outcomes. This review serves as a benchmark in describing how this emerging intervention is conducted in various populations. Results demonstrate that these interventions are effective at increasing sleep duration and vary widely on the educational and behavioral methods used to achieve these gains.

Practice points

  1. Sleep extension interventions overall increase short term sleep duration for a variety of populations.

  2. Direct interventions produced greater effect sizes than indirect interventions, which used techniques more commonly found in education and coaching programs.

  3. Effects are larger for adults and youth compared to college students.

  4. Further research is needed to understand the role of behavior change components, but the most common elements of existing programs include educational components and goal setting.

Research agenda

  1. This review suggests that further detail and refinement of intervention components is needed to advance the field of sleep extension.

  2. Improvements in the rigor of study design, including procedures for randomization and blinding, are needed to reduce potential bias in this area of research.

  3. Research demonstrates that interventions improve sleep duration significantly, but further research is needed to determine:

    • Which populations benefit from sleep extension

    • Isolate the critical behavioral intervention components to extend sleep duration

    • Effects on critical physical and emotional health outcomes

    • How long sleep duration changes are maintained

    • Sustained effects of sleep extension

    • Methods of behavior change maintenence
