Post-infection Irritable Bowel Syndrome following Clostridioides difficile infection: A systematic-review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Background:

Post-infection irritable bowel syndrome (PI-IBS) affects ~14% patients after acute bacterial enterocolitis.

Aims:

To conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to find the prevalence of PI-IBS following Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI).

Methods:

We systematically searched MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science from inception through 1/20/2020 for cohort studies assessing PI-IBS following CDI. Primary outcome was pooled prevalence calculated using inverse variance heterogeneity model [MetaXL (v. 5.3)]. A priori subgroup analyses were done (by IBS diagnostic criteria-Rome vs others, time of IBS diagnosis-<6months or >6months, exclusion or inclusion of pre-existing IBS and CDI treatment-antibiotic with fecal microbiota transplantation vs antibiotic only). Heterogeneity was considered substantial if I2>50%.

Results:

From 2007–19, 15 studies were included (10 prospective, 5 retrospective; 9 full-text, 6 abstracts). Data from 1218 patients were included in the quantitative analysis. Risk of bias was low in 7, medium in 4 and high in 4 studies. Pooled prevalence of PI-IBS was 21.1% [95% confidence interval (CI), 8.2% – 35.7%], I2=96%. Common PI-IBS subtypes were diarrhea-predominant in 46.3% (50) patients, and mixed in 33.3% (36) patients. Subgroup analyses by IBS diagnostic criteria, time of IBS diagnosis or CDI treatment did not significantly change the primary outcome (all p>0.05), nor decrease heterogeneity. Funnel plot analysis revealed publication bias.

Conclusion:

Over 20% of patients develop PI-IBS after CDI. Factors such as diagnostic criteria for IBS and CDI treatment did not affect prevalence, though small numbers limit the confidence in these conclusions. Larger, well conducted studies are needed to study PI-IBS in CDI.

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a common gastrointestinal disorder, affecting 7–16% of the population [1]. It often presents as abdominal pain, discomfort, distention or a change in stool pattern [2]. Acute infectious enterocolitis is one of the strongest risk factors for IBS [1]. Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) that occurs after an initial episode of gastrointestinal infection is termed as post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome (PI-IBS). The pathophysiology of PI-IBS involves several possible mechanisms including inflammation and mucosal injury that develop following an infection, as evidenced by a study that reported the presence of macrophages and T lymphocytes in intestinal biopsies [3]. Similar studies have also reported elevated interleukin levels, which further implicates the role of inflammation in the development of PI-IBS [4, 5]. Increased intestinal permeability leads to increased submucosal exposure to foreign antigens which further meditates inflammation in PI-IBS[6]. Studies have hypothesized that prior gastrointestinal infection plays a role in the pathogenesis of IBS [7]. High levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10 and TGB-1β in PI-IBS demonstrate a close association with infection, also indicating that continued low grade inflammation is implicated [8]. A study that aimed to compare PI-IBS and non PI-IBS evaluated the levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) and intestinal fatty acid binding protein (IFABP) (a protein found in the small intestine enterocytes that is responsible for the uptake and transport of polar lipids) in patients with IBS and found that IFABP levels were higher in PI-IBS patients than non-PI-IBS patients, implying that injury to the intestinal mucosa can occur in PI-IBS [9].

Altered serotonin metabolism and enterochromaffin cells, which are responsible for maintaining healthy gastrointestinal function, are also suspected to be involved [10]. Other mechanisms include alterations of the gut microbiota which are thought to predispose to gastrointestinal infection [11] and the observation that PI-IBS in itself causes gut dysbiosis (which is a disruption in the microbial structure) [12].

PI-IBS after acute enterocolitis has been widely studied, with an incidence of ~10%, though estimates vary widely and differ by pathogen [13]. Approximately 9% of IBS in the community can be attributed to PI-IBS [14]. Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is the commonest infectious pathogen implicated in healthcare-associated infections [15]. Annually, over 450,000 persons in the US alone suffer from this disease [16]. The long term effects of CDI on gastrointestinal health have not been studied extensively. A few studies have reported the frequency of PI-IBS after CDI, with estimates varying from 4% to over 80% [17, 18]. Studies reporting PI-IBS rates after CDI have used different study designs, diagnostic criteria for CDI and IBS, and variable follow up. A recent meta-analysis reported PI-IBS in 14% of patients with CDI [19]. However, the meta-analysis included only three studies, and did not explore the association of various patient and disease related factors on incidence.

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the prevalence of PI-IBS following CDI, to explore the effect of patient and disease related factors on prevalence, and explore natural history of PI-IBS (IBS subtype, treatment response).

Methods

All procedures used in this meta-analysis were consistent with the MOOSE guideline (Appendix 1) [20].

Selection Criteria

Prospective and retrospective studies that reported PI-IBS after CDI were included. Case-control studies, case reports or case series were excluded; studies reporting other functional gastrointestinal disorders but not IBS were also excluded. In studies not reporting prevalence of PI-IBS (and where data was not derivable), corresponding authors were contacted electronically. If prevalence data was not derivable, the study was included in the qualitative review but excluded from the quantitative analysis. Similarly, if data for CDI was grouped with other gastrointestinal infections, authors were contacted for CDI-specific information. If required data were not available, the studies were excluded. Of the four authors contacted, one responded; however, required data was not available and the study was excluded.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive search of Ovid MEDLINE Epub Ahead of Print and In-Process & Other Non-Indexed Citations, Ovid MEDLINE Daily and Ovid MEDLINE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, EMBASE, and Web of Science from inception through January 20, 2020 was conducted. The study investigators (S.K. and S.S.) designed the search strategy and conducted the searches, which were limited to English language only. The Mayo Clinic library staff also independently designed and conducted the same searches. A controlled vocabulary supplemented with keywords was used to search for studies that presented data on IBS after CDI. The detailed search strategy is shown in Figure 1 and Appendix 2.

Two authors (S.S. and S.S.) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of the identified studies, and those that did not answer the research question of interest were excluded (Figure 1). The articles that remained were reviewed to determine whether they fulfilled the inclusion criteria. In addition, reference lists of included articles were reviewed for pertinent studies. Conflicts in study selection were resolved by a third investigator (S.K.).

Data Abstraction

A data collection form was developed, and data were independently abstracted to this form by 2 investigators (S.S. and S.S.). Data collected included study design, study duration, year of publication, number of patients, patient characteristics, criteria for diagnosis of IBS, time frame for IBS diagnosis, exclusion or not of pre-existing IBS, duration of follow-up and survey response rates. Details of CDI [diagnostic criteria, symptoms, previous episodes, treatment, complications] and PI-IBS subtype [diarrhea predominant (IBS-D), constipation predominant (IBS-C), mixed (IBS-M) and unknown;], treatment and outcomes were abstracted where available. Conflicts in data abstraction were resolved by consensus, referring to the original article.

Risk of Bias

The Newcastle Ottawa Scale for Cohort studies was used to assess methodological quality of the included studies (Supplementary Table 1).

Outcomes Assessed

The primary outcome assessed was pooled prevalence of PI-IBS following CDI. Patients who did not have adequate follow-up data or died during the study before outcome was assessed were excluded from analysis. In studies reporting prevalence at multiple time points, the time closest to the 6 month time point was considered for analyses. Secondary outcomes included risk factors and natural history of PI-IBS (IBS subtype, treatment response). For risk factors, the most adjusted estimates were reported.

Statistical Analyses

The primary outcome of the pooled analysis was prevalence of PI-IBS calculated as number of cases with IBS/total number of CDI cases. We used the inverse variance heterogeneity (IVhet) model of meta-analysis with corresponding 95% CI for the overall and subgroup analyses. The IVhet model is a modification of the fixed-effects models that is less affected by study heterogeneity compared to the random-effects model, and retains the individual weights of the studies [21]. Heterogeneity within groups was assessed with the I2 statistic; I2 >50% suggested substantial heterogeneity [22]. Calculations were performed and graphs constructed using MetaXL software version 5.3 (Epigear). A priori subgroup analyses were performed by IBS diagnostic criteria (Rome criterion vs others), inclusion or exclusion of pre-existing IBS, follow up duration (<6months or >6months), survey response rates (>50% vs ≤50%) and CDI treatment given [fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) plus antibiotics vs antibiotics alone]. Studies that did not report data on these variables were excluded from the respective subgroup analyses. In studies with data available for patients in different subgroups (eg, FMT plus antibiotics vs antibiotics alone), data was extracted for each subgroup separately. For subgroup analysis by IBS diagnostic criteria, studies using either Rome II or Rome III criteria were pooled together into the subgroup ‘Rome criteria’. Sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding outlier studies, studies with <6 months of follow up, studies with medium or high risk of bias, and studies published as conference abstracts only. Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots. Extraction of data from graphs, where data was not available in text, was performed using a graph reader tool (http://www.graphreader.com/). Overall quality of evidence was determined using the GRADE approach.

Results

Study selection process and characteristics of included studies

Database search revealed 1170 records; 14 of these records and 1 additional study from the reference lists were included. Thus, 15 studies (published between 2007 and 2019) were finally included in the systematic review (Figure 1). Of these, 2 studies were included in the qualitative analysis and excluded from the quantitative analysis as prevalence data was not in the format necessary. Of the included studies, 10 were prospective cohort and 5 were retrospective cohort studies (Table 1). Six of the studies were published as conference abstracts [23–28], while the remaining were full text articles. A total of 5340 patients were included in the qualitative analysis, while data from 1218 patients were included in the final quantitative analysis. Age of patients ranged from 18–93 years; proportion of females ranged from 16%–80%. Three studies were conducted in Europe, while 12 were from North America.

Eight studies specified the inclusion criteria for CDI cases [polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test positivity in 2 studies, culture and toxin A/B positivity in 1, any stool assay positivity in 1, two-step algorithm with toxin enzyme immunoassay and PCR in 1, and ICD-9 diagnosis code in 3] (Table 2). Eight studies included CDI patients treated with FMT, 2 included patients treated with antibiotics only, while 5 did not specify treatment given. For patients receiving FMT, the most common mode of delivery of stool product was via colonoscopy; other routes were via nasogastric tube/gastrostomy and oral capsules. Only three of the fifteen studies had a non-CDI control group. Follow up of patients ranged from 0.5 week to 7.5 years after CDI.

For the diagnosis of PI-IBS, 10 studies used Rome criteria (1 Rome II, 9 Rome III); 2 used a questionnaire, 2 used ICD-9 codes and 1 did not specify (Table 2). Among the eight studies including patients receiving FMT for CDI, the diagnosis of PI-IBS was made by Rome questionnaire in 4 studies, 1 study used a modified Rome questionnaire (no details on modification used), 1 study reported IBS-like symptoms, and 2 studies did not specify whether IBS was diagnosed using a validated questionnaire or only using IBS-like symptoms. Six studies excluded pre-existing IBS, 7 did not exclude it (1 study provided separate estimate for PI-IBS based on whether patients had pre-existing IBS), while 2 did not provide that information. The time point for diagnosis of PI-IBS ranged from immediately after CDI, to >1 year later in different studies. Two studies reported prevalence at multiple time points. Six studies used surveys, six studies used clinical assessment, one study used both (clinical assessment for short term and questionnaire for long term follow up) and two used diagnostic codes only. In the 5 studies with information available, survey response rates ranged from 46.1% –71.7%.

Prevalence of PI-IBS

In two studies that were included only in the qualitative analysis, IBS was reported as a rate ratio comparing CDI and non-CDI controls [28, 29]. One study reported rate ratios of IBS following gastrointestinal disorders including esophagitis, gastritis and duodenitis, gastroenteritis and colitis, diverticulitis, CDI, ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease. The rate ratio of IBS after CDI was 6.9 per 1000 person-years, which was higher than other disorders such as esophagitis, where the rate ratio was 4.1 per 1000 person-years [28]. They also noted that the rate ratio was as high as 9 per 1000 person-years for Crohn’s disease. A second study evaluated the development of functional gastrointestinal sequalae such as IBS, gastroesophageal reflux disease, dyspepsia and constipation after CDI [29]. Here, the incidence of PI-IBS was reported separately based on mode of acquisition of CDI. Interestingly, the largest rate ratios were seen for IBS in community-associated as well as healthcare-associated CDI. Incidence was 3.2 per 100,000 person-years for community-associated CDI, and 1.9 per 100,000 person-years for healthcare-associated CDI; rate ratio was 8.9 per 100,000 person- years (for community-associated CDI) and 5.5 per 100,000 person- years (for healthcare-associated CDI) compared to non-CDI controls[29].

In the remaining 13 studies that were included in the quantitative synthesis, prevalence of PI-IBS ranged from 1.5% – 84.5%. The pooled prevalence was 21.1% [95% confidence interval (CI), 8.2% – 35.7%], I2=96% (Figure 2). On excluding the outlier study [17], pooled prevalence was 17.0% (95% CI, 9.2% – 25.6%), I2=88%; the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.62).

The outlier study was a retrospective cohort study from a single hospital district in Finland [17]. CDI patients treated with FMT and matched antibiotic-only controls were included. Using Rome III criteria, the authors reported the frequency of IBS-like symptoms beyond 6 months in 77.8% of cases and 92.3% of controls (p=0.06).

Visual inspection of the funnel plot showed evidence of publication bias, with few studies reporting lower prevalence (Supplementary Figure 1). Assessment of study quality revealed 7 studies with low risk of bias, 4 with medium risk of bias and 4 with high risk of bias (Supplementary Table 1). The overall quality of evidence was considered very low due to the presence of publication bias.

Subgroup analyses

There was substantial heterogeneity in the primary outcome; hence, several a priori specified subgroup analyses were undertaken (Table 3; Supplementary Figures 2–5). Subgroup analyses by IBS diagnostic criteria (Rome or not), time of diagnosis, exclusion or not of pre-existing IBS patients, and CDI treatment modality revealed no statistically significant differences in pooled prevalence between the two groups (Table 3). The study that did not exclude patients with pre-existing IBS, but removed them from estimates of PI-IBS was included in the subgroup ‘excluded pre-existing PI-IBS’ for the subgroup analysis.

We did not conduct subgroup analysis by survey response rates as only 5 studies had sufficient data available. The prevalence in the study with <50% response rate was 25.4% [30]; in the other 4 studies with >50% response rate, the prevalence were 77.8%, 12.2%, 29% and 84.5% [17, 31, 32].

Sensitivity analyses

On excluding studies with medium or high risk of bias, pooled prevalence was 16.8% (95% CI, 9.0% – 25.5%), I2=80%. Excluding studies published as conference abstracts only, the pooled prevalence was 20.1% (95% CI, 3.7% – 40.0%), I2=97%. These estimates were statistically similar to the overall pooled prevalence (p>0.05). On excluding studies with <6 months of follow up, pooled prevalence was 20.2% (95% CI, 3.0% – 41.2%), I2=97%, which was similar to the overall pooled prevalence (p=0.93). Of note, 2 of the 4 studies in which PI-IBS was diagnosed within 6 months, used Rome criteria without the 6 month cut-off [18, 33]; 1 study used modified Rome criteria (this study was published as an abstract, and details on the exact criteria were not published) [25]; 1 study used a questionnaire [34]. Thus, study quality, type of article and duration of follow up did not affect estimates of prevalence. The outlier study did not affect the overall estimate or results of the subgroup analyses (Table 3).

Natural history and risk factors for PI-IBS

Five studies (108 patients with PI-IBS) reported IBS subtypes: IBS-D in 46.3% (50), IBS-M in 33.3% (36), IBS-C in 17.6% (19), and unknown in 2.8 % (3) patients. Treatment and outcomes of PI-IBS were reported in 1 study [35]. Patients were treated with dietary fiber, antidiarrheals, antispasmodics and tricyclic antidepressants. Resolution of symptoms occurred in 43.7% (7/16) patients during the follow up period (ranging from 1–3 years), while 9 were lost to follow up.

Five studies reported risk factors for developing IBS [18, 25, 30, 34] including: pre-existing IBS, IBD, BMI, anxiety score, and CDI symptom duration >7 days, Marine Corps service (in a study on US active military service members), infectious gastroenteritis (Table 2). In another study, patients with IBS symptoms immediately after CDI had longer duration of abdominal pain, mucus passage and shorter duration of vomiting [18]. In this study, only 1 patient retained the diagnosis of PI-IBS at 3 months; analysis of risk factors at this time point was thus not possible. Due to the small number of studies with available information, we did not synthesize these data mathematically.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 studies reporting on PI-IBS following CDI in 5340 patients, we found that approximately 1 in 5 patients with CDI develop PI-IBS. Although this estimate remained unchanged in subgroups based on diagnostic criteria for IBS, time frame for diagnosis, exclusion or not of patients with pre-existing IBS and CDI treatment given, results from these analyses should be interpreted with caution, as the number of studies in each subgroup was small and all comparisons had significant heterogeneity. Pooled prevalence estimates were also not affected by an outlier study, studies with medium or high risk of bias and studies published only as conference abstracts. Overall, the most common subtype of PI-IBS was IBS-D followed by IBS-M which is similar to the observation with PI-IBS phenotypes reported following other bacterial enterocolitis in the recent meta-analysis (3). There was evidence of publication bias, with lack of published studies providing lower prevalence estimates.

The pooled prevalence of 21% in our study is higher than the 14% prevalence reported in a previous meta-analysis [19]. The previous meta-analysis included only 3 studies with 269 patients. A prevalence of 21%, though similar to the 10%–25% prevalence of IBS in the community, is a cause for concern[36]. CDI affects over 450,000 adults yearly in the U.S. alone [16]. Using the estimates from our study, ~95,000 patients annually may develop PI-IBS post CDI in the U.S alone. Several factors should be taken in to account while interpreting these estimates. The diagnosis of IBS requires ruling out other ‘organic’ GI diseases, which may present with similar symptoms. Although some studies in our meta-analysis excluded patients with other GI diagnoses at the outset, several did not provide such details. In addition, evaluation for other GI diagnoses needs to be a continuous exercise; without sufficient information, we could not determine if this was carried out. This could have led to the overestimation of PI-IBS.

Chronicity of symptoms is important in diagnosing IBS. The recent Rome IV criteria require symptoms at least once a week for ≥6 months [37]. Studies in our meta-analysis had variable follow up and variable cut-off times for diagnosing IBS. Several studies reported PI-IBS at <6 months following the CDI. This is interesting, as even the previous Rome version mandated ongoing symptoms for at least 6 months for diagnosis. However, our pooled prevalence estimates were similar regardless of the cut-off time used.

In comparison to the previous meta-analysis19 conducted, we focused on an important aspect which is the natural history of PI-IBS in this patient population. A study conducted in young, otherwise healthy patients with CDI, found that the risk of functional GI diagnoses was elevated up to 24 months after the episode of CDI, with highest risk in the first 12 months [38]. GI diagnoses considered in this study included IBS and non-IBS symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, failure to thrive, dyspepsia etc. In another study, CDI patients had a ~6 times higher risk of developing IBS than matched controls [29]. The incidence of functional GI disorders was 14% in CDI vs 6% in controls; these disorders tended to be more prevalent in the first year after CDI. In both studies, IBS was lumped together with other disorders. Only one study in our meta-analysis provided details of the natural history of PI-IBS.

Adding further to the data provided by the former meta-analysis 19, we assessed the risk of developing PI-IBS in different treatment groups of CDI.FMT is now standard of care for treating recurrent CDI [39]. There are potential risks associated with transferring microbiota from one person to another, in particular for diseases thought to be associated with gut dysbiosis such as IBS. In a meta-analysis of over 4000 patients receiving FMT, GI adverse events such as abdominal distension, pain and diarrhea were frequent (up to 13%) during an average follow up of 7.7 months [40]. In our study, the prevalence of PI-IBS was similar in patients receiving antibiotics only and antibiotics followed by FMT. It should be noted, however, that only 3 studies were included in the antibiotic subgroup, and most studies in the FMT subgroup were at medium to high risk of bias. Additionally, IBS-like symptoms are common after FMT, which complicates the assessment of PI-IBS in these patients. We noted that over half of the studies including post-FMT patients used Rome questionnaire for PI-IBS diagnosis, and only one reported IBS-like symptoms. This increases our confidence in our estimates.

Our study is the largest meta-analysis till date studying PI-IBS after CDI. Strengths of our study include: comprehensive search of multiple databases, assessment of all aspects of the pathogen (CDI diagnosis, treatment), and disease related factors (IBS diagnostic criteria, time of diagnosis, natural history, subtype, treatment). By performing several subgroup and sensitivity analyses, we explored the effect of various factors on our primary outcome.

However, there are several limitations to our study. There was substantial heterogeneity in our primary outcome, as is often seen in prevalence meta-analyses. Heterogeneity did not decrease even after pre-planned subgroup and sensitivity analyses, indicating that other factors could be implicated. Though pre-planned, we could not undertake a subgroup analysis based on survey response rates, due to the limited number of studies. In the subgroup analysis by IBS diagnostic criteria, the different Rome criteria were pooled together in one group, due to the small number of studies using each criterion. Additionally, not all studies used the Rome criteria, but rather utilized ICD9 coding or similar administrative codes which were loosely used for the diagnoses of PI-IBS. Another limitation was the limited details available regarding the CDI episode, associated symptomatology and past CDI history. There are several diagnostic assays available for CDI, including toxin enzyme immunoassays and nucleic acid amplification tests. Due to variable sensitivity, different tests would affect selection of the CDI cohort and diagnosis of recurrent diarrhea during follow up. It would be of interest to see how the use of different tests affects PI-IBS estimates. Clinical factors such as duration of diarrhea, abdominal pain, comorbid psychiatric disorders etc. could also influence the risk of PI-IBS, as seen in previous studies of PI-IBS in infectious enteritis [13]. In the absence of sufficient information, we could not adequately study these factors. Lastly, only one study in our meta-analysis had a control (non CDI) group, which precludes assessment of PI-IBS risk compared to the unexposed derived from the same demographic/study population.

In conclusion, we found a 21% prevalence of PI-IBS following CDI. Factors such as CDI treatment and IBS diagnostic criteria did not affect prevalence. Our study highlights several gaps in this field, such as the limited data on various risk factors affecting PI-IBS and lack of information on natural history of PI-IBS. Well conducted larger studies are mandated to further study these aspects.
