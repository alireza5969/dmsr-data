The cost impact of telehealth interventions in pediatric surgical specialties: A systematic review

Abstract

Background:

Telehealth is a rapidly expanding care modality in the United States. Pediatric surgical patients often require complex care which can incur significant expenses, some of which may be alleviated by telehealth. We performed a systematic review comparing telehealth and in-person visits, and telehealth’s impacts on the cost of healthcare across pediatric surgical specialties.

Methods:

A systematic review was performed using the following databases: PubMed (MEDLINE), Scopus (Elsevier), and CINAHL (EBSCOHost), searched from inception to July 10th, 2022. Studies were included per the following criteria: (1) investigated a telehealth intervention for pediatric surgical care and (2) provided some metric of telehealth cost compared to an in-person visit. Non-English or studies conducted outside of the U.S. were excluded.

Results:

Fourteen manuscripts met inclusion criteria and presented data on 7992 visits, including patients with a weighted average age of 7.5 ± 3.5 years. Most (11/14) studies used telehealth in a synchronous, or “real-time” context. Of the studies which calculated dollar cost savings for telehealth visits compared to in-person appointments we found a substantial range of savings per visit, from $48.50 to $344.64. Cost savings were frequently realized in terms of reduced travel expenditures, lower opportunity costs (e.g. lost wages), and decreased hospital labor requirements.

Conclusions:

This review suggests that telehealth provides cost incentives to pediatric surgical care in many scenarios, including post-operative visits and some routine clinic visits. Future work should focus on standardizing the metrics by which cost impacts are analyzed and detailing which visits are most appropriately facilitated by telehealth.

Level of evidence:

V.

Introduction

1.

The United States healthcare landscape is ever-changing, with innovative technologies playing an increasingly important role in improving care delivery and affordability. Telehealth, defined as the use of telecommunication or digital communication technology (e.g. mobile phones, computers, etc.) to deliver health-related services [1], represents a rapidly-expanding care modality. Although this technology has grown in recent decades [2], the COVID-19 pandemic ushered in the widespread use of telehealth among many specialties that previously used it. The shift to virtual care to reduce in-person viral exposure led to the expansion of Medicaid and Medicare telehealth reimbursement in March 2020, with many private insurers following suit [3–5]. This change facilitated the adoption and growth of virtual healthcare across many specialties and settings.

Given the rapid expansion of this technology, its comparability to traditional in-person visits is unknown. Many providers are integrating telehealth into their regular practice, and a multitude of platforms are being investigated across medical and surgical specialties to compare the outcomes of telehealth-based care to in-person encounters [6–8]. One component of telehealth that is being increasingly studied is its impact on the cost of healthcare. A benefit of virtual healthcare is a reduced burden of traveling to and from appointments, which often represents a significant challenge for patients traveling from rural or urban underserved communities. For example, previous studies have shown significantly lower costs associated with telehealth visits for rural patients receiving care for pulmonary care or diabetes management [9, 10]. Prior studies have also shown that telehealth can decrease the cost of care for specialist consultations, while simultaneously experiencing high patient satisfaction [11–13]. From the perspective of the health system, data suggest that increased telehealth utilization may result in lower overall hospital costs [14, 15]. U.S. healthcare spending grew by 9.7% in 2020, and accounts for nearly 20% of the country’s Gross Domestic Product [16], among the highest in the developed world. Thus, further analysis of the cost impacts of telehealth to both patient and provider is warranted.

Pediatric surgical subspecialty care represents a unique subset of patients who engage with telehealth, as these patients are frequently complex and require intricate care coordination. Implementing telehealth for this population may present an opportunity to decrease the overall cost of care by alleviating travel burdens, reducing the opportunity cost of parental time away from work, and decreased labor costs related to health care staffing. While prior studies have demonstrated telehealth cost incentives across other realms of pediatric medicine [17, 18], a comprehensive review of its cost implications within the pediatric surgical population is lacking. The purpose of our research is to assess whether telehealth, compared to in-person visits, provides cost incentives for pediatric surgical patients. To evaluate this topic, we performed a systematic review of the literature to identify studies comparing the cost of telehealth care to that of traditional in-person visits for pediatric surgical patients. In performing this review, we hope to better inform clinicians, stakeholders, and health systems of the ways which telehealth may influence the cost of healthcare within this pediatric sector in the U.S.

Methods

2.

This study was conducted according to the guidelines set forth by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [19]. The search strategy was developed in conjunction with a university librarian specialist (E.B) and utilized the following databases: PubMed (U.S. National Library of Medicine, National Institutes of Health), Scopus (Elsevier), and CINAHL (EBSCO). The databases were searched from inception to July 10th, 2022. A combination of subject headings (e.g., MeSH in PubMed) and keywords for the following three concepts were used to develop the search strategy: telehealth/telemedicine, pediatrics and surgery, along with appropriate synonyms. A comprehensive description of the search strategies used for each database are listed in Appendix 1.

The review management software Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation Ltd, Melbourne, Australia) was used for study screening and selection. Criteria for inclusion required manuscripts to possess the following qualities: a pediatric population, within a pediatric surgical subspecialty, describing a telehealth intervention, and published in the English language. Furthermore, studies were limited to those performed in the United States owing to the variability of health care systems in different countries that may affect telehealth implementation and outcomes. Acceptable study designs included randomized-controlled trials, prospective cohort, retrospective cohort and case control, and cross-sectional studies. Exclusion criteria consisted of adult or non-surgical patient populations, non-English language, non-human studies, review articles, case reports of fewer than four patients, duplicates, inaccessible articles, and incomplete or missing statistical data. Given that this study only included de-identified patient information from previously published studies, institutional review board approval was not required.

Titles and abstracts were independently assessed by two reviewers (W.J. and R.G.) to identify articles meeting inclusion criteria. The senior author (P.P.) resolved any conflicts. The manuscript full texts were subsequently reviewed by W.J. and R.G. to identify articles to be included in the final analysis. The Oxford centre for Evidence-Based Medicine criteria was used to critically assess the level of evidence of included articles [20]. The Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies -of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was used for non-randomized studies [21] to evaluate the following categories: bias owing to confounding, bias in selection of participants into the study, bias in classification of interventions, bias owing to deviations from intended interventions, bias owing to missing data, bias in measurement of outcomes, and bias in selection of the reported results. The risk of bias for each aspect was graded as low, unclear, or high, and the assessment was independently performed on all studies and compared for consistency. Any disagreements were resolved by the senior author (P.P.).

Data extraction was independently performed by two authors (W.J. and R.G.) and results were compared for accuracy. Data were extracted into a standardized Excel template, and included author, publication year, study design, visit setting, patient cohort demographics, pediatric surgical subspecialty, and total number of visits. Furthermore, metrics of cost impacts documented in each manuscript were extracted and broken into three main categories: financial impacts, travel/timing impacts, and additional metrics. The latter two categories were included in order to capture costs which may be described in terms other than specific dollar amount (e.g. ‘travel time saved’), given that these metrics have a direct impact on the opportunity cost of attending a healthcare appointment. Finally, the primary and secondary outcomes of each manuscript were extracted.

Results

3.

The initial literature search yielded 1227 unique articles. Title and abstract screening excluded 1044 articles, and a full text review of 183 articles was conducted, of which 13 met final inclusion criteria [22–34]. One additional manuscript which met inclusion criteria but was not captured by the search was selected by hand and added to the final collection [35]. A PRISMA diagram describing the comprehensive search process is included in Fig. 1. The included studies demonstrated an acceptably low risk of bias, shown in Fig. 2. The categories which exhibited the most bias across the studies were owing to bias in participant selection and bias in measurement of outcomes. All manuscripts were categorized as level 4 or level 5 per the Oxford Level of Evidence scale.

Studies evaluating the cost implications of telehealth interventions included the following pediatric surgical subspecialties: General Pediatric Surgery (2), Neurosurgery (2), Ophthalmology (2), Orthopedic Surgery (3), Otolaryngology (1), and Urology (3) (Table 1). One manuscript described findings across several pediatric surgical specialties [33]. The studies presented data on 7992 visits, including patients with a weighted average age of 7.5 ± 3.5 years. The manuscripts were published between 2008 and 2022 and were inclusive of institutions within the United States. The type of visit was divided into four separate categories: clinic visits (seven studies), post-operative visits (four studies), diagnostic or screening exams (two studies), and one study which involved preoperative planning. Most studies (11/14) described telehealth in a synchronous, or “real-time” context, while two studies used telehealth in an asynchronous format, and one study evaluated theoretical savings. Study outcomes are further detailed in Table 2.

Financial Impacts:

Most of the cost savings comparing telehealth visits to in-person visits were described in terms of dollars saved by reducing travel expenditures for families of pediatric surgical patients. This included direct costs were related to fuel or hotel stays, as well as costs owing to lost caregiver wages. The method by which ‘cost of care’ was calculated varied substantially across studies, with cost savings ranging from $48.50 to $344.64 per visit by patients engaging in virtual care. Further information regarding specific cost savings data is shown in Table 1. One manuscript presented theoretical cost savings calculations: Laferriere et al. did not implement an actual telehealth clinic, but projected cost savings of $112.53 per patient visit based on the distance patients were traveling for in-person visits. Weaver et al. presented the savings in the context of children being screened for Retinopathy of Prematurity at a rural hospital, suggesting that hundreds of thousands of dollars would have been spent air-transporting patients to the nearest tertiary care center had the patients forgone virtual screening.

Three studies described the cost implications of telehealth from the provider or healthcare system perspective [33, 34, 36]. Atanda et al. described clinic costs savings owing to lower labor costs associated with telehealth visits, totaling $90/visit compared to $114/visit for in-person appointments. These calculations were made by extrapolating the labor wages for staff involved in the care of telehealth patients and did not include indirect costs associated with facility usage. Chwa et al. calculated the differences in visit charges and reimbursement rates across several surgical specialties for telehealth visits, concluding that reimbursement rates stayed largely stable while visit total charges decreased for virtual visits. Simko et al. found that pediatric neurosurgeons providing care to families at a ‘remote outreach clinic’ saved $178.28 per telehealth visit in the form of provider salary costs and did not experience worsened short-term outcomes relative to in-person appointments.

Travel impacts

3.1.

Multiple studies reported savings in terms of time for families using telehealth. For example, Hayward et al. noted that families engaging in telehealth saved an average of 2.5 h of round-trip travel time and 134 miles of travel distance for each visit that was conducted virtually. Similarly, Metzger et al. found that over 25% of patients surveyed had traveled more than 100 miles to attend their appointment, and over 50% had traveled 40 miles. The study published by Robinson et al. described the greatest travel burden of any study, with the average round trip distance equating to 498 miles per family. Importantly, three studies (Canon et al., Hayward et al., Simko et al.) used telehealth interventions that were deployed at rural ‘satellite’ sites, where patients travel to a rural clinic and then conduct a virtual visit with a provider at a larger tertiary care center. This model still required families to travel to the satellite site, but this was located significantly closer than the main hospital campus to the patients’ homes. One study by Simko et al. noted that providers were spared an average of 156 miles of round-trip travel when shifting care to the virtual realm for eligible ‘remote outreach clinic’ visits.

Additional metrics

3.2.

Beyond the financial and travel findings described above, the included studies evaluated other relevant appointment metrics. Three studies (Atanda et al., Finkelstein et al., and Sinha et al.) assessed the amount of time spent in the waiting room and time spent with the provider, with all three studies endorsing shorter wait times or higher percentage of total time spent with provider for virtual care compared to in-person visits. Jackson et al. used Medicare reimbursement data to calculate potential cost savings of virtual retinopathy of prematurity screening, presenting the data in terms of “Quality-Adjusted Life Years” (QALY), concluding that telehealth screening provided one QALY for $3193 compared to a cost of $5617 for patients undergoing physical ophthalmoscopy. Govil et al. described telehealth in terms of visit billing and coding, noting that more than half (50.98%) of the virtual pediatric Otolaryngology visits were billed at a Level 4 billing code or higher as patients were seen at the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Discussion

4.

Telehealth is an expanding care modality throughout the U.S. and world, presenting an opportunity to streamline care and reduce costs for patients and health systems alike. However, data is lacking on how this technology is being leveraged throughout pediatric surgical specialties, particularly with regards to its impact on the cost of healthcare within the U.S. system. We performed a systematic review of the literature to capture data on how the cost of telehealth compares to traditional in-person care across pediatric surgical subspecialities. In total, we identified 14 studies that investigated the financial impacts of telehealth in multiple contexts: direct cost impacts, travel savings, and miscellaneous cost aspects of telehealth care. Our review identified multiple telehealth interventions which lowered the total cost of care for pediatric surgical patients, often in the setting of routine clinic and post-operative follow-up visits.

Cost benefits are a major argument for the implementation of telehealth across pediatric surgical practices. Indeed, among the 14 studies summarized by our review, we identified a sizeable range of cost savings ($48.50 - $344.64 per visit). Furthermore, we found no studies which indicated that the telehealth option was more expensive than a conventional in-person visit. Similar savings have been identified in telehealth implementation across adult specialties [11, 12], indicating that these benefits have the potential to improve costs across the scope of healthcare. One payment structure gaining notoriety is that of value-based healthcare, defined as “the measured improvement in a person’s health outcomes for the cost of achieving that improvement” [37]. Though value-based care does not solely rely on the reduction of healthcare costs, telehealth in the proper context provides an opportunity for health systems to promote value in the care of pediatric surgical patients. Our review identified telehealth being used in a variety of pediatric surgical settings, including screening, post-operative, and clinic visits, indicating that this modality has flexibility to be applied across multiple visit categories. Moving forward, standardized metrics should be developed to better understand telehealth’s role in value-based healthcare as it pertains to the pediatric surgical population.

Beyond the considerations noted above, telehealth can potentially reduce cost burdens for rural patients who must travel to obtain pediatric surgical specialty care. Multiple studies included in our systematic review commented on travel distance as a hindrance to receiving care, with one study highlighting that nearly 1 in 4 patients had traveled over 100 miles to attend their appointment [29]. Not only is this relevant in terms of the incurred costs of travel, but also within the scope of opportunity cost, as parents are frequently required to miss work or coordinate care for other family members in order to attend a child’s appointment. Finkelstein et al. calculated an opportunity cost of $23.75 per minute during in-person pediatric Urology visits, compared to just $1.14 per minute for telehealth visits. One unique concept we encountered was the ‘pediatric neurosurgery telemedicine clinic’ described by Hayward et al. and Simko et al., where patients from rural Georgia travel to a clinic staffed by nurses and engage in visits conducted virtually with surgeons in Jacksonville, Florida [26, 34]. This model enables greater care access for rural patients by greatly reducing travel distance, while also allowing pertinent vital signs and physical exam components to be captured remotely. Our analysis suggests that telehealth can yield significant benefits to pediatric surgical patients hailing from rural regions, while maintaining a high standard of care for these patients.

While reducing travel distance is particularly relevant for patients hailing from rural areas, incentives also exist for the deployment of telehealth in urban settings. Specifically, several studies included in this review detail opportunity costs incurred by parents coordinating care for their children [24, 26, 29, 30], which affects parents regardless of where they live. Furthermore, urban underserved communities may stand to benefit from well-designed telehealth platforms, and recent initiatives have been developed to target these communities to improve metrics such as no-show rates [38, 39]. The studies in this review included patients across a broad geographic spectrum, highlighting the flexibility of telehealth as a tool for patients living both nearby and far from medical facilities. Further work should be undertaken to stratify the unique ways in which telehealth will impact the cost of care for both rural and urban communities.

As more Americans have access to technology capable of using telehealth [40], it may be wise for pediatric surgical specialties to develop dedicated ‘telehealth blocks’ during clinic days in order to more consistently incorporate telehealth into their routine. When surveyed, parents and guardians are generally enthusiastic about having the option for their child to be seen virtually and have endorsed high satisfaction rates [23, 41]. Multiple studies included in this review found that telehealth facilitated significantly shorter waiting room times compared to in-person visits, leading to parents spending a higher percentage of total visit times with the provider [24, 31, 35]. This work suggests that concerted efforts by pediatric surgical providers to increase the number of telehealth offerings may help to alleviate direct and indirect cost burdens experienced by parents obtaining care for their children.

Though there are many positive outcomes of shifting pediatric surgical visits to telehealth, this modality also presents drawbacks. There are aspects of in-person appointments that may prove difficult to replicate via telehealth, such as performing a comprehensive physical exam, establishing a high-quality patient-provider relationship, and observing certain body language and other cues. One common critique of telehealth is that it may lead to the deterioration of provider-patient relationship, thus negatively affecting outcomes [42, 43].

Some of these downsides are likely to be addressed as technological capabilities and broadband improve [44], but this will likely take years for sufficient patient and provider adoption. Furthermore, equity in telehealth accessibility must be addressed, as prior studies have identified decreased telehealth access among various racial and lower socioeconomic groups [45–48]. We were unable to stratify the sociodemographic characteristics of the patients included in our study, limiting our ability to address how telehealth may reduce access to care among marginalized communities. Telehealth barriers often exist in the form of lacking access to reliable Wi-Fi or adequate telehealth infrastructure, and if not addressed these deficiencies risk further exacerbating inequities in care access and outcomes [49]. These systemic issues were acknowledged in the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security (CARES) Act, which earmarked funding to improving broadband and purchasing devices to increase access to telehealth care in underserved populations [50]. However, great strides still need to be made in the fight for accessible telehealth in these communities.

Our study presents multiple intrinsic limitations. Given the heterogeneity of the data reported in the included studies, we were unable to meta-analyze any of the cost components of comparing telehealth to traditional visits, limiting our ability to provide concrete estimates of savings which may be recognized across pediatric surgical subspecialties. Furthermore, given the complexity and wide scope of pediatric surgical subspecialty care, it is likely that certain encounters may not be conducive to telehealth interventions, but the paucity of literature on this topic prevents a more targeted recommendation. Finally, we are limited by the study design of the included manuscripts, as there were no randomized controlled trials among our study cohort, limiting the strength of our evidence.

Conclusion

5.

Pediatric surgical subspecialty care encompasses a wide scope of conditions and treatments, some of which are likely conducive to being treated via telehealth. This systematic review suggests that telehealth provides cost incentives to pediatric surgical care in certain scenarios, such as clinic and post-operative visits, and can improve access to care by decreasing travel time and limiting opportunity costs. An increasing number of Americans continue to have access to the technologies necessary to implement telehealth, but caution should be exercised to ensure equity in its application, particularly in underserved communities. Future work should be directed towards standardizing the metrics by which cost savings are analyzed and detailing which types of visits are most appropriately facilitated by telehealth to improve access among children in need of surgical care.

Table 2

Summary of outcomes of studies included in the systematic review.

Author (Year) Stakeholder Group Summary of Outcomes
Atanda (2018) Patients, Providers/Healthcare system The hospital saved $24 in labor costs per telehealth visit. Patients saved an average of 85 round trip travel miles per visit and had significantly shorter wait times for telehealth appointments compared to in person visits.
Canon (2014) Patients Patients traveled significantly further to attend in-person appointments than telehealth appointments. Every 23-mile increase in distance to the children’s hospital increased patients’ odds of choosing telehealth by 111%.
Chwa (2022) Providers/Healthcare system Evaluated hospital charges and reimbursement rates for telehealth vs in person visits across pediatric surgical specialties. For example: plastic surgery charged an average of $76.59 less per telehealth visit but was reimbursed $21.82 more per visit under a virtual care model.
Finkelstein (2019) Patients Families saved a median 113 min of travel time per telehealth visit. Median virtual waiting room time was 5.0 min, and no adverse postoperative outcomes were observed.
Finkelstein (2020) Patients Parents missed significantly less work time and children missed significantly less school when using virtual care compared to in person visits. Opportunity cost was $23.75 per minute of in person visit, and $1.14 per minute virtually.
Govil (2020) Providers/Healthcare System Over half (50.98%) of visits were billed at level 4 billing code. Median time from virtual patient rooming to checkout was 24.6 min.
Hayward (2019) Patients Families saved an average 2.5 travel hours and 134 travel miles per virtual visit. Average transportation cost and missed work savings per family across multiple virtual visits was $223.
Jackson (2008) Patients Cost per quality-adjusted life year gained was $3193 for virtual visits and $5617 for standard ophthalmoscopy. Telehealth proved to be more cost-effective than standard care for retinopathy of prematurity screening.
Laferriere (2020) Patients Calculated theoretical cost savings of implementing telehealth in pediatric surgical patients across Hawai’i. Determined that over 30% of outpatient encounters over 4 years would have been candidates for telehealth delivery.
Metzger (2021) Patients 25% of parents reported driving more than 100 miles to surgical care appointments, and more than 25% estimated costs of at least $30 per in person visit. One adult had to miss work to attend appointments in 43% of households.
Robinson (2017) Patients Using telephone prescreening for cerebral palsy surgery, each family saved 658 miles of travel. Each 10-mile increase in distance from health center increased odds of patient utilizing telephone screening increased by 10%.
Simko (2022) Providers/Healthcare System Compared the total provider cost of telehealth visits to in-person visits for rural outreach patients in Georgia. Providers would have had to drive an average 156.1 extra miles round trip to travel to the outreach clinic, compared to virtual visits. No patients seen by telehealth were admitted to the emergency department in the following 7 days.
Sinha (2019) Patients A survey administered to parents of orthopedic surgery patients determined that telehealth visits significantly reduced travel time and costs compared to in person clinic visits.
Weaver (2012) Patients Retinopathy of prematurity screening of children in a remote location included 137 infants who underwent screening, with 13 eventually being air-lifted to a higher-acuity care center. Had telehealth screening not been available, total travel costs would have exceeded $1.1 million.
