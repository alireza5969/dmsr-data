Sensitive periods in development and risk for psychiatric disorders and related endpoints: A systematic review of child maltreatment findings

Abstract

There is tremendous variation in the mental health of individuals exposed to maltreatment. One hypothesis is that this variation is attributable, in part, to maltreatment timing—specifically, whether maltreatment occurs during sensitive periods in development when the brain in maximally sensitive to certain types of environmental input. To determine whether there is scientific consensus around when periods of peak vulnerability occur, we conducted a systematic review of human observational studies. Although 74% of the 118 unique cross-sectional or longitudinal cohort studies we identified reported timing effects, no consistent sensitive periods were identified for any of the most studied outcomes. Thus, it appears observational research on child maltreatment has yet to converge on a single period (or set of periods) of heightened vulnerability. This review identifies study characteristics that may contribute to these between-study differences and uses observations from the review to derive a comprehensive set of recommendations for subsequent research.

INTRODUCTION

Psychiatric disorders affect an estimated 25% of the global population each year and 70–80% at some point during their lifetime.(1–3) A major driver of psychiatric illnesses is child maltreatment, a form of early adversity that encompasses both the absence of expected parent/caregiver inputs (e.g., physical or emotional neglect) as well as the presence of harmful, threatening inputs (e.g., physical, sexual, or emotional abuse). Childhood maltreatment is common, with worldwide prevalence estimates ranging from 13% (sexual abuse) to 36% (emotional abuse).(4,5) People with maltreatment histories also have, on average, at least a two-fold risk for psychiatric illness,(6) an effect that appears causal.(7,8) Nevertheless, it is clear that not all maltreated children are equally affected. One important determinant of maltreatment’s effects may be its developmental timing—specifically, whether it occurs during sensitive periods in development, life stages when certain environmental inputs may be more influential. Identifying when these periods exist could have major benefits, including advancing understanding of the mechanisms linking maltreatment to later psychopathology and informing the development of targeted strategies to facilitate more timely intervention.

One powerful determinant of the extent to which different life experiences become biologically embedded is neuroplasticity, which refers to the brain’s dynamic capacity to undergo maturation and change structurally and functionally in response to experience.(9) Critical and sensitive periods are states of elevated plasticity when the effect of experience on neurodevelopment is especially strong and lasting.(5–7) Critical periods have long been viewed from a deterministic perspective: after the critical period ends, the effects of experience are permanent and irreversible; the window of plasticity closes and remains shut. But windows of sensitive-period plasticity do not fully close; rather, some degree of plasticity remains, though considerably more effort is required to produce change after a sensitive period ends compared to when it was open. We therefore favor the term “sensitive period” in this manuscript, given the potential for symptomatic improvement, remission, and/or recovery in nearly all common forms of psychopathology.

The neurobiological mechanisms that give rise to sensitive periods are complex and encompass those that inhibit, initiate, and later, put the brakes on, plasticity.(10) These encompass both processes that govern the initial wiring of the brain (e.g., synapse formation and overproduction) as well as those that shape consolidation of neural networks (e.g., synapse elimination and pruning).(9) The associated psychological literature also commonly groups these mechanisms into those that are time-limited and activated by ubiquitous experiences across the human species (sometimes labeled “experience-expectant”), and other ongoing learning mechanisms thought to be unique to the individual and their environment (“experience dependent”).(11) The former category are more commonly implicated in the formation of sensitive periods; however, the distinction between the two types of mechanisms is not necessarily clear-cut, as even “expected” experiences often differ between individuals in ways that strongly impact future development.(12)

Interest in sensitive periods can be traced back nearly a century, when the concept was first articulated by Charles Stockard, an embryologist. Stockard observed that birth defects in fish offspring occurred only when mothers were exposed to extreme temperatures and toxic chemicals just as a specific embryonic organ was developing, dubbing these time periods “moments of supremacy” or “critical moments”.(13) In the 1930s, ethologist Konrad Lorenz was the first to apply this concept to animal behavior, noting that young ducklings, goslings, and chicks would fail to “imprint” in a species-typical fashion if their encounter with a moving object was delayed.(14) In the latter half of the 20th century, animal researchers also began generating evidence to support the existence of critical “socialization periods” when specific social inputs were necessary for normal adult social and emotional functioning.(15) Harry Harlow’s classic study showed rhesus monkeys kept in social isolation between 3–12 months of age had marked and enduring social and emotional deficits (whereas isolation at later ages appeared not to have such an effect), and in the 1970s, Nobel laureates David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel began establishing critical periods in the development of sensory systems.(16,17) The early animal socialization studies, in particular, paved the way for human research on attachment and approach learning, as well as the hypothesis that early caregiving relationships play a crucial role in determining later emotional and behavioral health.(18)

Since the 1950s, studies mentioning sensitive or critical periods have steadily increased, mirroring the increase in psychiatry and psychology publications (broadly defined) across the same period. Articles additionally mentioning maltreatment are fewer but increased over 40-fold in less than two decades (Appendix, pg. 4). Published reviews investigating timing effects for maternal deprivation or institutionalization suggest a sensitive period between ages 6–24 months.(19) However, periods of peak vulnerability to maltreatment remain unknown.(20,21)

In this systematic review, we investigated whether evidence has converged to identify sensitive periods when the developing brain is most vulnerable to the effects of child maltreatment. Specifically, we asked the followed questions:

  1. What proportion of studies testing for maltreatment timing effects find them? And how consistent are the periods of peak vulnerability across studies?

  2. Some researchers have postulated that sensitive periods are a property of individual biological systems (e.g., neural circuits) rather than complex phenotypes like mental disorders.(22) Do findings appear more consistent across studies at more “biological” levels of analysis?

  3. Do findings become more consistent when studies are stratified by different study characteristics (e.g., sample size, statistical approach, assessment methods)?

  4. Research has repeatedly suggested a high degree of multifinality in psychopathology, wherein the same risk factor increases risk for multiple types of disorder simultaneously.(23) Does this principle extend to studies of maltreatment timing effects (i.e., do multiple disorder types show similar timing effects)?

  5. Relatedly, the principle of equifinality suggests that the same mental health problems can arise through multiple different developmental histories and risk factors.(23) Does this principle extend to studies of timing effects (i.e., do different types of maltreatment show similar timing effects)?

  6. Are there sex differences in the periods identified across studies?

METHODS

Search strategy and selection criteria

Following PRISMA guidelines (24), we performed a systematic review of human observational studies that tested for maltreatment timing effects using MEDLINE, PsycInfo, and CINAHL. Because some researchers have speculated that sensitive periods might be more readily identified for more “biological” outcomes, we sought to identify both studies examining whether timing of maltreatment was related to psychopathology (e.g., psychiatric disorder diagnosis or higher scores on symptom scales) as well as studies connecting timing of maltreatment to related constructs within other levels of analysis, including neuroimaging (i.e., brain structure and/or functioning), epigenetics (i.e., DNA methylation), psychophysiology (e.g., cortisol reactivity, cortisol awakening response), and behavior (e.g., personality, cognitive test performance). In addition to covering multiple levels of analysis, search terms were also selected to capture a range of Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) constructs including negative valence systems, positive valence systems, cognitive systems, social processes, arousal and regulatory systems, and sensorimotor systems.(25)

Inclusion criteria used in the present review were: (1) studies reported results from human participants, (2) the research was published in English, (3) participants were assessed for one or more relevant outcomes, (4) participants were assessed for exposure to maltreatment alone or alongside other measures of adversity, (5) maltreatment exposure was assessed for at least two non-overlapping timing periods that each preceded the outcome assessment (studies assessing maltreatment exposure across a continuous range of ages were considered to meet this criterion), and (6) the article reported results for at least one test of sensitive period effects, whether that was an implicit (i.e., non-statistical) or explicit comparison of associations between maltreatment occurring during 2 or more different age periods and a given outcome. Studies were labeled as being of “maltreatment” if they assessed any of the following exposures: physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional/verbal abuse, physical neglect, or emotional neglect. Although we required that studies start their assessments of maltreatment prior to age 18 years, we did not exclude any studies based solely on participant age (as many were retrospective studies of adults). These criteria and definitions were established before conducting our systematic search; however, the search procedure was not pre-registered and a public protocol was not prepared.

Our search proceeded in four steps. First, we searched each database from inception through 8/9/2020 using strings shown in the Appendix (pgs. 5–9), exporting articles to the systematic review application, Rayyan.(26) Second, two authors independently screened titles and abstracts of articles retrieved from the search before reviewing the text of potentially eligible studies. Third, two authors screened the text of retrieved articles to identify those meeting all inclusion criteria. Fourth, two authors independently extracted study characteristics and our primary outcome (the sensitive periods identified). Discrepancies at each step were resolved by conferencing. We arranged studies into subgroups based on key characteristics including the outcome of interest, maltreatment type assessed, and other study design features.

All studies were evaluated for risk of bias by two independent raters using an adapted Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies developed by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute.(27) Discrepancies were resolved by a third rater.

Finally, we used permutation testing to statistically evaluate sensitive periods for the most studied outcomes in each level of analysis (i.e., depression, language/cognitive ability, and hippocampal volume). See Appendix for additional details.

Role of the funding sources

The sources providing funding for each of the authors played no role in the study design; collection, analysis, and interpretation of data; writing of the report; or decision to submit the paper for publication.

RESULTS

Search Results

We identified 10,830 studies. Figure 1 summarizes the study selection procedure. 118 independent reports met our inclusion criteria. Of these, 71 tested for associations between maltreatment timing with psychopathology, 8 with neural indices, 5 with DNA methylation, 25 with other RDoC units of analysis, and 9 examined outcomes at multiple levels. Study details are reported in Table S2.

Study Characteristics

Despite a shared focus on maltreatment, the 118 studies differed substantially. First, sample sizes were highly variable, ranging from 15 to 978,647 participants. Most studies (86%) included over 100 participants, with about a third (31%) reporting over 1000 participants (Figure 2A).

Second, maltreatment exposure was measured using reports of individual maltreatment types, maltreatment clusters, and maltreatment experiences co-occurring with other childhood adversities (Figure 2B). Only 28 of the 118 studies (24%) employed prospective assessments; the remainder (76%) used cross-sectional or retrospective designs. Roughly half (52%) used measures that aggregated across different maltreatment types or combined maltreatment with other kinds of adversity. 42% examined only specific maltreatment types (most commonly sexual abuse). The remainder (6%) used both aggregated and specific measures.

Third, several dozen outcomes were studied; psychopathology was the most common. Within our three major outcome categories (psychopathology, imaging, other RDOC units of analysis), the most common outcomes were depression, hippocampal volume, and language/cognitive ability (Figure 2C).

Fourth, researchers operationalized maltreatment timing differently. Definitions of timing included: (1) age at first or worst exposure; (2) presence/absence of maltreatment during different windows of development (e.g., comparing ages 0–11 vs. 12–18); (3) scores capturing the extent of exposure during different windows of development (e.g., frequency, severity, total number of maltreatment events, or number of types of maltreatment), and (4) grouping by recency of maltreatment, often combined with maltreatment onset (e.g., comparing “early-maltreated” to “early-and-recent” and/or “recent-only” maltreated groups). Timing was most often treated as a categorical measure, with development divided into periods roughly corresponding to early-childhood, middle-childhood, and adolescence. Considerably fewer studies used dichotomous or continuous measures of age (Figure 2D).

Fifth, studies varied in the analytic rigor with which they identified sensitive periods. The least rigorous type of study, which we labeled indirect comparison studies (n=41; 35%), described, rather than statistically tested, differences in the magnitude of the association between maltreatment and outcome as a function of exposure timing. Direct comparison studies (n=55; 47%), provided a stronger test by statistically evaluating whether the effects of maltreatment differed by time point. The most rigorous type – competing hypotheses studies (n=20; 17%) – directly compared the effects of maltreatment occurring during 2 or more time periods and evaluated alternative explanations for observed timing effects, including maltreatment accumulation (number of exposure types or times exposed), chronicity (duration), and/or recency (only timing of the most recent exposure). Two of the 118 studies did not fit into any of these categories (classified as “other”) (Figure 2E).

Risk of Bias Across Studies

Our assessment of study quality revealed risk of biased results from this literature was high overall (Table S3). Across studies, the mean number of study quality criteria met was 5.5 (SD = 1.9) out of 13. The most commonly “failed” item was whether the paper presented a justification for selecting or recruiting the number of participants included and/or discussed statistical power. Only 21 studies (18%) included this information; of these, 15 (71%) indicated they were likely underpowered for at least some of the analyses undertaken.

Sensitive Periods for Maltreatment Identified Across Studies

Across all 118 studies, 74% reported at least one sensitive period. Although sensitive periods appeared relatively more consistent when exposure age was dichotomized, no consistent sensitive periods were identified for each of the most commonly-studied outcomes in each major level of analysis (depression, hippocampal volume, and language/cognitive ability) (Figure 3). This observation was confirmed through permutation testing, which indicated insufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis that associations between childhood maltreatment and these outcomes did not vary by maltreatment age (Appendix, pg. 11). No clear timing patterns were observed among the next most studied outcomes within each level as well, though the number of studies within these domains was too small for permutation testing (Appendix, pgs. 12–13).

Though some experts have suggested that sensitive periods might be more readily found for more “biological” outcomes,(22) we observed only weak evidence supporting this hypothesis. For example, while neuroimaging and epigenetic studies were more likely to report sensitive period effects than studies of psychopathology (100% of the 8 neuroimaging studies were positive vs. 80% of 5 epigenetics studies vs. 75% of 71 psychopathology studies), these differences did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the small number of studies in some groups (χ2=2·66, df=2, p=0·264).

The proportion of studies reporting at least one sensitive period were also statistically comparable across categories of analytic rigor (90% of competing hypotheses studies were positive vs. 69% of direct comparison studies vs. 76% of indirect comparison studies; χ2=3·47, df=2, p=0·176).

Investigation of Potential Factors Contributing to Between-Study Heterogeneity

Given the range of factors that could contribute to the mixed findings we observed, stratifying papers by outcome alone may be insufficient. We therefore next tried organizing papers on depression (the most common study outcome) in other ways—including by sample size, analytic rigor, maltreatment assessment approach, study design, and use of diagnosis vs. symptom outcomes—to see if more consistent findings would emerge (Figure 4). They did not. Similarly, no clear patterns emerged when grouping studies according to timing of the outcome assessment—that is, whether depression was measured in adolescence, young adulthood, or in heterogeneous age groups (Appendix, pg. 14).

We tested whether multiple types of mental health problems might share similar sensitive periods by reviewing studies assessing internalizing and externalizing disorders. While these outcomes overlap, they are more etiologically and symptomatically distinct than disorders within the same diagnostic family (e.g., anxiety and depression). Interestingly, although we found significant between-study variation in the sensitive periods observed for both internalizing and externalizing, individual studies tended to find similar periods of peak vulnerability for both outcomes (Appendix, pg. 15).

To explore whether different maltreatment types share similar sensitive periods, we identified 20 studies that tested sensitive-period effects for at least two maltreatment types. Of these studies, 15 reported at least one positive sensitive period finding, with all but 2 (both of which used a dichotomous measure of age) also finding that sensitive periods differed at least somewhat across maltreatment type (Figure 5). However, there was little consistency across studies in the sensitive periods identified for specific types of maltreatment (e.g., physical versus sexual abuse) or broader maltreatment categories (e.g., abuse versus neglect).

Sex differences in the timing of sensitive periods could also be expected, given known sex differences in maltreatment prevalence and timing,(4,28) brain development,(29) and psychopathology risk,(30,31) as well as sex-specific critical periods for hormonal and corticolimbic system functioning in rodents.(32,33) The few studies examining sex differences were almost equally split on whether sex differences were found (Appendix, pg. 16–17).

DISCUSSION

Although nearly three-quarters of the 118 studies included in this systematic review reported timing effects, we found little converging evidence for maltreatment-related sensitive periods. Sensitive periods were not more consistent among studies with similar characteristics, including outcome type, operationalization of maltreatment timing, or study rigor.

Why did we observe such little convergence? There are many possible explanations. One contributor may be that timing and magnitude of sensitive periods has been shown to vary as a function of both genes and environmental input; this variability likely complicates efforts to identify “universal” periods of enhanced neuroplasticity.(31,34) It is also possible that sensitive periods in childhood are both less numerous and their impact more nuanced than periods occurring pre- or perinatally. Periods of neuroplasticity that occur later in development may therefore be considerably more difficult to identify in observational work.

However, neither of these explanations would explain why our maltreatment results contrast those found for maternal deprivation or institutionalization, which suggest larger effects on future psychopathology during the first 6–24 months of life.(20) This discrepancy might arise, in part, because maternal deprivation and institutionalization both represent omissions of expected care, whereas the category of “maltreatment” is more heterogeneous, encompassing both omission of expected care (i.e., neglect) as well as unexpected harm (i.e., abuse). However, the deprivation/institutionalization literature is still characterized by considerable complexity, with some reports indicating a second sensitive period in preadolescence(35) as well as both sex-specific and sleeper (or latent) effects.(8,36,37) Even studies employing randomized assignment to study the effects of institutionalization are unable to disentangle the effects of exposure duration from timing.(19) These sources of complexity also extend to studies of maltreatment, perhaps further contributing to inconsistent findings across studies.

Another possible contributor, cited in nearly all systematic reviews, is methodological heterogeneity. We attempted to understand this heterogeneity by grouping studies with similar characteristics. However, these efforts were hampered in many instances by the small number of studies in some subsets. The possibility of unintended “apples-to-oranges” comparisons makes it difficult to discern if conflicting findings indicate a true lack of sensitive periods or simply methodological and statistical confounds. At the very least, however, they suggest that if maltreatment timing effects exist, they are likely sensitive to aspects of study design. Below, we outline some of the likeliest contributors to these discrepancies and steps to mitigate them going forward (summarized in Table 1).

Defining maltreatment and its timing

We found that the majority (75%) of studies assessing children for multiple types of maltreatment reported distinguishable sensitive periods related to each type. This observation aligns with theories organizing exposures along distinct continuums of adversity that differentially impact neural systems and behavior (e.g., the Dimensional Model of Adversity and Psychopathology).(38,39) Thus, our findings suggest sensitive periods might be clarified by examining specific forms of maltreatment or dimensions of adversity rather than broad or aggregated composites.

Our results additionally suggest a need for greater precision in maltreatment timing assessments. Dichotomous operationalizations of age appeared to contribute to a false sense of consistent findings, which disappeared when fine-grained measures of maltreatment timing were used (see depression findings in Figure 3C). Studies with more fine-grained measures of timing using instruments such as the Maltreatment and Abuse Chronology of Exposure (MACE) scale(28) may also help identify relatively brief (e.g., < 3 years) sensitive periods that would otherwise be missed.(40–42)

Selecting outcomes

Surprisingly, studies using “objective” outcome measures (e.g., brain morphometry, stress physiology) were not statistically more likely to identify sensitive periods than studies of “subjective” outcomes (e.g., psychiatric symptoms). However, the small number of high-quality studies using objective outcomes combined with heterogeneity in the outcomes studied limited our ability to make strong inferences about whether shifting focus to more transdiagnostic, biological measures would better capture maltreatment timing effects.

Regarding “subjective” outcomes, we observed that individual studies assessing both internalizing and externalizing symptoms generally identified overlapping sensitive periods for these constructs. This finding may reflect within-study correlations of these outcomes; it also suggests sensitive periods relevant to maltreatment could manifest as increased vulnerability to the development of general psychopathology, rather than specific disorders or diagnostic families. This latter explanation aligns with decades of research indicating that the effects of maltreatment are non-specific, increasing risk for multiple types of psychiatric disorders simultaneously.(7,43,44) Consequently, our understanding of maltreatment timing effects may benefit from first examining outcomes at the level of general psychopathology, absent compelling theoretical reasons to examine disorder-specific measures.

Study design

Risk of bias was high across studies, as many failed to meet even half of the criteria in our quality assessment tool. One salient limitation was that few studies reported being adequately statistically powered. This finding suggests interpreting the null or mixed findings summarized here as strong evidence that there are no sensitive period effects is likely premature. Future sensitive period studies should address this limitation by both estimating statistical power and pre-registering analytic plans on open science platforms to reduce concerns about multiple testing. Studies should also seek to assess and communicate the extent to which the reported frequency of a given exposure (e.g., sexual abuse) varies across development, as this variation could lead to negligible power to detect sensitive periods at certain ages (e.g., very early in development).

Only a minority of studies (24%) adopted a prospective assessment strategy. Although sensitive periods for depression from prospective studies were not more reliable, known challenges with retrospective measures suggest their predominance is a limitation of the field. Compared to prospective measures, retrospective measures typically overestimate maltreatment effects on subjective outcomes and underestimate maltreatment effects on objective outcomes.(45,46) Retrospective and prospective measures of maltreatment occurrence also show low levels of agreement.(47) To our knowledge, agreement between retrospective and prospective measures of maltreatment timing has not been systematically studied, but might be even lower due to cognitive biases, including telescoping effects (the tendency to perceive recent events as more remote and more distant events as more recent).(48) We therefore encourage investigators examining maltreatment timing effects to use prospective assessment strategies whenever possible.

In addition to more rigorous measurement of maltreatment, we also urge for innovation in the development of new and more objective measures of early-life stressors, as these could reduce measurement error and increase statistical power to detect timing effects. Combining self- and parent-report measures of maltreatment with glucocorticoid levels sampled repeatedly across development, for example, might help researchers to partially account for some of the known issues that plague the maltreatment literature (e.g., recall failure, cognitive biases).(49) Children’s primary teeth have also recently been proposed as a promising new tool, because of their unique ability to record the timing of early-life stressors at a daily and weekly resolution.(50) Teeth may well be a new and noninvasive biospecimen that records life experiences objectively and can guide etiological-based research on how and why psychiatric disorders emerge after experiences of adversity.

Analytic strategy

When assessing sensitive periods in observational studies, two key challenges exist: multicollinearity and multiple testing. Multicollinearity arises because maltreatment exposure can be highly correlated across adjacent ages. Multiple testing issues arise when numerous age ranges or regression models are compared. To mitigate these issues, new analytic strategies have been developed to test sensitive-period effects alongside accumulation-of-risk and other theories, including the Structured Life Course Modeling Approach (SLCMA)(51,52) and machine-learning approaches (e.g., random forest regression).(53) In addition, specification curve analysis can address methodological heterogeneity issues, as this technique examines how effect sizes vary across hundreds or even thousands of models that operationalize key parameters (e.g., maltreatment type, timing) in different ways.(54)

Ruling out alternative explanations for apparent sensitive period effects is critical. What appears as developmental differences in plasticity could instead reflect age-related differences in rates, severity, or accumulation of maltreatment exposure, or other factors that vary by age, such as child coping resources and cognitive capacity (e.g., younger children may internalize maltreatment because they lack the cognitive/socioemotional sophistication to attribute it to parent characteristics). Ruling out all alternative explanations is only possible in an experimental context. Nevertheless, investigators with observational data could increase confidence in their findings by testing as many plausible competing hypotheses as possible.

In short, although theories on the importance of developmental timing exist in multiple fields, including life-course epidemiology,(55) developmental neuroscience,(22) and developmental psychopathology,(56) this systematic review demonstrates that these theories are not yet consistently supported by evidence from human observational studies on child maltreatment. We hope this review inspires more rigorous research to investigate these timing effects, which, if known, could inform the optimal timing of intervention efforts aimed at improving mental health at the individual and population levels.

CONCLUSIONS

Researchers and clinicians should be cautious before deeming a particular phase of development a “sensitive period” for the effects of maltreatment on psychopathology and related constructs, as existing research does not currently converge on any single period of heightened vulnerability. Importantly, however, this observation should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence that maltreatment-related sensitive periods do not exist, as it could also arise due to methodological heterogeneity, study designs characterized by high risk of bias, and low analytic rigor. Studies that directly address these limitations are needed before information regarding maltreatment timing can be productively incorporated into etiological models and clinical decision-making.

Overview of the existing literature on sensitive periods for child maltreatment

(A) Scatterplot showing increases in sample size across sensitive period studies over time. (B) Frequency of child maltreatment exposures examined across sensitive period studies. Studies could be counted more than once in this panel if they examined multiple maltreatment types. “Maltreatment composite” refers to studies that combined multiple types of maltreatment into a single exposure measure. “Adversity composite” refers to studies that combined maltreatment and other non-maltreatment adversities into a single exposure measure. (C) Frequency of specific outcomes examined across sensitive period studies. Studies could be counted more than once in this panel if they examined multiple outcomes. (D) Operationalization of exposure timing across sensitive period studies. Researchers inferred the occurrence of sensitive periods based on findings related to the effects of maltreatment reported at different time points in development, with timing based on: (1) age at first or worst exposure; (2) presence/absence of maltreatment during different windows of development (e.g., between ages 0–11 vs. 12–18, or each year between ages 0–18); (3) scores capturing the extent of exposure during different windows of development (e.g., frequency, severity, total number of maltreatment events, or number of types of maltreatment), or (4) grouping by recency of maltreatment, often combined with maltreatment onset (e.g., comparing an “early-maltreated” to “early-and-recent” and/or “recent-only” maltreated groups). This figure displays the distribution of ways in which these various definitions of “maltreatment age” were operationalized across sensitive period analyses, including dividing age into 2–6 categories or by examining year of age (“continuous”). (E) Frequency of analytic strategies used across sensitive period studies. Indirect comparison studies (n=41; 34.7%) refer to those that describe, rather than directly test, differences in the magnitude of the association between exposure and outcome as a function of exposure timing. Direct comparison studies (n=55; 46.6%) provide a stronger test of the sensitive period hypothesis, because they empirically evaluate whether the effect of exposure at one point in development differs from the effect of exposure at another. Competing hypotheses studies (n=20; 16.9%) are the strongest test, because they directly evaluate alternative theoretical explanations, including accumulation, chronicity, and/or recency. Two studies (1.7%) did not fall cleanly into any of these three categories and thus are not shown.

Table 1.

Key challenges and recommendations for future research on maltreatment timing effects

Key Challenges Future Research Recommendations
• Timing effects likely differ for different types of maltreatment, which peak in frequency and severity at different ages and work through partly separable neural mechanisms. • Stratify maltreatment analyses by maltreatment type or broad dimensions of adversity (e.g., deprivation versus threat) when possible
• Overreliance on crude measures of age of exposure (e.g., dichotomized into “early” versus “late”) may obscure brief sensitive periods and contribute to a false sense of consistency that disappears when finer-grained measures of age are used. • Avoid dichotomous operationalizations of age in favor of multi-categorical or continuous options
• The effects of maltreatment on psychopathology are likely general (i.e., increasing risk of multiple types of mental health problems) rather than specific; yet, most sensitive period studies examine disorder-specific outcomes • Absent compelling reasons to examine disorder-specific measures, consider first examining outcomes at the level of general psychopathology or broad psychiatric spectra (e.g., internalizing symptoms)
• Risk of biased findings appears high as many studies are of poor quality • Future studies can take steps to improve quality by adopting the changes described here as well as ensuring adequate statistical power and pre-registering analyses
• Overreliance on retrospective measures of maltreatment, which show poor agreement with prospective measures, typically overestimate maltreatment effects on subjective outcomes, and may generate biased estimates of maltreatment timing. • Use prospective assessment strategies with frequent assessments whenever possible, and consider incorporating objective measures of exposure (e.g., biomarkers)
• Observational studies of sensitive periods face statistical challenges including multicollinearity (because maltreatment exposure tends to be highly correlated across adjacent ages), multiple testing (which occurs when numerous age ranges or regression models are compared), and many ways to operationalize relevant constructs. • Consider using advanced analytic approaches that account for these statistical challenges including the Structured Life Course Modeling Approach (SLCMA), random forest regression, or specification curve analysis
• Ruling out alternative explanations is critical; what appears as developmental differences in plasticity could instead reflect myriad other age-related differences (e.g., rates of exposure, child coping resources) • Although no study will be able to account for all potential alternative explanations, investigators could increase confidence in their findings by testing as many plausible competing hypotheses as their data allow
