Hybrid tumors with perineurioma components: A systematic review of the literature and illustrative case

Abstract

Purpose:

Hybrid peripheral nerve sheath tumors (HPNST) are a newly recognized class of peripheral nerve sheath tumor, composed of a at least two areas characteristic of perineurioma, schwannoma, or neurofibroma. The literature consists only of case reports and small series; therefore, we present an illustrative case and an analysis of all reported cases of HPNST with a perineurioma component in the literature.

Methods:

A systematic search of the literature was performed to identify all reported cases of hybrid perineurioma-schwannoma or perineurioma-neurofibroma in the world’s literature. Individual cases were analyzed for demographics, clinical features, imaging, and outcomes.

Results:

A total of 159 cases were identified across 41 studies. Hybrid tumors tended to present in mid-adulthood (median 38.5 years), predominantly affected females (57%, 89/156), as a painless (63%, 63/100) mass or swelling. Ten patients (10/74, 14%) had a history of Neurofibromatosis 1 and 2 patients a history of neurofibromatosis 2 (2/74, 3%). The majority (78%, 122/157) of cases occurred superficially, most commonly in the lower extremity (25%, 39/157). Perineurioma-schwannoma was the most reported (86%, 137/159) pathologic diagnosis, with 3 cases presenting with malignant features. Two cases reoccurred after resection.

Conclusion:

HPNST tend to present in mid-adulthood present as a slowly progressive, painless, superficial masses, with have a heterogenous appearance on imaging. These entities pose a unique diagnostic challenge, and likely remain underrecognized in the literature and current clinical practice. They pose low risk of recurrence or malignant transformation, and future work regarding the association with Neurofibromatosis and genetic profiles is needed.

INTRODUCTION

Among benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors, the term perineurioma denotes a rare entity comprised of a localized proliferation of perineural cells[36]. These benign tumors have traditionally been subclassified based on their relationship to the histological boundaries of the nerve from which they originate as intraneural perineuriomas[36] or extraneural (soft tissue) perineuriomas[51]; other histologic variants – sclerosing[20,35,9,79], plexiform[84,58,43], granular[4], and reticular[76,58,47] perineurioma – have also been acknowledged in the literature. Clinically, intraneural perineuriomas tend to present as slowly progressive motor or sensory mononeuropathy in adolescents or young adults[52]. In contrast, soft tissue perineuriomas tend to present in mid-adulthood as a painless mass and carry a more favorable prognosis[34,22].

More recently in the literature, new case reports have described an uncommon variant of peripheral nerve sheath tumor composed of a proliferation of more than one histologic cell line: perineuriomas occurring in combination with schwannomas[59] or neurofibromas[83], denoted as hybrid peripheral nerve sheath tumor (HPNST)[19]. Indeed, these entities were recently recognized in the 4th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors of Soft tissue and Bone[21] and the revised 4th edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System[54]. However, due to the novelty of this entity and the availability of only case reports and small case series for HPNST with a perineurioma component, the pathogenesis and clinical features of this entity remain largely unknown. Herein, we aim to present one illustrative case from our institution and a systematic review with an analysis of the literature to summarize the knowledge regarding clinical features, histology and immunohistochemistry, imaging features, and genetic profile of hybrid perineurioma-schwannoma and perineurioma-neurofibroma in the world literature.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Literature Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

The systematic review of the literature was performed in agreement with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines[62]. A medical librarian designed a detailed electronic search at our institution with contributions from the study’s principal investigator. The search was conducted using Ovid, Medline/PubMed, Ovid Embase, Ovid Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews performed using the keywords “nerve tumor,” “nerve sheath neoplasms,” or “perineurioma,” and “hybrid” from January 1, 1946, to September 15, 2021.

Two study co-authors (KAL and DDM) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text evaluation of all articles identified in the initial search. All case reports, case series, and cohort studies on hybrid perineurioma-schwannoma or perineurioma-neurofibroma that were confirmed by histopathology were included in the study. To be included in the study, cases required tissue diagnosis with reported histology and immunohistochemistry included in the manuscript; perineural areas needed to report positivity for EMA and negativity for S100 protein, and conversely schwannomatous or neurofibromatous areas staining positively for S100 protein and negatively for EMA, as well as ultrastructural features consistent with the diagnosis of HPNST. Studies that did not meet the above immunohistochemical criteria for perineurioma-schwannoma or perineurioma-neurofibroma HPNST, included duplicate series, review articles or commentary, or did not report patient-level data were excluded from the analysis. Non-English articles were translated by native speakers and included if they met the above inclusion criteria. Any inclusion-related discrepancy was resolved by the senior author (RJS) discussion.

Data Extraction and Analysis

Study-specific data (author, year, country, number of patients with HPNST) were included. Additionally, patient-level data were abstracted from the studies: age at onset of symptoms and clinical presentation, sex, duration of symptoms prior to presentation, chief complaint and associated symptoms, relevant past medical history, including Neurofibromatosis 1 (NF1) or 2 (NF2) or prior malignancy, birthmarks/congenital signs of syndromes, gross features, tumor size, availability of imaging and features imaging features, ultrastructural histologic features, immunohistochemical staining, genetic profile and analysis, treatment strategy, clinical follow-up and outcome, the persistence of lesion following resection, and recurrence were recorded.

All data were analyzed and reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) or as frequencies and proportions as appropriate. Missing data were also recorded for all studies and variables of interest. The data were summarized for demographics, clinical presentation and features, histology and immunohistochemistry, genetic profiles, and features on imaging. All imaging presented in the included studies was further reviewed and summarized by our radiology coauthor.

RESULTS

Illustrative Case:

A 38-year-old man presented for evaluation of a left shoulder mass he noted about three months prior to presentation at our clinic. The mass was associated with a “toothache-like” discomfort when direct pressure is placed on the area, with associated tingling and numbness that does not radiate down his extremity. He also noted slight weakness in that extremity with overhead activities. Outside CT report identified a left posterior upper shoulder mass with prominent lymph nodes in the left axilla, although with a benign appearance which prompted a referral to our clinic.

On examination, a prominent, visible, and easily palpable mass in the posterior left axilla was immobile, sold, and firm. Palpation and percussion of the mass did not reproduce any symptoms. His muscle strength was normal in abduction and external rotation. He had no features or history of neurofibromatosis, although he had a medical history significant for a melanoma excision from his left posterior shoulder, without subsequent radiation therapy. MRI performed at our institution, shown in Figure 1, was significant for a heterogeneous ovoid mass in the deltoid muscle; the mass is not explicitly associated with a nerve or the myotendinous junction and measured 3.7 × 2.8 × 3.4 cm in the most significant dimensions. Following intravenous gadolinium, the mass showed irregular enhancement and central necrosis, which was non-specific and worrisome for a primary or metastatic sarcoma. He subsequently underwent a biopsy, which was consistent with benign soft tissue perineurioma. He subsequently underwent marginal excision, and the final pathology was significant for hybrid perineurioma-neurofibroma.

At follow-up three months later, he noted some mild, muscular type pain extending from the scapular region superficially to the proximal arm. He denied any neuropathic pain, loss of strength, or new numbness. On further follow-up with his primary care provider, his pain had resolved.

Systematic Review

An electronic literature search yielded 204 results after the removal of duplicates. One paper was identified by examination of references of the screened papers. After initial screening and full text-evaluation lead to inclusion of 41 studies with a total of 159 cases that met inclusion criteria (Figure 2) [1,2,5,7,8,10,11,13–16,23,26–28,30,32,37–40,44,45,48,50,53,55–57,59,61,63,64,66,68,69,71,74,77,80,83]. Consensus was achieved on all studies by the two reviewing authors. Most articles were published in English (n = 38), with three articles published in Spanish. Seven articles were published between 2000–2009 (72 cases), 27 articles between 2010–2019 (49 cases), and seven articles between 2020–2021 (38 cases).

Of the 159 cases reported in the literature, patients tended to present in mid-adulthood, with the median age (count, interquartile range – IQR) at an evaluation of 38.5 years (n =159, 28–54 years). Patients first noted symptoms about 24 months (n = 51, 6–69 months) prior to presentation for clinical evaluation, around the age of 32 years (n = 51, 20–50 years). The majority of patients were female (89/156, 57%). The most common indication for evaluation was a painless palpable mass or swelling (63/100, 63%), painful mass or swelling (11/100, 11%), pain alone (9/100, 9%), abdominal symptoms (6/100, 6%), incidental finding on imaging (4/100, 4%), pulmonary symptoms (2/100, 2%), nasal obstruction (2/100, 2%), pathologic fracture (1/100, 1%), and paresthesias (1/100, 1%). Ten patients had a history of NF1 (10/74, 14%), and two had a history of NF2 (2/74, 3%). Six patients had a previous history of malignancy; two patients had a history of pulmonary carcinoma; and one had a history of uterine carcinoma, adenocarcinoma of the colon, rectal carcinoma, and osteosarcoma. Four patients had a history of trauma to the location of their hybrid tumor (4/56, 7%). Demographics and clinical features are summarized in Table 1.

The majority, 78% (122/157), of hybrid tumors in this study occurred superficially (epidermis, dermis, or subdermal layer). The most common locations included the lower extremity (39/157, 25%), trunk (30/ 157, 19%) upper extremity (29/157, 18%), and the head/neck (24/157, 15%). Other locations that were reported in the literature included the gastrointestinal tract (10/157, 6%), nasal mucosa (5/157, 3%), brachial plexus (5/157, 3%), spinal cord (5/157, 3%), mediastinum (3/157, 2%), intraosseous (2/157, 1%), lumbosacral plexus (2/157, 1%), with one case being reported in the internal auditory meatus, orbit, and cerebellopontine angle, respectively (Table 2).

Based on ultrastructure on histology as well as immunohistochemical staining, the majority of cases described were consistent with perineurioma-schwannoma (137/159, 86%), followed by perineurioma neurofibroma (20/159, 13%), and two lesions demonstrating hybrid perineurioma-neurofibroma-schwannoma morphology (2/159, 1%). Of the HPNSTs that did not demonstrate malignant features, the Ki-67 index was <2% in 40 cases, between 2–5% in four cases, and 5.7% in one case. Malignant features were present in three cases (n = 159, 2%)[26,64,40], with the malignancies classified as low-grade malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors in all three cases. Of these patients, one had NF1, none had NF2, and none had any history of prior radiation to these sites. Of the three malignant hybrid peripheral nerve sheath tumors reported, one paper described “numerous mitotic figures.” Conversely, one hybrid tumor reported a Ki67 of 10% with four mitoses per high-powered field, mild nuclear pleomorphism prior to reoccurrence, and Ki67 of 20% when the lesion reoccurred. Finally, the last malignant hybrid tumor demonstrated 5–10 mitosis per high-powered field, with moderate nuclear atypia and areas of necrosis.

Investigations into the genetic profile of hybrid tumors with a perineuriomatous component yielded ten lesions that demonstrated CHD7-VGLL3 rearrangement in fluorescence in situ hybridization studies[14]. Similarly, two tumors demonstrated a rearrangement of CHD9-VGLL3, two of MANDL1-VGLL3, and one DST-BRAF rearrangement[14]. Further investigations demonstrated two lesions with a loss of 22q[29] and one tumor with a point mutation in exon 15 of the NF2 gene[45]. In contrast, five other tumors did not have an abnormality of chromosome 22[16,40]. One lesion did not have any rearrangements of SYT[49]. Five tumors were investigated for H3K27me3 activity, which was noted to be intact in all cases[14].

Fourteen papers presented imaging findings in the form of figures in the manuscript[7,28,48,53,13,16,23,26,38,65,67,72,10,75]. MRI was the most common modality presented (n = 9), followed by radiographs (3), CT scans (2), and ultrasound and positron emission tomography (PET) scans. On MRI, these lesions tended to present as unencapsulated, spherical, or ovoid lesions, with marked areas of heterogeneity on T2 weighted imaging and gadolinium administration.

Operative interventions managed all cases, with all reported cases being managed solely by surgical resection of the lesion (157/158), and one case managed with curettage and internal fixation of the lesion (1/158, 1%)[13]. In lesions that did not demonstrate any malignant features, the median follow-up was 15.5 months (range 1–156, n = 56). Eight cases reported improvement or resolution of preoperative symptoms. Of the cases with follow-up, 6 cases reported some residual disease on postoperative imaging (n=56). One recurrence[32,26] was reported in the literature, which reoccurred 15 months postoperatively without management information. Of the three cases of hybrid tumors that demonstrated features of malignancy included in our study, two papers did not report any follow-up data after surgical resection. In contrast, one paper reported that the patient developed a reoccurrence of symptoms five months postoperatively after a period of complete resolution of symptoms. Recurrence was noted in MR imaging, and the patient was subsequently treated with re-excision and localized radiotherapy.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review of the world literature was undertaken to summarize the clinical presentation, histologic features, genetic profile, radiological findings, and clinical outcomes for biopsy-proven HPNST with a perineurioma component (perineurioma-schwannoma or perineurioma-neurofibroma). Our investigation yielded 159 distinct cases across 41 studies, representing the most extensive collection of cases of hybrid perineurioma-schwannoma and perineurioma-neurofibroma reported in the literature[15,60]. Clinically, these lesions tend to present in mid-adulthood (median onset of symptoms 32 years) as painful or painless superficial masses and come to clinical attention about two years after initial symptoms are noted. Although cases have been reported in the literature, malignant transformation and recurrence of these lesions are rare.

Clinical Presentation and Outcomes

Based on our literature review, the clinical presentation of hybrid perineurioma-schwannoma and perineurioma-neurofibroma is heterogeneous, although most come to clinical attention as a palpable mass. Of the two subtypes of perineurioma, soft tissue presents in adulthood as solitary, painless subcutaneous masses in the legs and trunk, with an almost 2:1 predilection for females[22]; clinicoradiographic features are summarized in Table 3 Conversely, intraneural perineuriomas typically present in adolescence or early adulthood as a slowly progressive, painless mononeuropathy with significant loss of motor and/or sensory function[52]. Of the cases in our study, 74% presented as a painless subcutaneous mass without any other associated motor loss, resembling soft tissue perineuriomas. While a select few studies of HPNST did note the presence of mild motor weakness or sensory loss localized to the tumor site, these lesions tended not to be directly associated with an identifiable nerve. They could be attributed mainly to compressive neuropathy by the mass.

Interestingly, while we noted that this lesion was more common in females (57%), this was only slightly compared to soft tissue perineuriomas. Of the cases we analyzed of HPNST that did not present as a subcutaneous mass, the majority occurred in the gastrointestinal tract. While there are no case reports of intraneural perineuriomas occurring in the mucosa of the gastrointestinal tract, there have been cases of soft tissue perineuriomas in the literature, although uncommon[33]. Based on our review of the literature, the majority of HPNSTs present most similarly to soft tissue perineuriomas when compared to the intraneural variant, which should be considered during diagnosis and surgical planning.

Interestingly, there were three cases (34, 42, 54) of HPNST with malignant features on biopsy, with one of these cases recurring following surgical resection. While intraneural perineuriomas and soft tissue perineuriomas are classically defined as benign entities, there have been few reported cases of peripheral nerve sheath tumors demonstrating perineural cell differentiation[67,31,42,82,65,73,18] of low histologic grade, with Ki-67 of less than 20%[67]; of these cases, one patient later had local recurrence, and one patient returned with distant metastases years after his initial surgical resection. Similarly, in this study, the three reported HPNSTs with malignant features were low grade and had a Ki-67 of 10% at initial presentation and 20% upon reoccurrence, with less than ten mitoses per high-powered field. Recurrence was noted in a single patient treated with reoperation and localized radiotherapy without reported outcomes and follow-up[26]. HPNSTs have a low potential for malignant transformation and tend to present as low-grade malignancies with a rare risk of recurrence. However, further literature is required to understand these lesions true incidence, management strategy, and prognosis.

Imaging

Our review of hybrid perineuriomas showed that, in general, the lesions presented as well-circumscribed, rounded, or ovoid soft tissue masses that were not specifically associated with nerves, without distinctive imaging features indistinguishable from other soft tissue tumors, including soft tissue perineurioma[8]. In contrast, intraneural perineuriomas commonly have pathognomonic imaging features, including identifiable origin from a nerve with homogenous increased T2 signal and avid, smooth enhancement[78].

Genetic Profile

In the current literature, the molecular pathogenesis of peripheral nerve sheath tumors remains incompletely understood, with even less available data regarding the genetic profile of HPNST. Among PNST, mutations in the NF1 gene are associated with neurofibromas[76]. In contrast, mutations in the NF2 gene are more common in schwannomas[3]. Furthermore, genetic analysis of intraneural perineurioma has demonstrated abnormalities on chromosome 22[17] and TRAF7[46], a gene commonly associated with meningiomas, while the soft tissue variant is associated with abnormalities of chromosome 22[22] and chromosome 17[12,40]. Indeed, in this present study, we were able to identify three which HPNSTs that demonstrated abnormalities of chromosome 22 [29,40], with one study reporting a mutation occurring in the NF2 gene. However, other tumors were negative for any abnormalities in this locus. While there are inconsistent findings in the literature, this may represent a common pathway for pathogenesis of HPNST with the perineuriomas, schwannomas, or comorbid neurofibromatosis; however, further large-scale studies are warranted to explore this association further. Unfortunately, no reported cases in the literature examined the association between the development of HPNST and mutations in TRAF7. Therefore, this association remains unclear. Finally, we also included a study regarding genetic investigations of HPNST that reported tumors bearing molecular novel rearrangements of CHD7-VGLL3, and CHD9-VGLL3[14], potentially suggesting an alternative, heterogeneous molecular pathway for the development of hybrid schwannoma-perineurioma. Previous studies have implicated VGLL3 in the process of nerve formation and neural crest migration[70], with amplification associated with the development of sarcoma[41,24,73], while CHD has been shown to promote neural crest formation and neural progenitor differentiation[6,81]. In a study by Harder et al. [25], HPNSTs with neurofibromas and schwannomas have also been described in the context of schwannomatosis, although whether this association is present in patients with HPNST with perineurioma components remains unknown in the literature. Unfortunately, none of the malignant hybrid peripheral nerve sheath tumor cases included in this study underwent any genetic analysis. However, despite these associations, future studies are warranted to explore the role of these proteins in the pathogenesis of HPNST to better understand the true pathogenesis of this lesion and its association with neurofibromatosis.

Strengths and Limitations

This study presents the most extensive collection of HPNST with a perineural component that is presented in the current literature with illustrative cases from our institution. We reviewed the literature comprehensively and collected individual patient-level data, including non-English articles. However, there are some limitations to this study. Firstly, there was significant variability in data reporting between different studies that were included, with certain variables missing significant proportions of data for analysis, which limited the ability to evaluate all features of this tumor reliably. This, along with the risk of reporting bias in the literature, may lead to inadvertent high or low reporting of key clinical features. Furthermore, the observational nature of all the included studies and the risk of selection bias in this systematic review remain limitations.

CONCLUSION

Hybrid peripheral nerve sheath tumors with a perineuriomatous component tend to differ in clinicoradiologic features compared to perineurioma. They present in mid-adulthood, are more common in females and are slowly progressive, painless superficial masses, occasionally associated with Neurofibromatosis, with low risk of recurrence or malignant transformation. These entities pose a diagnostic challenge and likely remain underreported and underrecognized in the literature and current clinical practice. Our study highlights some areas for future literature due to a paucity of literature data regarding imaging and the genetics profile in HPNST, which are significant gaps in knowledge that need to be addressed in further work.
