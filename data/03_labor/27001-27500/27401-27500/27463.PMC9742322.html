Abstract

Self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITBs), which include suicidal ideation (SI), suicide attempts (SA), and nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI), are prevalent and associated with impairments in functioning and elevated risk of suicide deaths. Preventing suicide is a complex problem, with numerous systems likely contributing to the onset and maintenance of SITBs, and there is a critical need to identify more precise predictors of risk. Positive valence systems (PVS) are an understudied domain with promise for improving understanding of risk processes underlying SITBs. In this systematic review, we evaluate the evidence for the potential role of altered PVS function in SI, SA, and/or NSSI, including alterations in reward responsiveness, learning, and valuation assessed through behavioral, physiological and circuit measures. Results provide preliminary support for associations between distinct aspects of PVS function and alterations in SITBs. Specifically, SI appears to be characterized by low reward responsiveness, whereas little research has examined reward responsiveness in SA, and NSSI has been characterized by hyper-responsiveness to rewards. Alterations in reward learning and valuation are commonly examined in SA, with some evidence that they may be more strongly associated with attempts than SI or NSSI. At the same time, the literature is limited in that some constructs are commonly examined in one form of SITBs but not others. Further, research is predominantly cross-sectional and focused on adults raising questions about the role of PVS function in developmental pathways to SITBs. We conclude by integrating the research to date and highlighting promising directions for future research.

Over 47,000 people in the U.S. and over 700,000 people worldwide die by suicide each year (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020; World Health Organization, 2020), and concerningly, the prevalence of self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITBs) is even higher (National Survey of Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), 2019; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020; Cipriano et al., 2017). SITBs include suicidal ideation (SI), ranging from passive thoughts of death to active thoughts of killing oneself, nonfatal suicide attempts (SA), defined as engaging in self-harm with any intent to die, and nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI), defined as purposefully self-injuring without any intention to die. SITBs are among the most established predictors of future SA and death by suicide (Franklin et al., 2017; Klonsky et al., 2013; Ribeiro et al., 2016). Further, while SITBs are often considered in the context of depressive illnesses, these thoughts and behaviors are broadly associated with a wide range of other disorders including anxiety (Daukantaitė et al., 2021; Fergusson et al., 2005; Mars et al., 2014) and substance use disorders (Goldman-Mellor et al., 2014). Our ability to identify those at greatest risk for SITBs remains limited and our interventions are only modestly effective (Fox et al., 2020; Franklin et al., 2017). Correlates and risk factors associated with specific types of SITBs are thought to be distinct, given that many more people have thoughts of suicide than those who go on to engage in SA (May & Klonsky, 2016). As such, there is a critical need to identify risk processes underlying specific forms of SITBs to inform targeted intervention efforts.

Overview of Positive Valence Systems

Over the past decade, the National Institute of Mental Health Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework has promoted research to characterize psychopathology at the extreme ends of core dimensions that can be assessed across units of analysis from molecules and cells to circuits and physiology to behavior and self-report. SITB research has focused heavily on dysregulation within negative valence systems (Glenn et al., 2018), which has been critical in informing understanding of SITBs, but no single system underlies SITB risk, and there is a need for research examining new methods and processes to identify robust combinations of predictors (Franklin et al., 2017). RDoC’s positive valence systems has been highlighted as a priority for suicide research (National Institute of Mental Health, 2020), raising questions about what is already known in this area and directions for future research.

PVS is broadly defined as the processes involved in motivated behaviors and hedonic responses, and can be subdivided into three subconstructs: reward responsiveness, reward learning, and reward valuation (National Institute of Mental Health, 2021). Table 1 summarizes tasks and measures of PVS subconstructs in the SITB literature (National Advisory Mental Health Council Workgroup, 2016). Reward responsiveness reflects processes that drive responses to reinforcers, including anticipation of potential rewards, initial response to the receipt of rewards, and satiation with repeated reward receipt. Reward responsiveness is commonly assessed using electroencephalogram (EEG)/event-related potential (ERP) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to measure activation of reward-related brain systems (Kujawa et al., 2020; Sescousse et al., 2013). Tasks in which participants win money are commonly used to examine brain function and behavioral performance as a function of anticipation of rewards and in response to reward feedback. Reward responsiveness is also examined through physiological responses to appetitive stimuli, such as images of desirable foods or erotic scenes.

Reward learning refers to forming associations between stimuli, contexts, or behaviors and reinforcers, and can be further subdivided into probabilistic and reinforcement learning, reward prediction error, and habit. Reward learning is commonly assessed behaviorally using tasks that measure tendencies to select responses that are most likely to be rewarded. Finally, reward valuation refers to estimating how positive potential outcomes are depending on factors like probability of reward, delay before receipt, and amount of effort. Reward valuation is commonly measured behaviorally using delay discounting tasks in which participants select between smaller monetary rewards delivered immediately or larger rewards delivered at varying temporal delays. An individual’s rate of discounting the value of rewards as a function of the delay can then be calculated as an indicator of reward valuation. Both reward learning and valuation tasks can also be used with physiological and neuroimaging methods to examine neural processes involved as associations are formed, options are weighed, and selections made (Bartra et al., 2013; Dombrovski & Hallquist, 2017).

The Potential Role of PVS Function in SITBs

PVS dysfunction is known to play a key role in forms of psychopathology that commonly co-occur with SITBs, include depression, bipolar disorders, and substance use disorders (Baskin-Sommers & Foti, 2015; Gili et al., 2019; Nusslock & Alloy, 2017). Yet not all patients with these disorders report SITBs and questions remain about the associations between PVS function and SITBs specifically. Prior theoretical and empirical work highlights a potential role of PVS in SITBs, although the conceptual models and directions of associations likely vary across specific forms of SITBs.

Impaired reward learning and decision-making is theorized to play a key role in SA (Dombrovski & Hallquist, 2017, 2022). These impairments are thought to manifest in part as preferences for immediate reinforcement through the relief of emotional pain by engaging in SA, rather than waiting for potential delayed future rewards that come from continuing to live through painful times. Relatedly, research on motivations for SA suggests that immediate reinforcement may play a key role, including a desire to feel something and communicate to others (Bryan et al., 2013; Nock et al., 2007). Further, hopelessness about the future, which can be conceptualized as low valuation of possible future reinforcers, is a common motivation for SA (May et al., 2016). Importantly, however, SI and SA are characterized by distinct risk processes (May & Klonsky, 2016), and alterations in learning and decision-making process may underlie SA, specifically.

NSSI is thought to be motivated in part by both negative and positive reinforcers, such as feelings of relief, improved mood, and satisfaction (Hooley & Franklin, 2018). When a person decides to engage in NSSI, these purported benefits must be weighed against the costs of self-harm (Hooley & Franklin, 2018; Selby et al., 2014), highlighting a potential role of PVS processes, particularly disruptions of reward valuation. Together, these disparate lines of research point to a relationship between PVS function and SITBs, but also raise important questions regarding the specific PVS components that are altered and the extent to which specific SITBs are characterized by common or distinct alterations.

Theories of PVS function in SI are less well-established than those of SA and NSSI. Some studies, which we review in detail below, indicate blunted reward responsiveness in SI. Importantly, similar patterns of low reward responsiveness have been observed in depression (Keren et al., 2018), but there is evidence that associations between SI and low reward responsiveness persist even when accounting for symptoms of depression or anhedonia (Albanese et al., 2019; Hutchinson et al., 2021; Tsypes et al., 2019; Weinberg et al., 2016). This could indicate that those with particularly blunted reward responsiveness, beyond that typically observed in anhedonia or depression, are at greatest risk of experiencing SI. Taken together, existing theory and research points to a need for an integrative model of the role of PVS in multiple forms of SITBs.

Current Review

Given PVS function is a priority area for NIMH suicide research, a synthesis of the existing literature is needed. Further, a review of the state of the literature beyond self-report measures is needed to understand the clinical utility of other units of analysis. Although several prior reviews have addressed related topics, this is the first systematic review to integrate evidence for alterations in each of the PVS subconstructs across units of analysis. In a prior meta-analytic review of impulsivity and SITBs, significant associations were found with NSSI and SA (Liu et al., 2017). Similarly, in a more recent meta-analysis, inhibitory control and impulsive decision-making were associated with SITBs (McHugh et al., 2019). Although these meta-analyses included tasks relevant to PVS function, they also included a broader range of “cold” executive processing measures without potential rewards and did not consider reward responsiveness at the neural and physiological level. Two recent meta-analytic reviews found evidence for poorer performance on decision making tasks, including the Iowa Gambling Task (IGT) and Cambridge Gambling Task (CGT), in those with SA histories (Perrain et al., 2021; Sastre-Buades et al., 2021). Finally, Glenn et al. (2018) examined the evidence of longitudinal associations between RDoC domains broadly defined and SI and SA, excluding NSSI. We extend these reviews by synthesizing the literature on a broad range of PVS-relevant tasks and integrating data at the behavioral, physiological, and circuit levels of analysis, including both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, as well as studies assessing NSSI. Critically, we also evaluate evidence for common and distinct PVS patterns across SITBs and highlight gaps in the literature to move RDoC-informed approaches to SITB research forward.

Literature Search

The initial search was conducted on June 15, 2021 and updated on March 24, 2022 (see Figure 1). The primary search used subject terms for SITBs (self-injurious behavior, suicide prevention, attempted suicide, suicidal ideation, self-mutilation, suicide, or self-inflicted wounds) and PVS (external rewards, rewards, positive reinforcement, reward learning, preferred rewards, positive emotions, internal rewards, delay discounting, reward allocation, social reinforcement, monetary rewards, or decision making) with the Thesaurus feature and explode function in PsycInfo. Search results were limited to human empirical studies published between 1980-present. A similar search was also conducted in PubMed and Google Scholar to identify missed studies in our initial search. We also reviewed introduction sections and prior meta-analyses to identify other studies.

Initial review of titles and abstracts was conducted to remove duplicates and those that were clearly outside of the scope. Full texts were then reviewed and coded by 2 independent raters, with group consensus used to resolve discrepancies. Raters determined whether each paper included a measure of SI, SA, or NSSI that differentiated between these constructs (studies of suicidality generally or combined SITB groups were excluded) and examined individual or group differences on a PVS measure. Measures that differentiated between SI, SA, and NSSI were necessary to examine whether patterns of PVS function differ by SITB. PVS measures were defined as behavioral (e.g., reaction time, decisions made during a task), physiological (e.g., EEG/ERP), or circuit (e.g., fMRI) measures in response to positively valenced, motivational, or reward-related stimuli or contexts (e.g., pleasant images, decision-making in the context of perceived or actual rewards, anticipation of or receipt of rewards). Studies that used only a self-report measure of PVS (e.g., self-reported positive affect) were excluded. Included papers (n = 57) were organized by SITB type and PVS subconstruct. Although some tasks disentangle specific PVS processes, others assess multiple overlapping processes and lack a clear fit in a single subconstruct (National Advisory Mental Health Council Workgroup, 2016). For the purposes of this review, we organized each of the tasks and measures into a single subconstruct of PVS that it assesses most closely (Table 1). Below, we describe findings in each of these domains.

SI and PVS Function

Reward Responsiveness

Studies examining SI are presented in Table 2. Eight studies examined SI and reward responsiveness, although methods varied. Five studies used ERP to measure neural responses to monetary reward feedback (Tsypes et al., 2019), pleasant emotional images (Albanese et al., 2019; Kudinova et al., 2016; Weinberg et al., 2016), or cues indicating the potential for affective rewards (i.e., pleasant images) (Song et al., 2019). Two studies found that SI was associated with blunted neural responses; in one study of children, this finding emerged in response to reward relative to loss feedback (i.e., reward positivity [RewP] difference score) in a community sample of children (Tsypes et al., 2019), and in the second study, this finding emerged in response to pleasant images (i.e., late positive potential [LPP]) in a clinical sample of adults (Weinberg et al., 2016). A third study indicated that SI in a community sample of adults was not associated with the LPP during passive viewing but was associated with less ability to upregulate responses to rewarding images (Albanese et al., 2019). In contrast, a study of undergraduate students found an association between SI and the LPP in response to dysphoric, but not pleasant, emotional images (Kudinova et al., 2016). Further, in a sample of adults with depression, SI was associated with enhanced neural responses to cues indicating potential incentives and receipt of feedback, although these patterns did not appear specific to reward cues and some associations were also apparent for depressive symptoms, raising questions about specificity (Song et al., 2019).

Two studies used fMRI to measure activation of brain circuits during anticipation and receipt of monetary and social rewards. In one clinical sample of adults, SI showed unique associations with ventral striatum functional connectivity relative to other symptoms of depression, such as anhedonia. Specifically, SI was associated with greater left ventral striatum coupling with middle and superior PFC and ACC for losses relative to rewards. In contrast, anhedonia was associated with stronger coupling within the striatum and depression severity with ventral striatum-precuneus connectivity. Although replication of these patterns in larger samples is needed, this could indicate that high SI is associated with greater performance monitoring with negative outcomes (Quevedo et al., 2017). In a longitudinal study of adolescent girls recruited from the community, reduced activation of the striatum in anticipation of social reward feedback prospectively predicted greater likelihood of SI during the COVID-19 pandemic (Hutchinson et al., 2021).

Finally, one study examined reaction time in monetary and affective incentive delay tasks as an indicator of motivation to obtain rewards in adult patients with depression (Xie et al., 2014). In the affective incentive delay task only, SI was associated with slower reaction time in the context of potential rewards, but faster reaction time in the context of potential punishment.

Taken together, these studies provide preliminary support for associations between SI, blunted neural responses to rewards, and reduced motivation to obtain potential rewards. There is preliminary evidence of prospective associations (Hutchinson et al., 2021), but more research is needed to determine whether low reward responsiveness may be a vulnerability underlying the later emergence of SI. Still, reward responsiveness findings in SI are mixed, likely due to variability in sample characteristics and task design.

Reward Learning

Seven studies examined SI and reward learning. Only one study used fMRI, finding that adults with a history of SA and SI demonstrated alterations in ventromedial PFC activation during reward learning, but those with SI only did not differ from depressed patients with no SI and non-clinical comparison participants (Brown et al., 2020). Relatedly, in a behavioral study, difficulties with reward learning emerged for adults with SA relative to those with active SI and those in other comparison groups (Dombrovski et al., 2019). Two other behavioral studies failed to find effects of SI on decision making (Adrián Alacreu-Crespo et al., 2019; Clark et al., 2011).

Three behavioral studies suggested the possibility of subtle alterations in reward learning in SI, including non-significant trends for SI associations with poorer learning performance in adolescents and adults (Dombrovski et al., 2010; Sheftall et al., 2015), and poorer performance in adults with both SA with current SI and current SI only compared to those with a history of SA without current SI (Westheide et al., 2008).

In summary, there is limited evidence for alterations in reward learning in those with SI only, and preliminary support for specificity in associations with SA, suggesting a potential risk process for differentiating those with SI only from those who engage in SA.

Reward Valuation

Four studies (three articles) examined associations between SI and reward valuation. Greater discounting of delayed rewards was found in one study of adults with SI and adults with SA compared to adults with depression without SI/SA (Cáceda et al., 2014; Tsypes et al., 2022). However, three studies failed to find differences in delay discounting for adults with SI relative to comparisons (Millner et al., 2020; Tsypes et al., 2021).

Summary: SI and PVS Function

There is preliminary support for a link between SI and low responsiveness to rewards and pleasant images, but more limited evidence of alterations in reward learning and valuation, which may be more specifically linked to SA than SI alone. Studies rely predominantly on adult samples and cross-sectional designs, limiting conclusions related to developmental course and potential vulnerabilities.

SA and PVS Function

Reward Responsiveness

Studies examining SA are presented in Table 3. Two cross-sectional adult studies examined neural reward responsiveness and SA using EEG/ERP. One study found evidence of blunted neural responses in anticipation of incentives vs. neutral feedback and response to reward vs. loss feedback (Tsypes et al., 2021). The second study, however, failed to find effects of SA history on ERPs to pleasant images (Albanese et al., 2019).

Reward Learning

Twenty-seven studies examined reward learning and SA, primarily using behavioral measures. Five studies assessed neural indices of reward learning using fMRI. Three of these studies found differences between those with SA histories and clinical comparisons during the IGT, although in varying regions of the brain (Fabrice Jollant et al., 2010; Olié et al., 2015; Pan et al., 2013). In one study, adults with SA demonstrated reduced left occipital cortex and OFC activation during risky choices relative to a control group (Fabrice Jollant et al., 2010). In another study, adults with SA demonstrated reduced dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC) activation during risky choices and greater dlPFC, ACC, and OFC activation in response to wins relative to patient comparisons (Olié et al., 2015). In a third study, adolescents with SA histories showed increased caudate activation relative to healthy comparisons and reduced middle temporal gyrus, hippocampal, and thalamic activation relative to patient comparisons during low-risk choices (Pan et al., 2013). Finally, two studies using reward learning tasks identified reduced ventromedial PFC value signals in adults with SA histories compared to those without (Brown et al., 2020; Dombrovski et al., 2013)

Among studies using behavioral measures of reward learning, 15 found evidence of associations between reward learning and SA. Studies using the CGT and the Game of Dice, which assesses decision making in the context of risk, consistently found that those with SA histories show poorer quality of decisions and/or increased capacity for risky decisions (Ackerman et al., 2015; Chamberlain et al., 2013; Clark et al., 2011; Deisenhammer et al., 2018). Relatedly, SA was associated with poorer task performance on the IGT in seven studies (Alacreu-Crespo et al., 2020; Bridge et al., 2012; Hegedűs et al., 2018; FJollant et al., 2005; Malloy-Diniz et al., 2009; Richard-Devantoy et al., 2016; Westheide et al., 2008). In addition, using a probabilistic reversal learning task, Dombrovski et al. (2010) found that older adults with SA showed more impaired reward learning than comparison groups. Similarly, adults with recent SA, particularly those with high-lethality SA, were less responsive to reinforcement relative to comparison groups (Dombrovski et al., 2019). Using the Trust Game, which requires participants to choose the amount of money to invest with a perceived partner, with the partner’s expression indicating the likelihood of gaining money (i.e., happy face indicates a gain trial), one study found that depressed adults with SA had fewer gains and invested less money in trials when the partner showed a happy facial expression relative to comparisons (Sánchez-Loyo et al., 2016).

Seven behavioral studies failed to find significant associations between SA and reward learning using the IGT (Alacreu-Crespo et al., 2019; Gilbert et al., 2011; Gorlyn et al., 2013; Ho et al., 2018; Martino et al., 2011; Ponsoni et al., 2018; Wyart et al., 2016). However, Wyart et al. (2016) found within group differences, such that among individuals with SA histories, those with violent attempt histories showed poorer performance on the IGT compared to non-violent attempters.

In summary, consistent with a recent meta-analysis (Perrain et al., 2021), there is evidence of impaired reward learning in those with histories of SA at the behavioral level. There is also evidence of alterations in neural processes underlying reward learning, although the specific brain regions identified vary across fMRI studies.

Reward Valuation

Thirteen studies (12 articles) examined associations between SA and reward valuation. Three of these examined neural indices of reward valuation using fMRI. Using a risk-loss aversion task which manipulates valuation as a function of probability, depressed adults with SA histories showed greater loss aversion and decreased activation in the left insula in association with higher losses, decreased subgenual ACC activation to the amount of potential gain, and reduced amygdala activity to potential loss relative to comparison groups (Baek et al., 2017). Using a delay discounting task, one study found adults with SA, particularly those with highly planned attempts, demonstrated reduced activation in the lateral PFC as the value difference favoring immediate rewards increased (Vanyukov et al., 2016). Finally, using structural MRI, one study found that adults with SA showed reduced gray matter voxel counts in the putamen, and lower putamen grey matter was associated with stronger preferences for immediate rewards at the behavioral level (Dombrovski et al., 2012).

Eight studies examined behavioral measures of reward valuation and SA. In five, significant group differences emerged using a delay discounting task (Cáceda et al., 2014; Dombrovski et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2012; Mathias et al., 2011; Tsypes et al., 2022). Cáceda et al. (2014) found that adults with a recent SA showed greater delay discounting compared to those with depression and no SI or SA. When assessed again 1-week after their reported SA, however, delay discounting scores decreased in this group suggesting there may be acute changes in reward valuation processes for those who engage in SA (Cáceda et al., 2014). One study found those with a history of low lethality SA showed a greater preference for immediate rewards compared to those with high lethality SA, and those with a more detailed SA plan were more willing to delay rewards (Dombrovski et al., 2011). In another study, those with a past year SA and elevated post-traumatic stress symptoms showed greater preferences for immediate rewards (Bryan & Bryan, 2021). In patients with substance use dependence, the SA group discounted both large and small rewards at similar rates and showed higher delay discounting compared to those without SA for larger rewards (Liu et al., 2012). In one study of adolescents, Mathias et al. (2011) found preferences for immediate compared to delayed rewards in those with past SA. In contrast, two studies did not find effects of SA on discounting rates (Bridge et al., 2015; Millner et al., 2020), and a recent study indicated that SA group differences in delay discounting may be most observable when considering consistency in valuation rather than overall preferences. Specifically, adults with high lethality attempts showed less consistent valuation of rewards than comparison groups (Tsypes et al., 2022).

Finally, one study focused on valuation as a function of effort to obtain rewards, using a task in which adolescents selected between easy responses for smaller rewards or more difficult responses for larger rewards (Auerbach et al., 2015). Adolescents with a history of SA were less likely to select higher effort tasks for uncertain rewards and were less sensitive to the effects of prior reward on selection of higher effort options.

Taken together, there is relatively consistent evidence of alterations in reward valuation, specifically greater preference for immediate compared to delayed rewards or inconsistency in valuation, at the neural and behavioral level in individuals with a history of SA.

Summary: SA and PVS Function

Compared to the SI literature, many studies have examined reward learning and valuation in SA, but few on reward responsiveness. The existing evidence supports associations between alterations in reward learning, increased risky decision-making, and greater preferences for immediate relative to delayed rewards and SA.

NSSI and PVS Function

Reward Responsiveness

Studies examining NSSI are presented in Table 4. Four studies examined reward responsiveness and NSSI. In one fMRI study, adults with NSSI demonstrated increased activation in the amygdala, OFC, and anterior PFC when viewing pleasant images compared to those without NSSI (Hooley et al., 2018). In a second study, participants with both borderline personality disorder and NSSI demonstrated greater OFC activation during reward trials and diminished functional connectivity in reward circuits compared with those with no psychiatric histories (Vega et al., 2018). One study used EEG/ERP to examine reward responsiveness in children with and without NSSI histories, finding that children with NSSI demonstrated enhanced neural responses to reward relative to loss feedback compared to those without NSSI (Tsypes et al., 2018). Finally, one study examined physiological responses to pleasant images in adults. After a stress induction, those with NSSI histories displayed blunted skin conductance responses to pleasant images relative to comparisons (Tatnell et al., 2018). In summary, these studies suggest the possibility of enhanced neural reward responsiveness in youth and adults with NSSI; however, each study used different stimuli and tasks.

Reward Learning

Five studies (three articles) examined reward learning and NSSI. In two samples of adults, no group differences were observed in performance on the Criticism Gambling Task, a modified version of the IGT with interspersed criticism, but higher NSSI frequency was associated with risky decision making (Allen et al., 2019). In three studies, no differences on IGT performance were identified in those with and without NSSI histories (Janis & Nock, 2009; Schatten et al., 2015). Overall, findings from these studies did not find consistent associations between NSSI and reward learning when assessed by decision-making tasks.

Reward Valuation

Only one study examined the association between reward valuation and NSSI, and no significant differences were identified between adult females with and without NSSI (Janis & Nock, 2009). Thus, no conclusions can be drawn regarding reward valuation alterations in NSSI.

Summary: NSSI and PVS Function

Research examining the link between NSSI and PVS is limited, but there is preliminary evidence that NSSI history is associated with altered reward responsiveness, particularly enhanced activation of reward circuits. On the contrary, there is little evidence for reward learning or reward valuation alterations in NSSI.

Discussion

In this RDoC-informed systematic review, we synthesized current evidence for alterations in PVS function in SITBs across neural, physiological, and behavioral measures. Despite discrepancies across studies and variability in tasks and measures, we found preliminary evidence for distinct alterations in PVS function across SITBs and promising avenues for future research. Specifically, SI appears to be characterized by reduced neural reward responsiveness, similar to patterns observed in unipolar depression (Keren et al., 2018), while NSSI may be associated with enhanced reward responsiveness. In contrast, impairments in reward learning have been observed across varying forms of SITBs, but the largest literature has focused on SA and suggests these alterations may be most apparent in those with a history of SA rather than those with only SI or NSSI. Alterations in reward learning appear to be driven in part by deficits in ventromedial PFC processing of potential rewards/values, and this relationship may depend on the level of planning and lethality of attempts (Dombrovski & Hallquist, 2017). Finally, within the reward valuation domain, there is consistent evidence of steeper discounting of the value of rewards as a function of temporal delay in SA, while support for these patterns in SI or NSSI was not found. Our review generally supports existing decision-making theories of SA (Dombrovski et al., 2019; Dombrovski & Hallquist, 2017), and highlights notable gaps in the literature on reward learning and valuation in SI and NSSI.

Together, findings from this review could indicate that SI is characterized by tendencies to allocate fewer attentional resources to positive aspects of the environment and experience the world as less rewarding. In contrast, NSSI may be associated with greater desire for rewards, and SA with impairments in reward-related decision-making and preferences for immediate rewards. This combination of desire for immediate reinforcement and difficulties with learning how to obtain rewards may underlie risk for SA, particularly in the context of other proximal risk factors, such as acute stress (Bagge et al., 2013, 2017; Liu & Miller, 2014). Although NSSI and SA reflect distinct types of behaviors, NSSI has been shown to be a more robust predictor of SA than SI (Franklin et al., 2017), suggesting that there may be some commonalities in risk processes.

Future Directions in Research Examining SITBS and PVS

Despite some emerging patterns in the literature, our review also highlights many questions that still require empirical investigation. Below, we discuss specific recommendations extending the existing literature to address key questions regarding the role of PVS in SITBs.

Employing Multimethod RDoC-Informed Approaches.

Most studies in this review relied on behavioral measures, with very little research examining the utility of neuroscience methods for advancing understanding of SITB. It remains an outstanding question the extent to which alterations in PVS-related brain function underlie SITBs and whether neuroscience methods can be applied to inform prediction of future SITBs. Further, most studies focused on a single method, and there is a need for examination of PVS function in SITBs integrating multiple levels of analysis. There is preliminary evidence that neural measures can improve classification of SITBs beyond self-report measures of related processes (Pegg et al., 2020), similar to evidence that neural reward responsiveness prospectively predicts depressive symptoms beyond self-report measures (Kujawa et al., 2019). In addition to integration of multiple measures, inclusion of multiple RDoC-informed constructs in SITB research is needed, as it is likely that many systems work synergistically to shape risk and resilience for SITBs in combination with contextual factors. Multimethod longitudinal research on PVS function in large, well-characterized samples with variability in distinct forms of SITBs is critically needed to test the utility of neural measures in predicting risk.

Transdiagnostic Considerations.

Importantly, although SITBs present transdiagnostically, much of the research on PVS function in SITBs has focused on individuals with histories of or current depression. There is some evidence that associations between SI and reward responsiveness persist when accounting for co-occurring depression, but other symptoms and diagnoses that could confound or moderate associations between SITBs and PVS are rarely considered. For example, research is needed to disentangle the role of anhedonia vs. depressed mood in the relationship between PVS alterations and SITBs. Further, exploration of the role of other co-occurring symptoms of psychopathology (e.g., mania, substance use), associated with distinct patterns of PVS function is needed to isolate associations with SITBs specifically. Notably, given much of the extant literature has relied on small clinical samples or larger community samples, it is likely that studies have been underpowered or included too limited variability in psychopathology severity to consider differences across presentations and diagnoses.

Understanding Changes Across Development, Individuals, and Context.

PVS function is known to change considerably across development (Kujawa et al., 2020), with these changes corresponding with elevated risk for SITBs beginning in adolescence (Cha et al., 2018; Swannell et al., 2014). This raises questions about how PVS-SITB associations emerge and change across development, as well as the nature of the relationship. One recent study provides intriguing evidence that altered PVS-related brain function can prospectively predict the later emergence of SI, suggesting a potential early-emerging vulnerability that could be used to identify those at greatest risk and targeted with intervention efforts (Hutchinson et al., 2021). Yet, longitudinal research across developmental stages is very limited, with most studies employing cross-sectional designs in adult samples. Further, no studies examined PVS measures as prospective predictors of future SA. Longitudinal research is needed both to elucidate the extent to which alterations in PVS precede the onset of SITBs or reflect a correlate or outcome of SITBs and to consider trajectories of PVS function associated with greatest risk.

In addition to changes over development, both SI and NSSI fluctuate within individuals over time, even within the course of a single day (Kleiman et al., 2017), and the dynamic assessment of SITBs may be informative when unpacking the relations between PVS function and SITBs. A growing body of research shows that ambulatory assessment of SITBs is both feasible and acceptable among participants, even those at very high risk (Czyz et al., 2018; Kleiman & Nock, 2018). To our knowledge, no studies have explored how PVS function may contribute to or inform periods of imminent suicide risk. Identifying what (if any) PVS factors may be most meaningful to monitor in real-time has great potential to advance suicide prevention efforts. Relatedly, there is growing interest in idiographic modeling of suicide risk and prediction, given increasing evidence for high levels of between and within person variability in suicide risk (Kaurin et al., 2021; Kleiman et al., 2019), which could lead to a more personalized approach to predicting SITB risk from multiple PVS and other RDoC domains.

It is also critical to consider how individual characteristics and developmental course interact with larger social systems and harmful experiences such as exposure to poverty, discrimination, and marginalization. For example, the adverse consequences of exposure to racism and race-related stress on mental health, including SITBs, are well-established (Arshanapally et al., 2018; Madubata et al., 2021; Walker et al., 2017). These factors may play a role in shaping PVS function, with the potential to elucidating mechanisms linking stress to SITBs. Further, as we examine individual-level processes, such as PVS function, and its relationship to SITBs, we must also place these findings in this broader sociocultural context that impacts risk.

Considerations for Studying Suicide Attempts.

Sample size and power are critical considerations in SITB research, particularly when seeking to examine predictors of SA. To address low base rates among the general population, studies of people at high risk for suicidal behavior, such as patients in psychiatric hospitals, are needed to ensure predictive power. When considering prevention efforts, however, it is also important to understand SITB risk among those who do not seek or require psychiatric services. Indeed, many people who may be at risk do not access mental health support prior to engaging in suicidal behavior or dying by suicide. Thus, recruiting large, nonclinical samples is also important to move research forward. Collaborative, large scale research efforts that can track participants over long periods of time may be critical to tackling these essential and complex research questions (e.g., Wiglesworth et al., 2021).

Expanding Scientific Approaches to Improve Clinical Utility.

The complexity and importance of preventing SITBs calls for a team-based science approach, including the expertise of a range of scientists and community members, is likely to have the biggest impact, both scientifically and clinically. Participatory team science encourages the involvement of the public in each phase of the scientific process, from the development to the translation and dissemination of findings (Tebes & Thai, 2018). This approach may be particularly impactful when considering the clinical translational value of research on PVS alterations and SITBs. Despite the promising role of altered PVS function in SITBs, the clinical utility of PVS in the assessment and treatment of SITBs remains to be evaluated. This is important when considering what methods to use in a study and the feasibility of implementing those in clinical settings, both in terms of the accessibility and cost of the tools used to collect data and the training and time required to implement such measures. We focused this review on behavioral, physiological, and neural measures of PVS function, excluding self-report measures which are most used in clinical settings. If researchers are to continue to invest time and resources into these methodologies, it is important that we demonstrate their ability to complement and add to self-report measures of relevant clinical constructs. Harnessing partnerships with clinical settings may be a particularly fruitful approach to improve clinical translation of these research findings.

Conclusions

In this systematic review, we found preliminary support for associations between altered PVS function and SITBs. Further, findings suggest both distinct and common alterations in PVS function and SI, SA, and NSSI. Notably, some constructs are understudied in relation to specific forms of SITBs, and very little longitudinal or developmental research is available to determine the extent to which PVS function may play a role in vulnerability for SITBs. In future research, particular attention should be paid to study design, developmental trajectories, and clinical translational value.

Highlights

  • Positive Valence Systems (PVS) may be altered in self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITB).

  • Preliminary support suggests blunted reward responsiveness and learning in suicidal ideation.

  • Studies find alterations in reward learning and valuation are associated with suicide attempts.

  • Findings suggest elevated reward response and poor reward learning in nonsuicidal self-injury.

  • Large, prospective studies are needed to clarify PVS and SITB relationships.
