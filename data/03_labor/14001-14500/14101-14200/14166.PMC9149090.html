How does PTSD treatment affect cardiovascular, diabetes and metabolic disease risk factors and outcomes? A systematic review

Abstract

Objective.

Prior research indicates PTSD is associated with cardiovascular and metabolic disease. A number of different treatments for PTSD can be effective in reducing PTSD symptoms. The aim of this study is to systematically review studies which determine whether treatment for PTSD is associated with better cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes.

Method.

Five different databases were searched in a systematic manner, and 11 relevant studies were recovered and analyzed.

Findings.

Treatments associated with PTSD improvement and found to be effective in improving cardiovascular or metabolic outcomes among individuals with PTSD include cognitive behavioral therapy (heart rate variability and blood pressure), prolonged exposure (heart rate and heart rate variability) and SSRIs (blood pressure).

Conclusions.

Multiple PTSD treatment modalities were associated with improved cardiovascular health and reduced risk of cardiovascular-related mortality. Given the small sample sizes, lack of follow-up studies and the extensive use of military populations in studies on PTSD and chronic diseases, these results should be interpreted with caution. More studies are needed that assess and verify whether PTSD treatments mitigate the risk for metabolic, diabetic and cardiovascular disease.

INTRODUCTION

Persons with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) have elevated rates of metabolic disease (Schnurr and Green 2004; Schnurr 2015; Talbot et al. 2015), metabolic syndrome (Solomon et al. 2017, Heppner et al. 2009) and cardiovascular disease (Edmondson & Cohen, 2013). There is also evidence that PTSD increases the likelihood of type 2 diabetes (T2D), as well as leads to weight gain, hypertension and hyperlipidemia. PTSD affects these metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes through a variety of different pathways which include stress regulation and related physiological abnormalities (Schnurr 2015; Scherrer et al., 2018), as well as poor health behaviors such as higher substance use (smoking, alcohol use), more sedentary lifestyle, and poor nutrition (van den Berk, et al., 2018).

In comparison to the large literature on depression treatment and physical health outcomes, there is relatively little known about the potential benefit of treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) on cardiometabolic disease risk. Decades of research on depression and physical health outcomes have established PTSD as a risk factor for poor ischemic heart disease outcomes (Schnurr, 2015). Meanwhile, there has been a number of review studies showing that adequate depression treatment is associated with reduced risk for T2D and CVD (Ramamurthy et al., 2013; Bradley et al., 2015; Zambrano et al., 2020)). However, to our knowledge, there has not been a systematic review assessing the relationship between PTSD treatment and change in co-morbid metabolic, diabetic and cardiovascular parameters. The purpose of this systematic review is to evaluate the evidence for an association between PTSD treatment and incident cardiometabolic diseases.

We hypothesized that receipt of treatment for PTSD would be associated with decreased risk for cardiometabolic disease and improvement in components of prevalent cardiometabolic disease. We, therefore, conducted a systematic review to explore whether treatment of PTSD was associated with a change in various measures of cardiovascular disease and diabetes, including metabolic measures, heart rate, blood pressure, cholesterol, cardiovascular mortality, respiratory rate, etc.

METHODS

We conducted a systematic literature review to identify studies which examined the effects of PTSD treatments on metabolic, cardiovascular disease and diabetes outcomes. Cardiovascular disease and diabetes health outcomes included common measures of metabolic syndrome, such as glycemic control, blood pressure, triglycerides etc., as well as incidence of and mortality due to diabetes and cardiovascular disease.

First, we identified literature from five different databases including EBSCO, PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and PsycInfo, with coverage up to January 2020. The following search terms were used: “PTSD” or “posttraumatic stress disorder,” and “treatment,” “therapy,” “intervention,” “cognitive behavioral therapy,” “prolonged exposure therapy,” “eye movement desensitization and reprocessing,” “SSRI,” and “metabolic,” “cardiovascular,” “heart attack,” “heart disease,” “stroke,” “diabetes,” “blood pressure,” “hypertension,” “high blood sugar,” “excess body fat,” “cholesterol,” “triglyceride.”

After removing duplicates, seven of the nine authors examined titles and abstracts of all studies to confirm eligibility criteria (Kansara, Fedorova, Renirie, Ju, Lee, Kao, Opada). Articles were excluded if they did not involve individuals with PTSD, administration of some type of PTSD treatment, measures of metabolic health outcomes, and adult human participants. Additional exclusion criteria included review studies, case reports, and studies that could not be found. Articles that were eligible for inclusion underwent a full-text screening by four of the authors (Kansara, Fedorova, Ju and Renirie). During the full text screening, five authors independently evaluated the complete manuscripts for inclusion and multiple reviews of included studies were also conducted to ensure reliability (Kansara, Fedorova, Ju, Renirie and van den Berk). For more detail on the review protocol, see Prospero # 166937. To determine the change in PTSD and effect of treatment on PTSD, we also calculated effect sizes using the Campbell Collaboration online effect size calculator (See Wilson, 2021).

To assess the quality of included studies, we used the Quality of Cohort studies (Q-Coh), which is a tool used to assess methodological quality and identify studies that would be a source of bias in the meta-analysis (Jarde, Losilla, Vives, & Rodrigo, 2013). The Q-Coh specifically focuses on cohort studies and it is divided into the following domains—representativeness, comparability of groups, quality of exposure measures, maintenance of comparability, quality of outcome measures, and attrition. This tool has been shown to have good inter-rater reliability and validity (Jarde et al., 2013). Q-Coh scores range from 0 to 26 and the quality of papers with low scores indicated a logical threshold of identification at Q-Coh score < 16.

RESULTS

We identified 1,241 citations from all sources. After removing duplicates and articles that were irrelevant (n=1,182), we reviewed the full text of 59 articles. Of these, 47 were excluded at the full text screening stage, leaving 12 articles for inclusion in the review (Figure 1). After reviewing whether PTSD treatment affected chronic disease outcomes (i.e., metabolic, cardiovascular, diabetic), we removed the article from Scherrer, Salas, Friedman, et al., (2020a) because it did not directly measure the relationship between PTSD treatment and chronic disease outcomes, which resulted in the final total of 11 articles.

As shown in Table 1, five studies were randomized controlled trials, one study was longitudinal, one study was observational, and three studies used retrospective data from medical records. A total of 24,244 participants were included in these studies. Types of trauma included combat, automobile accident, natural disaster and myocardial infarction. Mean age of participants in all studies was 51.4 (range 30.5 to 58.0) and about 85% of pooled participants were male. Of the 8 studies with racial information, 64.0% of the pooled sample was White. All studies consisted of PTSD patients and 23% of the total sample had a PTSD diagnosis.

We identified 8 studies examining more common used treatments for PTSD, including trauma focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)(Blanchard et al., 2002; Hinton, Hofmann, Pollack, & Otto, 2009; Scherrer et al., 2019b; Scherrer, Salas, Schneider, et al., 2020; Schubert et al., 2019; Shemesh et al., 2006), prolonged exposure (PE) therapy (PE; Bourassa et al., 2020; Scherrer et al., 2019b; Scherrer, Salas, Schneider, et al., 2020b; Shemesh et al., 2006) and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)(Tucker et al., 2003). Several studies defined treatment as minimally adequate duration of psychotherapy received in a Veterans Health Affairs PTSD specialty clinic (Scherrer et al., 2019b; Scherrer, Salas, Schneider, et al., 2020b). The Veterans Administration has heavily invested in expanding CBT and PE interventions because they have the strongest evidence base with veteran populations (Karlin et al., 2010), and previous research has shown that between 80–95% of veterans who utilize VA PTSD specialty clinics receive CBT/PE treatments (van den Berk Clark et al., 2019).

We also identified 3 studies that examined less commonly utilized treatments for PTSD but did show some potential, including devise-guided deep breathing treatment (DBT; Fonkoue et al., 2018), electroconvulsive therapy (ECT; Ahmadi, Moss, Simon, Nemeroff, & Atre-Vaidya, 2016) and trauma focused smoking cessation treatment (Waszczuk et al., 2017). DBT is 15 minutes in duration with breathing at 5 breaths per minute. This was compared to an identical sham device that allowed normal breathing of 14 breaths per minute (Fonkoue et al., 2018). ECT is a procedure whereby brief electric stimulation of the brain is administered under general anesthesia in order to trigger a brief seizure and reduce symptoms of severe psychiatric disorders (Ahmadi et al., 2016). Trauma-focused smoking cessation treatment included 8-week sessions comprised of psychoeducation, enlisting social support, cognitive restructuring, and nicotine replacement therapy. Cognitive interventions aimed at reducing somatic symptoms of PTSD, including in vivo exposure, were also integrated into a more intensive, trauma-focused treatment intervention (Waszczuk et al., 2017).

All studies but one (Fonkoue et al., 2018) focused on devise guided breathing reported results on PTSD outcomes. All studies reporting PTSD outcomes, as shown in Table 1, reported improvement in PTSD following treatment. Two studies were designed to control for receipt of minimally adequate CBT/PE in order to measure the total effect of experiencing vs. not experiencing a ≥20 point PTSD Symptom Checklist (PCL) decrease and risk of incident diabetes (Scherrer et al., 2019b), and incident cardiovascular disease (Scherrer, Salas, Schneider, et al., 2020b). Three studies reported PTSD change not just over time but also by group. Experiemental groups in these studies had higher PTSD improvement than control groups, as follows: d=0.80, 95%CI (0.59, 1.01)(Ahmadi et al., 2016), d=0.96, 95%CI(0.45, 1.47) (Blanchard et al., 2003), d=0.83, 95%CI (0.34, 1.32), and d=2.12, 95%CI(1.13, 3.13) (Hinton et al., 2009).

Studies had a mean Q-Coh score of 18.2±3.5 with a range between 11 and 22. All of the studies had explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria and applied it equally to the whole sample. In addition, all studies explicitly defined their exposure and outcome variables and used validated instruments to measure these variables. About half the studies accounted for known confounding variables, were studied in different contexts, and had different follow-up times. Also, it was noted that dropout and response rates were more often not reported (Table 3).

Heart Rate

A total of five studies (Bourassa et al., 2020 (PE, RCT, N=104); Fonkoue et al., 2018 (Quasi-Experimental, N=23); Hinton et al., 2009 (CBT, RCT; N=24); Schubert et al., 2019 (CBT, Prospective Cohort, N=45); Tucker et al., 2003 (SSRI, RCT, N=58)) assessed the association between PTSD treatment and heart rate (See Table 1 for more detail). As shown in Table 2, four (Bourassa et al., 2020; Fonkoue et al., 2018; Schubert et al., 2019; Tucker et al., 2003) out five heart rate studies showed that PTSD treatments reduced heart rate among individuals with subclinical PTSD or clinical PTSD. Only one of these studies (Bourassa et al., 2020), which measured the effect of PE on heart rate, was significant from baseline to posttreatment assessment (B=−5.06, 95%CI=−9.44, −0.76). Studies showed that CBT treatments, an RCT (N=24) among a Cambodian refugee cohort (Hinton et al., 2009) and a prospective cohort study (N=45) at a trauma outpatient clinic (Schubert et al., 2019), did manage to decrease heart rate, but not significantly. The RCT study utilizing the Cambodian refugee cohort (Hinton et al., 2009) was unique because it measured heart rate among two comparison groups, one which received treatment immediately, which resulted in a nonsignificant increased heart rate, and one which received delayed treatment, which resulted in a nonsignificant decreased heart rate. Two other PTSD interventions, SSRI (RCT, N=58, Tucker et al., 2003) and device-guided deep breathing (Quasi-experimental, N=23, Fonkoue et al., 2018) also reduced heart rate, but the results were nonsignificant.

Heart Rate Variability

Two RCT studies; examined the association between PTSD treatment and heart rate variability among individuals who had automobile accidents (Blanchard et al., 2002 (CBT, N=74)) and combat exposure (Bourassa et al., 2020 (PE, N=104)). Heart rate variability is the variation of heart beats over time and is believed to reflect the heart’s capacity to respond to different types of stimuli (Acharya, et al., 2006). Blanchard et al. (2002) measured heart rate variability in beats per minute (BPM) in response to audiotaped trauma scripts among PTSD patients that received CBT, psychotherapy, or control condition. They found that after successful CBT treatment (i.e., patients no longer met criteria for PTSD), PTSD patients had much lower heart rate variability (Mean=11.8±9.6 BPM pre-treatment vs. 3.4±3.7 BPM post-treatment, t(32)=4.22, p<.001) in response to trauma cues. According to Bourassa et al. (2020), PE also is effective in reducing heart rate variability among active-duty soldiers with PTSD (B=−2.46, 95%CI = −4.19, −0.74), but it should be noted that changes in heart rate variability were not associated with changes in PTSD symptoms.

Blood Pressure

Six studies (Ahmadi et al., 2016 (ECT Retrospective cohort, N=22,164); Fonkoue et al., 2018 (Devise Guided Breathing Quasi-Experiemental, N=24); Hinton et al., 2009 (CBT RCT, N=24); Scherrer, Salas, Schneider, et al., 2020b (CBT/PE/SSRI Retrospective Cohort, N=1079); Schubert et al., 2019 (CBT/PE/SSRI Prospective Cohort, N=45); Shemesh et al., 2006 (CBT Quasi-Experiemental, N=65); Tucker et al., 2003 (SSRI RCT, N=58)) investigated the association between PTSD treatment and blood pressure (Table 2). Four (Fonkoue, et.al., 2018; Hinton et al., 2009; Schubert et al., 2019; Tucker et al., 2003) of the six studies showed that PTSD treatment was associated with a significant decrease in either systolic, diastolic and/or arterial blood pressure (See Table 2). One retrospective cohort study (Scherrer et al., 2020b) indicated large, clinically meaningful reductions in PTSD symptom severity was associated with lower risk of incident hypertension.

The effect of CBT on blood pressure among individuals with PTSD was evaluated in three different studies (Hinton et al., 2009; Schubert et al., 2019; Shemesh et al., 2006). Schubert et al., (2018), a prospective cohort study of 45 German patients at a PTSD outpatient clinic, found between group differences at 2030h (p<.02), and 1630h (p<.02) for systolic blood pressure:, and 1630h (p<.03) for diastolic blood pressure:. Hinton et al. (2009), an RCT study of 24 Cambodian refugees at an outpatient clinic, found that CBT reduced systolic (from − 17.01±8.41 immediate/−16.33±7.30 delayed at baseline to −7.16±5.13 immediate/−7.50±5.47 delayed, Cohen d=1.31) and diastolic blood pressure (from −6.0±6.98 immediate/−4.33±3.39 delayed at baseline to −4.00±4.35 immediate/−3.25±3.27 delayed, Cohen d=0.30). When all three physiological measure change scores were grouped together, there was a significant group by time interaction effect (F(6,17)=4.95, p<.01).

Shemesh et al., (2006), a quasi-experiemtal study evaluated whether patients 6–9 months after a myocardial infarction, who received CBT, plus self-monitoring education related to heart disease, would be less likely to have high blood pressure (measured as arterial pressure) (N=65). They found that patients who received CBT, plus the self-monitoring education, no longer met the threshold for high blood pressure. It should be noted that only 6 patients were in the CBT and education category, 8 patients were in the education-only category, and 51 patients received no treatment. The results showed the effect as significant but given the size and distribution of cases vs. controls in the sample, we regarded this as more of a trend effect.

SSRI’s(Tucker et al., 2003) and device-guided slow breathing (DGB; Fonkoue et al., 2018) were found to significantly reduce blood pressure among patient samples with PTSD. According to Tucker et al., (2003), an RCT study among 58 patients in a university hospital outpatient setting, Citalopram was associated with a significant decrease in systolic (from 6.66±10.06 to 0.70±7.89, t(48)=−2.33, p<.03) and diastolic blood pressure (from 2.28±4.91 to −1.65±4.23, t(48)=−3.93, p<.004). Fonkoue et al. (2018)’s study of 23 9/11 pre-hypertension veterans in an quasi-experimental study of DGB also showed significantly reduced systolic (−9 ± 2 mmHg, p < 0.001) and diastolic blood pressure (−3 ± 1 mmHg, p < 0.019).

Cholesterol and Hyperlipidemia

According to Shemesh et. al., 2006 (Quasi-Experiemental, N=65), patients 6–9 months after a myocardial infarction and received CBT and self-monitoring education (n=6), experienced reductions in cholesterol levels (177 mg/dL pre-treatment to 160 mg/dL post-treatment), in addition to increased adherence to Aspirin treatment. Patients in the education only (n=8) and control groups (n=51) did not show any improvements in cholesterol levels. However, as stated previously, given that the groups were small and unevenly distributed, significance level could not be confirmed. In addition, a large retrospective cohort study found no association between clinically meaningful PTSD symptom improvement and incident hyperlipidemia (Scherrer et al., 2020a).

Type 2 Diabetes

A retrospective cohort study using VHA medical record data (N=1598) found a significant lower risk for incident type 2 diabetes (HR=0.51; 95% CI, 0.26–0.98) among patients with PTSD who received CBT/PE who did vs. did not experience a ≥20-point PCL decrease (Scherrer et al., 2019b). This association remained after controlling for baseline PTSD severity, receipt of minimally adequate duration of PE/CBT and numerous other confounding factors. In patients with PTSD and comorbid depression, lower risk of T2D was preceded by decreases in both PCL scores and depression symptoms, the latter measured with PHQ-9 scores. However, this same study also found a positive bivariate relationship between adequate PTSD treatment duration at the VA (via PTSD specialty clinics) and incident type 2 diabetes (HR=1.73, 95%CI 1.17, 2.55, p<.01, unadjusted).

Cardiovascular Disease and Cardiovascular Mortality

Adequate PTSD treatment (i.e, at least 9 sessions of CBT/PE) via VA PTSD specialty treatment was associated with increased incident cardiovascular disease (HR=1.56, 95%CI=1.12, 2.18, X2(1)=6.8, p<.009, unadjusted) in a crude hazard ratio analysis via a retrospective VA cohort (N=1598) (Scherrer, Salas, Schneider, et al., 2020b). After controlling for confounding using propensity scores and inverse probability of exposure weighting, there was no association between incident cardiovascular disease and clinically meaningful PTSD reduction. Ahmadi et al., (2016), also a VA retrospective cohort, showed that ECT resulted in a non-significant difference (RR=1.06, 95%CI=0.39–2.21, p<.10, N=22,164) in cardiovascular mortality between patients with PTSD who received ECT treatment and matched controls without PTSD or major depression. Meanwhile, among patients who received antidepressants, relative risk of cardiovascular mortality was significantly higher (RR=3.04, 95%CI=2.67, 3.45, p<.0001), compared to ECT. ECT, in comparison to antidepressants, significantly reduced cardiovascular mortality (RR=0.35, 95%CI=0.13, 0.72, p<.001). Baseline measures showed no differences in hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, body mass index and coronary heart disease.

Respiratory Symptoms

Comprehensive smoking cessation therapy reduced lower respiratory symptoms (such as shortness of breath, chest tightness, wheezing, and cough) and PTSD symptoms among PTSD patients that were exposed to the World Trade Center disaster and smoked at least five cigarettes per day (Waszczuk et al., 2017 (RCT, N=90)). Lower respiratory symptoms decreased from Mean= 9.7±5.16 at baseline to Mean= 5.26±5.06 during follow-up for both trauma-focused and non-trauma focused smoking cessation therapy (vs. control group) (t(178)=−5.828, p<.001). However, there were no group differences between trauma and non-trauma focused smoking cessation therapy. According to Waszczuk et. al., (2017) both treatments focused on PTSD management via stress management training for PTSD. Both treatments also provided psychoeducation, training on enlisting social support, cognitive restructuring, and nicotine replacement therapy. The trauma-focused, experimental condition however, also included additional behavioral and cognitive interventions targeted at somatic symptoms of PTSD and nicotine withdrawal sensations. These approaches used exposure exercises and corrective information exercises.

Moreover, treatment attendance was negatively associated with PTSD symptoms (standardized B=−0.57, 95%CI −0.08, −0.03), and more PTSD symptoms was positively associated with respiratory symptoms (standardized B=0.31, 95%CI 0.19, 0.43). Of interest, a 1-standard deviation change in PTSD symptoms a week before would lead to a 10% lower respiratory symptom standard deviation change the next week.

DISCUSSION

Results of this systematic review of PTSD treatment and risk for indicators of cardiometabolic disease suggests that a range of different PTSD treatments and related reductions in PTSD severity are associated with improved cardiometabolic disease severity or lower cardiometabolic disease risk. However, some of the interventions, like ECT and devise guided breathing, were not traditionally used to treat PTSD and there were no follow-up studies which confirmed results of these studies. We found that more commonly used PTSD treatments (CBT, PE and SSRIs) and large decreases in PTSD severity, (that were independent of PTSD treatment in two retrospective studies - Scherrer et al., 2019b; Scherrer, Salas, Schneider, et al., 2020b) are associated with significant reductions in heart rate, heart rate variability, blood pressure andlower risk for hypertension. Meanwhile, there was a positive relationship between adequate PTSD treatment (CBT or PE) and incidence of type 2 diabetes. There is little evidence for improved cholesterol and decreased risk for hyperlipidemia, as well as no association between PTSD improvement and risk for cardiovascular disease.

One common trend among studies showing a negative relationship between PTSD treatment and cardiometabolic outcomes, is the use of military populations. Those studies with military veterans have more males, have relatively younger samples and tend to take place in medical settings where there is integrated behavioral health care. A study of likelihood to get PTSD treatment showed that those who have more health problems overall, tend to be more likely to be treated (van den Berk Clark et. al., 2019). These patients are more likely to spend more time in health systems which increases their access to care and the convenience of getting mental health treatments, which is abetted by physical health comorbidities and integrated health care (i.e., VHA system).

Scherrer and colleagues (2019a) have also suggested that different mechanisms explain why PTSD improvement is associated with lower risk for type 2 diabetes and hypertension, but not for cardiovascular disease. These authors think that even though blood pressure and glucose are much more sensitive to reductions in chronic stress, the measure of both may not explain how cardiovascular disease develops over time. For example, atherosclerosis leading to cardiovascular disease diagnosis begins in young adults (McNamara, Molot, Stremple, & Cutting, 1971) and atherosclerosis may be present prior to patients seeking PTSD treatment. Thus, even major PTSD improvements may be insufficient to reverse this course of disease.

The results showing the use of combined trauma focused therapy and devise guided breathing interventions may be a promising approach. The study by Fonkoue et al., (2018) did not show whether there was an effect on PTSD but if combined with existing evidence-based PTSD treatments, there could be some potential for improving blood pressure and heart rate variability. It is important to point out that the Fonkoue study appears only to be a pilot study and more study is needed. PTSD treatment is associated with greater reductions in PTSD symptoms and associated chronic stress. This should have an immediate impact on diabetes and cardiovascular disease risk, given that a number of studies of patients with and without PTSD show that stress reduction techniques used in CBT and breathing therapies have the capacity to reduce blood pressure (Spruill, 2010) and improve glycemic control in general population studies (Lloyd, Smith & Weinger, 2005).

Although this is the first study to systematically review research on the relationship between PTSD treatment and cardiometabolic outcomes, some limitations should be noted. Eligible studies used different metrics and often had very small sample sizes, and different sized experimental and control groups. They also were primarily conducted with veteran samples which focus more exclusively on male populations with combat exposure. Further studies using longitudinal data collection are needed to elucidate whether some health conditions but not others can be mitigated by PTSD treatment.

CONCLUSIONS

PTSD is a well-established risk factor for numerous health problems. Our results indicate several types of PTSD treatments and symptom improvement can reduce some risk factors associated with cardiometabolic disease, such as blood pressure. Educating patients about the secondary health benefits of PTSD treatment may encourage more treatment seeking. Additional research is needed to identify the mechanisms underlying the link between PTSD treatment, reduced PTSD severity and cardiometabolic health. Prospective studies are needed to determine if alleviating specific PTSD symptoms or symptom clusters via PTSD treatments have a greater impact on health outcomes.
