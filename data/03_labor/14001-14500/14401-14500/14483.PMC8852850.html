Safety and Sedation-Associated Adverse Event Reporting Among Patients Undergoing Endoscopic Cholangiopancreatography: A Comparative Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Abstract

Background and Aim:

There is wide variation in choice of sedation and airway management for endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). The aim of this study was to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate safety outcomes of deep sedation with monitored anesthesia care (MAC) versus general endotracheal anesthesia (GETA).

Methods:

Individualized search strategies were performed in accordance with PRISMA and MOOSE guidelines. This meta-analysis was performed by calculating pooled proportions using random effects models. Measured outcomes included procedure success, all-cause and anesthesia-associated adverse events, and post-procedure recovery time. Heterogeneity was assessed with I2 statistics and publication bias by funnel plot and Egger regression testing.

Results:

Five studies (MAC:n=1284 vs GETA:n=615) were included. Patients in the GETA group were younger, had higher body mass index (BMI), and higher mean ASA scores (all P<0.001) with no difference in Mallampati scores (P=0.923). Procedure success, all-cause adverse events, and anesthesia-associated events were similar between groups [OR 1.16 (95% CI, 0.51–2.64); OR 1.16 (95% CI, 0.29–4.70); OR 1.33 (95% CI, 0.27–6.49), respectively]. MAC resulted in fewer hypotensive episodes [OR 0.32 (95% CI, 0.12–0.87], increased hypoxemic events [OR 5.61 (95% CI, 1.54–20.37)], and no difference in cardiac arrhythmias [OR 0.48 (95% CI, 0.13–1.78)]. Procedure time was decreased for MAC [standard difference −0.39 (95% CI, −0.78–0.00)] with no difference in recovery time [standard difference −0.48 (95% CI, −1.04–0.07)].

Conclusions:

This study suggests MAC may be a safe alternative to GETA for ERCP; however, MAC may not be appropriate in all patients given an increased risk of hypoxemia.

INTRODUCTION

While more than 500,000 endoscopic cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) procedures are performed annually in the United States, there remains wide variation in the choice of sedation and airway management during the procedure.1 Similar to other endoscopic interventions, the type of sedation utilized for the case can have an impact on both the procedural conditions and patient safety. Unlike standard endoscopy or colonoscopy, the complexity and longer duration of the ERCP procedure results in a more challenging environment to safely administer sedation, often necessitating anesthesia support.

Anesthesia provider-administered sedation may include deep sedation without endotracheal intubation (monitored anesthesia care [MAC]) or general endotracheal anesthesia (GETA).2,3 Deep sedation, typically with the administration of propofol in the United States, is defined as a stage where a patient cannot be easily aroused but responds purposefully following repeated painful stimuli.4 The purpose of sedation, whether it be MAC or GETA, is 3-fold: to improve the efficiency of the procedure, increase the quality of the results, and maximize comfort of the patient.5–7 However, sedation must also be balanced against the associated risk of adverse events which are responsible for the majority of complications related to diagnostic endoscopy.8 In fact, cardiopulmonary associated adverse events have been demonstrated to occur in approximately 1% of ERCP procedures with an associated mortality rate of 0.07%.9

Although previous literature has clearly demonstrated anesthesia provider–administered sedation is safer and associated with lower rates of ERCP failure when compared to endoscopist-administered sedation, there is limited data at present to guide clinicians regarding the choice between deep sedation with MAC versus GETA.10,11 Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to perform a comparative systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate safety outcomes of deep sedation with MAC versus GETA.

METHODS

Literature Search

Individualized literature search strategies were performed to identify all-cause and anesthesia-associated adverse events with ERCP. Systematic searches of PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library databases were performed from available literature from inception through November 30, 2019 using the following medical subject heading (MESH) term: endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) AND anesthesia AND adverse event. Related articles were subsequently reviewed by subject heading search terms, title and abstract for: sedation, deep sedation, monitored anesthesia care (MAC), general endotracheal anesthesia (GETA), hypotension, hypoxemia, cardiac arrhythmia.

All relevant English language full-text articles regardless of year of publication were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. From the initial search results, duplicate articles were extracted, and then the titles and abstracts of all potentially relevant studies were screened for eligibility. The reference lists of studies of interest were then manually reviewed for additional articles by cross checking bibliographies as shown in the flow diagram. Two reviewers (TRM and KEH) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all the articles according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any differences were resolved by mutual agreement and in consultation with the third reviewer (DWC). In the case of studies with incomplete information, contact was attempted with the principal authors to obtain additional data.

Study Selection Criteria

This study was prospectively submitted in PROSPERO, an international database of prospectively registered systematic reviews in health and social care. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement outline and Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) reporting guidelines for reporting systematic reviews and meta-analyses was used to report findings – Appendix 1 and Appendix 2.12,13 Full-text manuscripts as well as published abstracts were included this analysis. Only direct comparator studies of MAC versus GETA were included. Non-comparator studies were excluded. A study was also excluded if deemed to have insufficient data, as were review articles, editorials, and correspondence letters that did not report independent data. Case series and reported studies with <10 patients were excluded to minimize selection bias. Multiple published work from similar authors was evaluated for overlapping enrollment times to preserve independence of observations.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measurement in this study was total sedation-related adverse events associated with ERCP when deep sedation with MAC was compared to GETA. Combined anesthesia-associated adverse events were then subdivided by clinically significant hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias, and hypoxemia based upon previous guideline-directed outcomes. Hypoxemic events were defined as those that occurred during or immediately after the procedure while in the recovery room. All-cause adverse events were also recorded. These outcomes were uniformly reported in all studies with standardized definitions used to identify each associated adverse event. Additional baseline characteristics, including the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification system score and Mallampati score were extracted. Post-procedure recovery time, unanticipated conversion from MAC to GETA, and procedural success, defined as ability to complete the intended endoscopic procedure, were also recorded.

Statistical Analysis

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed by calculating pooled proportions. After appropriate studies were identified through systematic review, the individual study proportion was transformed into a quantity using the Freeman–Tukey variant of the arcsine square root transformed proportion. Then the pooled proportion was calculated as the back transform of the weighted mean of the transformed proportions, DerSimonian–Laird weights for the random effects model.4–7 The pooled rates were estimated using random effects models and presented as point estimates (rates) with 95% confidence intervals.15–17 In contrast to fixed effect models, which are used to estimate a common effect, random effect models estimate an average effect, and the variability of the effects represented by their average may have clinical implications.

Sensitivity analysis was performed for only randomized controlled trials and prospective studies, thus excluding retrospective studies. Additionally, univariate meta-regression was planned to be performed to assess the influence of type of positioning (supine versus prone) on primary and secondary outcomes; however, all studies reported prone positioning. All calculated P values were 2-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Tabular and graphical analyses were performing using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software, version 3 (BioStat, Englewood, NJ). Combined weighted proportions were determined by use of the Stata 15.0 software package (Stata Corp LP, College Station, TX).

Risk of Bias and Quality Assessment

Risk of bias and quality of observational studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale and JADAD score for quality of randomized trials.8,18 In this study, high quality was defined as a Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale score of ≥4 or a JADAD score of ≥3. Two authors (TRM and KEH) independently extracted data and assessed the risk of bias and study quality for each of the articles. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus, and in consultation with the third reviewer (DWC).

Investigations of Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was assessed for the individual meta-analyses using the chi squared test and the I2 statistic.16 Significant heterogeneity was defined as P<0.05 using the Cochran Q test or I2 >50%, with values >50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Further quantification of heterogeneity was categorized based upon I2 with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high amounts of heterogeneity, respectively.

Publication Bias

A funnel plot was created and visually inspected for asymmetry and quantitatively using Egger regression testing to assess for publication bias.19,20 If evidence of publication bias, then the trim and fill method was used to correct for funnel plot asymmetry and provide an adjusted effect.21 The classic fail-safe test was also applied to assess risk of bias across studies.

RESULTS

Baseline Study Characteristics

A total of 5 studies (n=1899 patients) were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis.22–26 There were approximately twice as many included individuals (2:1 ratio) that underwent deep sedation with MAC compared to GETA (MAC: n=1284 vs GETA: n=615). A PRISMA flow chart of search results is shown in Figure 1. All studies were published from 2013 to 2019. One randomized controlled trial, 2 prospective studies, and 2 retrospective articles were included. All studies were comparative in design with 3 full-text manuscripts and 2 published abstracts included with all studies from centers in the United States. A complete summary of baseline patient, study, and outcome characteristics assessing deep sedation with MAC versus GETA is highlighted in Table 1.

Included Patient Characteristics

Mean age of all patients was 61.26 ±3.79 years. GETA was more commonly performed in younger patients as compared to MAC (59.43 ±6.64 versus 61.93 ±1.34 years; P<0.001). A slight majority of all included patients were female (50.48%). There were more male patients included in the GETA cohort (52.41% versus 48.46%; P<0.001). Overall patient BMI and mean ASA score was 28.08 ±1.36 kg/m2 and 2.43 ±0.19; however, these were higher for patients that underwent ERCP with GETA [(BMI: 29.39 ±1.81 versus 27.70 ±0.90 kg/m2; P<0.001) and (ASA score: 2.62 ±0.16 versus 2.37 ±0.16; P<0.001)]. There was no difference in Mallampati scores (GA: 3.31 ±0.98 versus MAC: 3.32 ±0.96; P=0.923). Additional patient characteristics are highlighted in Table 1.

Safety and Procedural Sedation Outcomes

Overall, total anesthesia-associated events were not different between MAC and GETA groups [OR 1.33 (95% CI, 0.27 to 6.49); I2=91.72] – Figure 2A. When subdividing anesthesia associated events by type, MAC resulted in fewer episodes of clinically significant hypotension [OR 0.32 (95% CI, 0.12 to 0.87; I2=71.17], increased hypoxemic events [OR 5.61 (95% CI, 1.54 to 20.37) I2=40.15], and no difference in cardiac arrhythmias [OR 0.48 (95%, CI 0.13 to 1.78) I2=41.95] – Figure 2B, 2C, 2D. All-cause total adverse events were similar between groups [1.16 (95% CI, 0.29 to 4.70) I2=94.95] – Figure 3A. Patients undergoing MAC were converted to GETA in 0.61% (95% CI, 0.18 to 0.21) of ERCP procedures – Figure 3B. Mean procedure time was decreased for MAC though there was no difference in time to recovery from anesthesia [standard difference of −0.39 (95% CI, −0.78 to 0.00); I2=82.41 and standard difference −0.48 (95% CI, −1.04 to 0.07); I2=83.72, respectively] – Figure 3C, 3D. Procedural ERCP success was similar between MAC and GETA groups [(OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.51 to 2.64); I2=0.00] – Table 1.

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses

Sensitivity analyses limited to prospective and randomized controlled trials were performed and found no difference in sedation-associated adverse events [OR 1.35 (95% CI, 0.21 to 8.93)]. Notably, when excluding retrospective studies, there were no differences in hypotensive episodes and rates of hypoxemia between deep sedation with MAC and GETA [OR 0.42 (95% CI, 0.09 to 1.89) and OR 8.30 (95% CI, 0.47 to 147.46), respectively]. However, rate of cardiac arrhythmias with deep sedation with MAC became significantly different [OR 0.27 (95% CI, 0.09 to 0.81]. All-cause adverse events remained similar between groups [OR 1.33 (95% CI, 0.21 to 8.60)]. Mean procedure time was no longer significantly different [standard difference of −0.25 (95% CI, −0.72 to 0.22)] with only one study demonstrating a significant difference in recovery time [standard difference of −0.23 (95% CI, −0.43 to −0.03)]. Subgroup analyses were identical to the primary results as all studies performed ERCP in prone positioning.

Risk of Bias Assessment

All observation studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale scores and randomized controlled trials with JADAD score. Quality assessment for each study shown in Table 1. All included studies were considered to be of high quality with Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale scores ≥4 or JADAD scores of ≥3. Publication bias was also assessed. Visual inspection of the funnel plot demonstrated that smaller and statistically insignificant studies appeared to be missing likely due to publication bias – Figure 4A. With the Duval and Tweedie’s trim and fill method, overall rate of anesthesia-associated adverse events remained similar between deep sedation with MAC and GETA [OR 1.33 (95% CI, 0.27 to 6.49) to OR 0.71 (95% CI, 0.15 to 3.37) as evidence by overlapping confidence intervals – Figure 4B.

DISCUSSION

Previous literature has demonstrated that anesthesia provider–administered sedation is safer and associated with lower rates of ERCP failure when compared to endoscopist-administered sedation.10,11 Sedation and analgesia remain a critically important, and often under-emphasized element of endoscopic procedures, especially ERCP. While this systematic review and meta-analysis examined differences between deep sedation with MAC versus GETA, it is important to underscore the importance of having an anesthesiologist or nurse anesthetist present during ERCP. Anesthetist-directed sedation, either for deep sedation or GETA, is advantageous during ERCP, especially as the patient is often placed in a prone position (all included studies in this meta-analysis utilized prone positioning).10,11 This is critical for the endoscopist to understand as the prone position may be associated with altered cardiovascular and pulmonary physiology and may involve limited airway access.10,27

Deeper sedation as used during ERCP also increases the risk of oxygen desaturation compared with other endoscopic procedures, making patients with pre-existing pulmonary disease or those with underlying cardiopulmonary comorbidities more sensitive to sedation and potential hypoxemic events.28 Sedation-related adverse events are more frequent than gastroenterology-associated events.29 Currently, the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) recommends anesthesia provider–administered sedation be considered for complex endoscopic procedures such as ERCP; however, there is no comment regarding deep sedation with MAC or GETA (moderate quality of evidence).10 Thus, while we know that anesthesia provider-administered sedation is safer, data remains sparse as to how to best guide clinicians regarding the optimal choice of MAC versus GETA.

Based upon the results of this comparative systematic review and meta-analysis, deep sedation with both MAC and GETA appear to be safe and effective methods of providing anesthesia for ERCP. Total anesthesia-associated and all-cause adverse events were not statistically different on cumulative and sensitivity analyses between both sedation strategies; however, when stratifying by type of anesthesia-related adverse events, there are some important differences shown between MAC and GETA. Compared to GETA, deep sedation with MAC was associated with fewer hypotensive episodes but more hypoxemic events, both of which are intuitive findings. Hypotension is a well-known dose-dependent side-effect of propofol and many other anesthetics, and deep sedation with MAC typically requires lower doses of anesthesia than GETA, which would thus make these patients less prone to development of hypotension. However, the dose of propofol required to achieve deep sedation with MAC sufficient to tolerate ERCP could result in airway obstruction, atelectasis, and decreased respiratory drive sufficient to cause hypoxemia in a spontaneously breathing, non-intubated patient.

It is also important to note that after sensitivity analyses, hypotensive and hypoxemic events were no longer different. In terms of risk of cardiac arrhythmias, there was no significant difference between deep sedation with MAC and GETA on preliminary analysis, but sensitivity analysis demonstrated a lower number of cardiac arrhythmias with MAC compared to GETA. Induction during GETA may result in a sympathetic surge response that may increase the risk of cardiac arrhythmia. The discrepancy of results between pre- and post-sensitivity analysis for risk of hypotension, hypoxemia, and arrhythmias suggests that more robust research is needed before drawing any definitive conclusions. ERCP remains a complex and often time consuming diagnostic and therapeutic endoscopic procedure that requires a near motionless patient in order to facilitate an intervention requiring precision from the endoscopist.19 The decision whether to use deep sedation with MAC or GETA in a given case depends largely upon patient characteristics, procedural demands of the specific case, and existence of comorbid conditions.19–21

Specific limitations of this study include significant heterogeneity of included studies, including differences in patient population, indications for ERCP, and differences in populations undergoing deep sedation with MAC versus GETA with regards to age, gender, BMI, and ASA status. The two retrospective studies included did demonstrate differences in baseline characteristics (BMI and cardiac disease history) which may lead to selection bias and result in further heterogeneity of the results. While the randomized trial by Smith and colleagues included only high-risk patients (defined as : STOP-BANG≥3, ascites, BMI≥35 kg/m2, pulmonary disease, ASA class 4, Mallampati class 4, and moderate-to-heavy alcohol use), it is possible the non-randomized prospective trials may have unmeasured confounders that have influenced measured outcomes. Furthermore, procedure indication may also influence a provider’s choice of sedation during ERCP. However, among the 3 studies that reported ERCP indication, including the randomized trial, there was no difference in indication between the MAC vs GETA groups. Based upon the presentation of data included in these studies, a meta-regression analysis to identify factors associated with worse outcomes was not possible.

Additionally, both randomized and non-randomized studies were included in this analysis with a reliance upon observation studies with sensitivity analyses of randomized trials and prospective studies demonstrating similar results for the primary outcome of anesthesia-associated adverse events. Individual sedation-associated adverse events (i.e., hypotension, hypoxemia, and cardiac arrhythmias) also differed when excluding retrospective studies. Although all studies in this meta-analysis performed ERCP in a prone or semi-prone position, literature suggests there is no difference in anesthesia-associated adverse events between prone or supine positioning. One initial study demonstrated prone or semi-prone position was associated with a lower risk of cardiopulmonary adverse events, however, two subsequent, higher-powered studies found no difference in rates of hypotension, hypoxemia, or cardiac arrhythmias, based upon patient position.30–33 Thus, there is no guideline driven recommendation as to the most appropriate position for ERCP. Furthermore, there is limited data regarding the indication for ERCP in the included studies. Publication bias was also assessed and present in a meta-analysis; however, correction of such did not significantly alter our findings.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. Most importantly, our meta-analysis methodologically summarizes all available data to evaluate the safety and sedation-related adverse events with ERCP. A previous meta-analysis of propofol versus conscious (moderate) sedation for all advanced endoscopic procedures found that propofol was associated with shorter recovery times, better sedation, and higher rates of amnesia without higher rates of cardiopulmonary adverse events.2,30 To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to examine the safety and sedation-associated adverse event profile of patients undergoing ERCP with deep sedation with MAC versus GETA. While these results are associated with a high level of heterogeneity, additional sensitivity analyses excluding retrospective studies were performed in effort to reduce the possibility of selection bias. We hope these findings provide an important step forward in future research and impact shared clinical decision making among advanced endoscopists and anesthesia providers.

The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that deep sedation with MAC is a safe strategy for ERCP when compared to GETA in terms of overall anesthesia-related adverse events and procedure-related adverse events. Our cumulative meta-analysis revealed MAC was associated with a shorter procedure duration, though sensitivity analyses of only prospective and randomized studies revealed no difference in procedure time. Overall, this study suggests deep sedation with MAC may be increasingly utilized as a safe alternative to GETA for individuals undergoing ERCP; however, individual patient characteristics are pivotal to determine the appropriate level of sedation, as GETA and deep sedation with MAC each possess different risk profiles, as evidenced by different rates of hypoxemia, hypotension, and cardiac arrhythmia in this study.
