Awareness with paralysis in mechanically ventilated patients in the emergency department and intensive care unit: a systematic review & meta-analysis

Abstract

Objective:

Awareness with paralysis (AWP) is a devastating complication for mechanically ventilated patients and can carry long-term psychological sequalae. Hundreds of thousands of patients require mechanical ventilation in the emergency department (ED) and intensive care unit (ICU) annually, yet awareness has only been rigorously examined in the operating room (incidence ~0.1%). This report collates the global literature regarding the incidence of AWP outside of the operating room.

Data Sources:

We searched OvidMedline, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, ClinicalTrials.gov, conference proceedings, and reference lists.

Study Selection:

Randomized or non-randomized studies (except single case studies) reporting on AWP in the ED or ICU were eligible.

Data Extraction:

Two independent reviewers screened abstracts for eligibility.

Data Synthesis:

The search identified 4,454 potentially eligible studies. Seven studies (n = 941 patients) were included for analysis. A random effects meta-analysis of proportions along with multiple subgroup analyses were performed. Significant between-study heterogeneity in reporting of AWP was noted, and the quality of the evidence was low. Analyses stratified by 1) good quality studies and 2) use of the modified Brice questionnaire to detect awareness revealed estimates of 3.4% (0–10.2%, 95% CI) and 1.9% (1.0–3.0%, 95% CI), respectively.

Conclusions:

The incidence of AWP in mechanically ventilated patients in the ED and ICU, as evaluated in a small number of qualifying studies from this comprehensive systematic review, appears much higher than that reported from the operating room. Given the clinical and statistical heterogeneity, caution is warranted in the interpretation of these findings. Further high-quality studies are needed to both define the true incidence and to target the prevention of AWP in this vulnerable patient cohort.

Introduction

For patients undergoing general anesthesia, awareness with paralysis (AWP) is one of the most feared complications in the operating room (OR). This is of critical importance, as studies have shown that up to 70% of patients reporting AWP experience lasting psychological sequelae, including severe symptoms consistent with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).1–6 Thus, intense efforts to prevent AWP from occurring have been recommended.7 Extensive investigation has occurred in the OR, and data from several cohort studies and randomized controlled trials support an incidence of awareness of 0.1–0.2% of OR cases.8–11 However, the focus on this devastating complication has yet to extend outside of the OR.

Hundreds of thousands of patients require mechanical ventilation in the emergency department (ED) and intensive care unit (ICU) annually in the United States alone.12,13 Despite case reports documenting the terrifying psychological aftermath associated with AWP, a paucity of literature from the ED and ICU exists.14,15 Data demonstrate that mechanically ventilated patients in the ED, particularly those who are obese, may receive under dosed induction agents during rapid sequence intubation (RSI),16–18 as well as under dosed and delayed post-RSI sedation.19–22 These factors could increase the risk of AWP, providing rationale to examine AWP further in this vulnerable population.

Given the lack of data regarding AWP in mechanically ventilated ED and ICU patients, it is unknown to what degree AWP is a problem in this cohort. To bridge this critical knowledge gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to: 1) characterize the global biomedical literature examining AWP in mechanically ventilated ED and ICU patients; and 2) estimate the incidence of AWP in this population. We hypothesized that the incidence of AWP would be higher than that reported from the OR and that the literature would be limited in terms of methodological rigor and quantity.

Materials & Methods

Protocol and Registration

In accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocol (PRISMA), this review’s protocol was published in a peer-reviewed journal.23–26 The PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews registration number is CRD42020147236. This protocol was also registered on the Center of Open Science’s OSF registration site.27 The final results are reported according to PRISMA and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines (Supplemental Digital Content 1–2).24,28 This study did not require ethical approval.

Study Identification

An electronic search including the following databases was performed: Ovid Medline, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. These databases were searched from their earliest available dates (i.e. the beginning of each database) through April 2020. The search was conducted and designed in cooperation with a trained medical librarian (L.H.Y.), experienced in the conduct of systematic reviews. The full search strategy is reported in Supplemental Digital Content 3.

The reference lists of included manuscripts were manually screened to identify any additional qualifying studies. A manual search of abstracts published from 2010–2019 was conducted and included the following: Society of Critical Care Medicine, American Thoracic Society, CHEST, European Society of Intensive Care Medicine, International Symposium on Intensive Care and Emergency Medicine, American Association of Critical Care Nurses, American Society of Anesthesiologists, European Society of Anaesthesiology, International Anesthesia Research Society, American College of Emergency Physicians, and the Society for Academic Emergency Medicine. An online search of ClinicalTrials.gov of ongoing and completed trials was also conducted. Corresponding authors were contacted as needed for clarification regarding data and study details.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible if they reported on mechanical ventilation with neuromuscular blockade use in the ED or ICU. Any language or research design, excluding single case reports, was eligible for inclusion provided there was some report on AWP. Reviews, correspondence, and editorials were not eligible for inclusion.

The primary outcome of interest was AWP. The secondary outcomes were any additional awareness or memories reported, the survey methods used to detect AWP, and how AWP was defined. Other secondary outcomes of interest which were searched for but not reported in any of the included studies were outcome differences between patients with and without AWP, including perceived threat, PTSD, delirium, and lengths of stay.

Study Selection & Data Abstraction

Two reviewers (RDP & WW) independently screened abstracts of studies identified by the electronic search. A third reviewer (BMF) arbitrated cases of disagreement. One reviewer (RDP) extracted data from full texts using a standardized data extraction form. Author, publication year, study design, number of patients, study quality or risk of bias, and outcomes were recorded.

Study Quality Assessment

Randomized clinical trials were assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for bias assessment (Supplemental Digital Content 4).29 Cohort studies were assessed using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale and were assigned up to 9 points, with 5 or fewer indicating poor quality (Supplemental Digital Content 5).30 Case series were assessed using the Moga Quality Appraisal Checklist (Supplemental Digital Content 6).31 Quality assessment of abstracts was limited to the information available in the abstracts’ texts.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics and qualitative data were provided for study characteristics, study bias or quality, and methods of awareness reporting. This narrative overview was provided in order to report findings as comprehensively as possible. A meta-analytic approach was used to analyze the summary data using Stata (StataCorp. 2019. Stata Statistical Software: Release 16. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). A random effects meta-analysis of proportions model calculated pooled incidences of AWP with 95% confidence intervals. The Freeman-Tukey Double Arcsine Transformation was applied to stabilize variances.32,33 The I2 statistic calculated between-study heterogeneity.34 Publication bias was assessed for using a funnel plot of standard error versus log odds of AWP.35

Upon completion of study selection and data abstraction, we noted significant statistical and clinical heterogeneity among the studies. Further, the overall pooled event rate for AWP also seemed implausibly high (see below). We therefore conducted post hoc subgroup analyses in order to report results more transparently, account for study heterogeneity, and approach a more realistic and accurate event rate. Two subgroup analyses focused on quality assessment (good quality/low risk of bias vs. poor quality/high risk of bias) and the instrument used to assess for AWP (modified Brice questionnaire vs. other). The modified Brice questionnaire is a structured interview intended to detect awareness, has been used extensively in OR studies, and has been cited as the preferred modality for assessing intraoperative awareness with recall.36–38 These two analyses became the primary focus for reporting and discussion as they: 1) pooled the good quality studies and the most validated assessment tool for AWP; 2) accounted for > 85% of total study population; and 3) reported an event rate that had greater face validity. Another subgroup analysis was performed involving treatment location (ED vs. ICU). As there were six non-randomized studies and one randomized controlled trial included after the systematic review, another subgroup analysis excluded the randomized controlled trial in order to assess any effect that inclusion of a single randomized study had on heterogeneity. A final post hoc analysis was also conducted, which was a fixed effects model, in order to more appropriately distribute the weights to the large, higher quality studies for the pooled analysis.

Deviations from Previously Published Protocol

Given the extensive investigation that has occurred regarding bispectral index (BIS) monitoring in the OR, the published protocol intended to also examine the role of BIS monitoring for the prevention of AWP in the ICU or ED domains.7,38,39 However, because the electronic search did not yield any literature examining the impact of BIS monitoring on AWP outside of the OR, this portion of the protocol was not performed.

Results

Search and Selection

The comprehensive electronic search yielded 4,454 records, of which ten were selected for full text review (Supplemental Digital Content 7). Seven studies were included in the final analysis.40–46

Study Characteristics

The study designs included one randomized controlled trial, five prospective cohort studies, and one case series which were published between 1998 and 2020. Five are peer-reviewed articles, and two are conference abstracts. The total number of subjects across all studies was 941. The published protocol for the randomized controlled trial was reviewed for additional data extraction.47

Table 1 reports each included study and its characteristics. The randomized controlled trial was judged as low risk of bias in six of seven categories (Supplemental Digital Content 4). Three of the included cohort studies were judged as poor quality, and the remaining two were judged as good quality (Supplemental Digital Content 5). The case series was judged as high risk of bias (Supplemental Digital Content 6). Supplemental Digital Content 8 summarizes the reported sedation and neuromuscular blockade use from the included studies.

Three of the seven studies reported on the incidence of AWP in the ICU. PETAL Clinical Trials Network (2019) utilized the modified Brice questionnaire to detect AWP, Wagner et al. (1998) used a survey made de novo, and Arbour (2006) did not specify the survey tool used. All three studies reporting on the ICU assessed for AWP at any point during the ICU stay.

The remaining four studies reported on the incidence of AWP in the ED. Pappal et al. (2020) utilized the modified Brice questionnaire to detect AWP, Puller et al. (2017) used a survey made de novo, while Smith et al. (1998) and Miner et al. (2004) did not report the survey tool used. These latter three studies assessed for AWP only by determining whether subjects remembered undergoing RSI with placement of the endotracheal tube.

Meta-analysis

The aggregate results of the overall pooled meta-analysis on incidence of AWP is presented in Figure 1. The overall estimate of incidence of AWP was 12.3% (2.8–26.0%, 95% CI). The overall I2 was 93.3% indicating statistically significant heterogeneity (p < 0.001). Given the heterogeneity and results of the pooled estimate, as detailed above, two primary subgroup analyses were performed. As stratified by good vs. poor study quality (Figure 2), there was estimated incidence of 3.4% (0–10.2%, 95% CI) for good quality studies and 18.2% (2.7–41.5%, 95% CI) for poor quality studies. The subgroup meta-analysis stratified by the survey tool (modified Brice questionnaire vs. other) used to assess for AWP (Figure 3) demonstrated an estimated incidence of 1.9% (1.0–3.0%, 95% CI) for studies using the modified Brice questionnaire and 21.0% (5.8–41.3%, 95% CI) for the other questionnaire group. The former subgroup meta-analysis suggests heterogeneity between subgroups; however this was not found to be statistically significant (p = 0.089). The subgroup meta-analysis stratified by survey instrument showed statistically significant evidence of between-group heterogeneity (p = 0.001).

Additional meta-analyses were performed. A subgroup meta-analysis stratified by study location (Figure 4) revealed an estimate of incidence of AWP in the ICU of 10.7% (0–40.9%, 95% CI) and in the ED of 14.2% (0.4–39.3%, 95% CI). There was no evidence of heterogeneity between groups (p = 0.89). Another subgroup meta-analysis (Supplemental Digital Content 9) repeated the initial analysis except with exclusion of the lone randomized controlled trial. In response to removal of the randomized study, the overall I2 was minimally reduced from 93.3% to 91.9%, which remained statistically significant for substantial heterogeneity in the meta-analysis (p < 0.001). The final post hoc analysis, using a fixed effects approach with standard inverse-variance weighting, demonstrated an estimated incidence of AWP of 2.3% (1.4–3.3%, 95% CI) (Supplemental Digital Content 10).

The standard error of log odds was plotted against the log odds of AWP for each study in a standard funnel plot (Supplemental Digital Content 11). There was a symmetric distribution of studies across the log odds of the meta-analytic overall estimate, revealing no publication bias.

Discussion

Awareness with paralysis is a largely preventable complication of mechanical ventilation which can lead to devastating, long-term psychological sequelae.1–6 For surgical patients in the OR, AWP has been studied extensively, with a reported incidence of 0.1–0.2%.8–11 Risk factors for AWP include 1) under dosing of sedatives, 2) lack of protocolled monitoring of sedation depth, 3) the use of neuromuscular blocking agents, and 4) a total intravenous anesthetic approach (vs. inhaled anesthetics).48,49 The focus on AWP has yet to extend to the ED and ICU, where hundreds of thousands of patients undergo mechanical ventilation annually in the United States.12,13 This review was undertaken to assess the current evidence and estimate the incidence of AWP in the ED and ICU. Given prior literature documenting the suboptimal dosing of induction agents and post-intubation sedation in the ED,16–22 as well as the lack of rigorous study of AWP outside of the OR, we hypothesized that the incidence of AWP would be greater in the ED and ICU than the ~0.1% reported from the OR.

The main finding was that the pooled estimate of good quality studies and studies using the Brice questionnaire for assessment of AWP estimate the incidence of AWP to be 1.9% to 3.4% (with wide confidence intervals), an estimate substantially higher compared to the OR. The meta-analysis of all included studies revealed an overall estimate of 12.3% (2.8%−26.0%, 95% CI). In light of the substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity among the included studies, we focused on the subgroup analyses exploring estimates from the good quality studies and the studies that utilized the modified Brice questionnaire (the preferred modality for awareness detection), as these subgroups are likely to provide estimates closer to the true incidence of AWP outside of the OR. The modified Brice questionnaire subgroup had an estimated incidence of 1.9% (1.0–3.0%, 95% CI), and included 85.8% of the total study population.40,46 The good quality subgroup had an estimated incidence of 3.4% (0–10.2%, 95% CI) and included 86.9% of the total study population.40,42,46

Based on these subgroup estimates, the event rate from the overall meta-analysis is likely inflated secondary to 1) lower quality studies, and 2) the use of suboptimal questionnaires in the assessment of awareness with paralysis. Because the subgroup estimates exclude studies that are both poor quality and lacking the modified Brice questionnaire as the survey tool, it is possible that future large, rigorous trials examining AWP outside of the OR may yield estimates closer to these subgroup estimates of 1.9–3.4%. While lower than the overall meta-analytic estimate, these subgroup estimates are still ~20-fold greater than the well-established incidence of AWP in the OR, representing a substantially increased risk of this devastating complication for mechanically ventilated patients in the ED and ICU.

Another important finding is that although the meta-analysis demonstrates some concerning data with regards to the incidence of AWP, it revealed an overall dearth of literature for ED and ICU patients. Only seven studies fulfilled inclusion criteria, six of which were small and single-center. These facts, along with some of the methodological limitations of the included studies, further indicate a low confidence level regarding the incidence of AWP in critically ill patients. Our results suggest an urgent need to study this complication further in order to mitigate its morbid impact on survivors of critical illness.

This systematic review and meta-analysis has several limitations. Heterogeneity, both statistical and clinical, must be acknowledged. There was an I2 of 93.3% and differences in study size, quality, and design existed. Further, differences between the studies in the reporting of AWP is another source for the observed heterogeneity. For example, three of the four ED-based studies reported AWP in relation to RSI only (range of 5.9–45%), whereas ICU-based studies focused on the post-intubation mechanical ventilation phase of therapy. It seems unlikely that sustained neuromuscular blocker use in the ICU would confer the same risk as a single dose for RSI, but this is currently unknown, and we observed similar incidences of AWP between the ED and ICU studies. Different methods were used to assess for awareness events, and some studies did not report how AWP was assessed for at all. Different approaches have been used to detect AWP, with the most common being the Brice questionnaire as well as self-reporting.36 The inclusion of studies from both the ED and ICU domains is another source of heterogeneity. However, critically ill patients requiring mechanical ventilation are an inherently heterogeneous group of patients, with a wide range of illness and injury leading to respiratory failure. While similar critical care principles are applied in both environments by physicians, the inherent differences between the ED and ICU (e.g. staffing, acuity, etc.) add another source of heterogeneity that must be considered.

Despite a rigorous and extensive search, only seven studies were included, lending only a small collection of studies for meta-analysis. As our search methodology was exhaustive, we are confident that we have the most extensive report regarding the literature in this field to date. Among our studies, a standard funnel plot did not reveal any evidence of publication bias. As noted prior, we found evidence of substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity, which reduces confidence in the overall meta-analytic estimate.

There were also significant differences in design among the included studies. In particular, there were six non-randomized studies (both cohort and case series) and a single randomized controlled trial. We combined randomized and non-randomized studies for the final meta-analysis for two reasons: 1) meta-analysis after removal of the lone randomized study (Supplemental Digital Content 9) resulted in only a minor decrease in the overall I2 from 93.3% to 91.9%, which remained statistically significant for substantial heterogeneity, and 2) removal of the randomized study caused a substantial change in the estimated incidence of AWP in the overall meta-analysis (12.3% to 16.1%). Because the randomized trial was the most rigorous among the included studies, we felt it was necessary to include in the final analysis to ensure the most accurate overall estimate of incidence of AWP outside of the OR.

The survey tool utilized to assess for AWP varied substantially among studies, with only two studies using the modified Brice questionnaire, a survey tool originally developed to detect intraoperative AWP.36–38 The majority of included studies had small sample sizes, increasing the risk of inaccurate estimates of AWP. The included studies were published between 1998 and 2020, spanning over two decades during which emergency and critical care practices have evolved. Differences in standards of care over time may reduce our confidence in the external validity of our analysis, underscoring the need for updated studies on AWP. Finally, AWP can only be assessed in those patients that survive to extubation. Though it is impossible to assess for AWP in non-survivors, these patients may have a lower or higher event rate, so it should be noted that any estimation of AWP can only apply to the surviving proportion of the cohort.

Given the above limitations, we recognize that the pooling of studies in order to conduct a formal meta-analysis should be questioned and that the point estimates could be an inaccurate reflection of the true event rate. We chose to proceed with meta-analysis in order to provide a comprehensive and pragmatic estimate of the incidence of AWP anywhere outside the OR. We also conducted several subgroup and post-hoc analyses in order to provide a more realistic estimate of the incidence of AWP. The above methodological factors make it difficult to state with any confidence just how accurate our meta-analytic estimate of the incidence of AWP is, and we strongly encourage caution in the interpretation of these results. A more appropriate take away from the current work may be that a true single incidence cannot be found at this time, but there is likely a high degree of practice variation and a high degree of between-study variation resulting in great variation in reported event rates. Our results suggest that AWP is definitely not a zero event, occurs in an important minority of patients, and is factors higher than that reported in the OR.

Going forward, future multi-center studies should be performed that encompass the entire period of mechanical ventilation (i.e. ED and ICU) and robustly assess for AWP in large cohorts of patients using rigorous methodology, including established survey tools. Further, studies should include additional patient-oriented outcomes, such as the psychologic sequelae experienced by mechanically ventilated patients. These strategies would allow for better planning and conduct of clinical trials aimed at the reduction of awareness events in critically ill mechanically ventilated patients.

Conclusions

The incidence of awareness with paralysis in mechanically ventilated patients in the ED and ICU, as evaluated in a small number qualifying studies, is much higher than that reported from the OR. Given its negative impact on patient-centered clinical outcomes, AWP deserves further investigation in patients suffering from respiratory failure.
