Work as a social risk factor in pregnancy: a systematic review of screening practices related to working conditions and family leave among pregnant adults

Abstract

Background:

Paid family leave and working conditions are components of employment quality, a key social determinant of health across the life course, particularly during pregnancy. Increased research into social risk screening has not extended to employment quality. The objective of this systematic review was to identify screening practices and interventions in healthcare settings that address employment and working conditions as social risk factors among pregnant adults and to describe their properties and key findings.

Method:

We searched MEDLINE, PsychINFO, SocINDEX, EMBASE, and the SIREN Evidence and Resource library for studies published through 2/14/2022. We selected multiple search terms related to four domains: (1) employment or working conditions; (2) screening; (3) healthcare settings; and (4) pregnancy or maternal health.

Results:

Of the 2,317 unique titles and abstracts that were potentially relevant, eight articles met all inclusion criteria and focused on pregnant populations. The content of identified screening practices varied substantially, highlighting the multiple ways employment is conceptualized as a potential risk factor. Few studies included multidimensional measures of employment to assess working conditions, which may be particularly relevant during pregnancy.

Conclusions:

Our review suggests that screening for employment as a social risk factor does not regularly occur in prenatal care. While pragmatic properties of the screening tools we assessed are promising, tools seldom examine the multidimensional nature of work. Understanding the principal intent of screening for employment could provide greater opportunity to collect and interpret contextual factors that influence how both providers and patients respond to social risk.

BACKGROUND

Paid family leave and working conditions are components of employment quality, a key social determinant of health (SDoH) across the life course, with particularly acute impact during the critical period of pregnancy. Work schedule (e.g., rotating shift work or night shifts), exposure to physically demanding work (e.g., heavy lifting or prolonged standing), and occupational chemical exposure have been linked to a range of adverse birth outcomes.1–4 Paid family and medical leave, which enables workers to take time away from work both during pregnancy and to recover from childbirth and care for a new baby, is associated with decreased rates of low birthweight and infant mortality, increased breastfeeding, and improved maternal mental health.5–9 While work during pregnancy is generally safe, some people work in jobs or workplaces that present risks to their health and that of their baby, particularly if accommodations, like breaks to sit down, are not made. The effects of unsafe workplace practices are potentially far-reaching: two-thirds of women giving birth for the first time were employed during pregnancy, with most of these (86%) working full time and into their third trimester.10

The potential for pregnancy-specific risks from employment led to a recent Committee Opinion from the American Congress of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) detailing the ways in which obstetrician-gynecologists (OBGYNs) and other obstetric care providers can support pregnant working people.3 Yet many providers report that they do not have the tools needed to discuss work-related reproductive risks with patients.4 One study of U.S. obstetricians found that, despite acknowledging the importance of counseling patients about environmental exposures during pregnancy, including those from their job or workplace, the majority do not feel confident asking about these exposures and therefore do not regularly ask their patients about them.11 Participants in that study described concerns about discussing a topic about which they could not sufficiently answer questions or provide resources, and discomfort helping patients balance concerns about occupational risk with those about job loss and economic insecurity.

Health systems have increasingly come to recognize the importance of adverse SDoH (also known as ‘social risks’) and seek to mitigate their effects. One manifestation is through use of screening tools that attempt to systematically collect information about patients’ social risks such as housing instability, food insecurity, and intimate partner violence (IPV).12 A recent systematic review of interventions to address social risks among pregnant or postpartum patients found 26 studies, with the majority targeting IPV and just two that targeted employment or workplace exposures.13 Despite “work” being listed in the WHO’s definition of social determinants of health,i relatively little has been published about screening practices within healthcare settings for employment or working conditions as social risk factors. A 2017 systematic review of interventions to address patients’ social and economic needs identified four articles that targeted employment needs specifically, with a few additional studies including employment among a list of targeted social needs.15 In their systematic review of employment interventions in healthcare settings, Pinto and colleagues, using broader inclusion criteria for interventions, identified and synthesized 88 articles, mostly (89%) focusing on patients with mental illness.16 Importantly, they define a positive employment outcome as gaining employment in any form, but highlight this as a limitation given the rise of precarious jobs that may be of low quality. The authors urge future interventions to include job quality among measured outcomes.

Given the robust body of evidence that links certain working conditions, occupational exposures, and the lack of paid leave to adverse perinatal health, screening for the presence or absence of employment may not provide enough nuance to be clinically relevant for this population. We sought to identify and describe tools administered within the healthcare setting that screen for employment or working conditions among pregnant people.

The objective of this systematic review was to identify screening practices and interventions that take place in healthcare settings to address employment and working conditions as a social risk factor among pregnant adults and to describe their properties and key findings.

METHODS

Data sources

We searched electronic bibliographic databases including MEDLINE, PsycINFO, SocINDEX, EMBASE, and the SIREN (Social Interventions and Research & Evaluation Network) Evidence and Resource library for studies that described screening and related interventions that occur within a clinical healthcare services delivery system to address work (i.e., employment, work conditions) as a social risk factor in prenatal populations for studies published through February 14, 2022.

In consultation with a reference librarian, we selected multiple search terms related to four domains: (1) employment or working conditions; (2) screening; (3) healthcare settings; and (4) pregnancy or maternal health. Our complete search strategy with all search terms is included in Appendix Table 1.

We supplemented our database search with hand searches of reference lists of included studies and by entering included studies into Scopus to identify citing articles. We also searched bibliographies of published systematic reviews that came up through our database search but excluded due to their study design. The review was registered with PROSPERO (#CRD42021224406) before beginning study activities.

Study selection

We included observational and experimental studies in the review. Studies were selected if (1) screening practices and/or interventions were integrated into a clinical healthcare services delivery system and (2) they included some quantified employment exposure (e.g., lack of employment, precarious or unsafe employment, employment that is preventing healthcare utilization) or outcome (e.g., change in work conditions or employment rate among patients) in prenatal populations. There were no publication date restrictions for inclusion.

Articles were excluded if they were published in languages other than English, were not empirical, were identified as a duplicate, screened patient populations outside of healthcare settings (e.g., in workplaces or other community settings), or used a screening tool that did not assess any dimension of employment.

Data screening

We used Rayyan systematic review software for article title and abstract screening. Article titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (J.G., M.C.). Discrepancies were solved by open discussion between reviewers. Articles selected for full text review were then obtained and imported into EndNote, where they were independently assessed by the same two reviewers for inclusion. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus and, when necessary, a third reviewer (J-M.G.)

Data extraction and quality assessment

A standard template was constructed in Excel to extract the following data from included studies: year, country or region, study design, setting/context, patient and/or clinician characteristics, screening tool/practice, type of intervention, outcomes evaluated (including harms), and main findings. A single team member (M.C.) abstracted the data and a second team member (J.G.) checked the abstraction for completeness and accuracy.

The quality of each study was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) Version 2018,17 which permits appraisal of the methodological quality of five study categories: qualitative studies, randomized controlled trials, non-randomized studies, quantitative descriptive studies, and mixed methods studies. One reviewer (J.G.) independently completed the initial assessment of articles for inclusion criteria.

We used tables and narrative synthesis for each review question and primary outcome to summarize our findings.

RESULTS

Of the 2,317 unique titles and abstracts that were potentially relevant, 142 underwent full-text review. Eight articles met all inclusion criteria and focused specifically on pregnant populations (Figure 1).18–25 Seven of the included studies were quantitative descriptive studies and one was a non-randomized controlled trial. Table 1 summarizes the included articles.

We organize the remainder of the results section around the following domains: frequency with which providers ask about employment-related risks during pregnancy; feasibility, acceptability and sensitivity of tools/practices for identifying employment-related risks; prevalence of work-related risk; and, finally, interventions to address employment-related risks.

Frequency with which providers ask about employment-related risks during pregnancy

Relatively few OBGYNS report regularly asking about employment-related risks during pregnancy. We identified two studies that examined how often OBGYNs asked pregnant patients about their jobs, shown in Appendix Table 2. Pompeii et al. (2011) asked 384 OBGYNs in Texas who responded to a mail survey how often they ask patients about employment status and specific occupational activities. The survey was developed based on foundational key informant interviews with two obstetricians and four occupational medicine physicians to determine the types of information they collect. While an overwhelming majority of respondents reported routinely collecting information about employment status (96.3%), job type (82.5%), problems with pregnancy due to their job (73.1%), and job title (71.7%), far fewer routinely collected information about specific occupational activities such as lifting (46.2%), standing/walking (42.7%), shift or night work (34.4%), climbing ladders (28.5%), or bending (23.4%). Grindler et al. (2018) conducted an online survey of OBGYNs recruited through social media and the Department of OBGYN at University of Colorado to examine environmental screening practices at new patient visits. Of the 312 responses, fewer than 30% screened for any occupational or environmental exposures.

Feasibility, acceptability and sensitivity of tools/practices for identifying employment-related risks

The few existing screening tools for identifying employment-related risk factors appear to be feasible to administer, acceptable to respondents, and show promise in their ability to identify risks. Three studies described pragmatic properties of tools or practices for identifying employment-related risks among pregnant populations (Appendix Table 3). Price et al. (2017) described an antenatal risk factor survey that asked patients in the public waiting rooms of hospital clinics in Australia about whether they are currently employed (full-time, part-time, or no) and whether they have ever worked, among other psychosocial risks. A 10-item version of the survey, which included whether the respondent had ever worked but dropped current employment, was then compared to social risks identified through electronic medical record (EMR) data. Among women identified as having psychosocial risks through EMR, 92% were identified by a survey risk factor count of one or more. The survey was deemed feasible based on the high proportion of women who had time to consent and complete the surveys before their appointment, and acceptable based on the number of women who completed the survey and the lack of missingness on any item. In a later study across 10 Australian public birthing hospitals, Price et al. (2019) examined whether this same 10-item survey (here referred to as the brief risk factor, or BRF, survey) identified psychosocial and socioeconomic risk relative to a private, in-home interview. Among those approached to take the BRF survey, 87% agreed to participate and 80% of those eligible completed the survey. The BRF showed a dose-response relationship with risks identified through the in-person interview, indicating that it identified women experiencing psychosocial and socioeconomic risk. Zachek et al. (2019) assessed the feasibility of incorporating a self-administered occupational and environmental exposure questionnaire into obstetric clinics. The Pregnancy and Work Questionnaire asks employed prenatal clinic patients about job characteristics, workplace and hobby exposures, protective equipment use, and symptoms during pregnancy. 96% (69/72) of eligible women who were invited to participate enrolled; of those 87% completed the questionnaire, which took approximately 10 minutes to complete. Comparing two intentionally redundant ways of assessing hazard exposure – reported job tasks or activities and chemicals used – responses were highly consistent. Open-ended job activity questions were less sensitive than closed-ended questions and failed to identify exposure to the two most common hazards in their sample: physical stressors and cleaning products. The authors reported that clinic workflow was minimally disrupted by the study’s implementation; questionnaires were completed quickly in the waiting room before appointments and with limited assistance.

Prevalence of work-related risk

The prevalence of work-related risks among pregnant adults is hard to characterize based on the literature. Four studies report prevalence or frequency of work-related risks (Table 2), although the type of risk measured varies widely: current employment status, whether the respondent has ever had a job, how many jobs the respondent held during pregnancy, and exposure to workplace hazards. The little available literature focuses primarily on employment status (being employed/having ever held a job vs. not); just one study includes measures of working conditions. Mason et al. (2001) described a structured screening, monitoring, and evaluation instrument that includes an intake form to gather psychosocial information at the first or second prenatal appointment, followed by identification of psychosocial problems by a social worker in a clinical intake interview. Of the 435 patients who were screened, 25.3% were employed. Among 200 patients who were referred for ongoing social work services, 17 (8.5%) reported problems coping with their job. In a sample of 166 English-speaking pregnant women attending antenatal care at two metropolitan Australian hospitals, Price et al. (2017) found that 7% had no household income, 61% were not currently working, and 10% had never had a job. Goldfeld et al. (2018) examined prevalence, frequency, and co-occurrence between risk factors on the BRF survey in a sample of women receiving care across 10 Australian public birthing hospitals (same sample as described above in Price et al. (2019)). 11.7% of respondents reported no household income (i.e., no one in the household with a paid income) and 6.9% of respondents reported never having had a job. Among 385 women who never had a job, 55.1% had poorer global health, 51.7% had low educational attainment, 34.3% had no household income, and 33% were young (less than 23 years). In a socioeconomically diverse sample of employed pregnant women receiving prenatal care at a large public hospital in San Francisco, Zachek et al. (2019) found that participants worked an average of 1.2 jobs during pregnancy (range 1–4) and 93% of respondents reported exposure to at least one workplace hazard.

Interventions to address employment-related risks

While just two publications describe interventions that address employment-related concerns (Table 3), these studies illustrate strategies for addressing potentially harmful working conditions in the prenatal healthcare setting. In their study of the Pregnancy and Work Questionnaire, Zachek et al. (2019) referred participants that reported a combination of non-pregnancy-related symptoms and occupational or hobby exposure (or employment in an industry, occupation or job task associated with chemical exposure) to an occupational and environmental medicine clinic for follow-up. 52% of respondents received such referrals. Van Beukering et al. (2021) evaluated the effectiveness of a blended care program, Pregnancy and Work, that combines provider training and a patient-facing mHealth application to provide pregnant women and their obstetric care providers with personalized advice on work adjustments. Comparing employed women less than 20 weeks gestation in the Netherlands who either received the blended care program (n=119) or care as usual (n=122), they found that the intervention significantly increased the percentage of pregnant people receiving advice from their midwife or obstetrician about how to adjust their work (adjusted relative risk (aRR) 2.2, 95% CI 1.4 to 3.4), but no significant differences in realized work adjustments. Of the 90 healthcare providers (47 midwives, 10 OBGYNS and 32 occupation health physicians) who participated in the training, 98% rated the training as valuable, 94% would recommend the app to their patients, and 87% would use it.

DISCUSSION

Our systematic review of the literature suggests that screening for employment as a social risk factor during pregnancy does not regularly occur. Our systematic review identified just eight articles that describe screening practices for employment-related social risks among prenatal populations. This is consistent with recent systematic reviews of social needs interventions that show relatively limited focus on employment in both general adult and perinatal populations.13,15 The content of identified screening practices varied substantially, highlighting the multiple ways employment is conceptualized as a potential risk factor. Several studies included just a single question about whether the respondent has ever had a job21,22 or whether they were currently working.23 This is consistent with most commonly used social risk screening tools, such as the WE CARE Screening Tool and AAFP Social Needs Screening Tool, which focus on whether or not an individual is employed (or looking for work), but do not examine the nature of the work itself. While there is a robust literature that links certain working conditions, such as shift work, to health,26–29 multidomain screening tools targeting a general adult population may prioritize a relatively simple employment question. However, the binary nature of assessing individual employment leaves little room for collecting and interpreting contextual factors that may play a role in how providers respond to an individual’s risk. The lack of robust evidence-based screening tools could be a cause for concern among prenatal providers as some studies have shown their hesitancy to ask about risks that they are not equipped to address through the health system. Their perspective is echoed by that of patients, as they too, report frustration with being asked about social risks when they are not linked to solutions.30 Given that tools with binary employment questions are common, further investigation is needed to understand whether this type of question is trying to uncover some aspect of work or employment as a social risk factor, or whether it is being used as a proxy for income or poverty. Understanding the principal intent of asking about employment in social risk screening tools is integral to evaluate how health systems can respond.

Distinguishing the purpose of employment questions is especially important in pregnant populations as the need to understand a patient’s working conditions is potentially more acute. For example, some jobs involve exposure to chemicals or radiation that may carry some risk to all workers, but are particularly risky for pregnant workers because of their effects on a developing fetus (and potentially also the health of subsequent generations).31 Other jobs that pose minimal physical risks to a non-pregnant worker may carry with them increased health risks for pregnant workers, especially if no accommodations are made. For example, jobs that involve prolonged standing (e.g., cashiers, restaurant servers, janitors, nurses) do not necessarily pose a health risk unless a pregnant worker is not allowed opportunities to sit as needed. We found just one example of a screening tool that could be used to assess patients’ work-related physical and chemical exposures during pregnancy.18 Another argument for a more nuanced understanding of employment during pregnancy is that work adjustments during and after pregnancy are very common. Among women pregnant with their first child who worked at some point during pregnancy, 21% took paid leave from work, 19% took unpaid leave from work, 16% quit their jobs, 3% were fired during their pregnancy. After giving birth, almost one in five of these women who did return to work changed employers, and changes in hours, pay, and skill level were common.10 While commonly used social screening tools may suffice for a general population, tools implemented in settings serving specific populations may benefit from a more tailored approach. In pregnant populations, efforts could include incorporating additional employment questions related to working conditions that have been linked to health of this and subsequent generations and establishing best practices about the frequency of screening to capture the fluid nature of work during pregnancy and after giving birth.

Our findings should be situated within the broader question of what social risk screening is for. Screening itself helps to raise awareness of social risks experienced by an individual patient or patient population, but there is no strong evidence that screening in isolation addresses the underlying need.32 Instead, screening, along with other forms of raising awareness of social risks, may help to inform additional activities to integrate social care into healthcare delivery, as outlined in a 2019 National Academies committee report.32 In the case of employment-related risk, these could include offering prenatal care outside of normal work hours to facilitate attendance among people working full-time or discussing strategies for avoiding or mitigating specific occupational risks (adjustment); for patients in need of workplace accommodations during pregnancy, providing information about workplace rights, writing doctors’ notes to support work accommodation or leave requests, and/or referring patients to legal aid (assistance); and working to promote paid family leave policies at the local, state, or national levels (alignment and advocacy). Our review uncovered very few tools with sufficient detail to inform adjustment activities,18,19 and just one that approaches assistance.19 This highlights a need for more detailed study of screening practices and other activities to align employment-related support with prenatal care.

Our systematic review initially set out to answer additional questions that were not discussed because of the complete lack of published literature. No studies examined the direct relationship between screening practices and health outcomes, nor did any studies examine potential harms associated with these screening practices or related interventions.

Limitations

First, the lack of literature related to screening for employment in healthcare settings, and more specifically, in pregnant adults limits the ability to do a meta-analysis or synthesize results quantitatively. Second, the self-reported provider surveys described in the results section could have biased findings related to the frequency with which providers ask about employment-related risks during pregnancy. Lastly, though our search strategy was robust and gray literature was assessed, our inclusion criteria limited articles to those published in English which could have unintentionally excluded studies from countries that have more nationally based health systems and more commonly participate in related work.

Conclusions

This review sought to identify screening practices and interventions to address employment and working conditions as a social risk factor among pregnant adults. While pragmatic properties of the screening tools assessed in this review are promising, the tools often did not examine the nature of the work itself. Understanding the principal intent of screening for employment beyond binary measures could provide greater opportunity to collect and interpret contextual factors that play a role in how both providers and patients respond to an identified risk. The perceived lack of ability to address risks through the health system from provider and patient perspectives establishes that more efforts are needed to assess the relationship between screening for employment in healthcare settings and work adjustments in pregnant adults. Future research should consider how relational findings between the two can be used to develop more tailored approaches to screening for employment and other social risk factors in pregnant populations. Relatedly, to develop a more robust understanding of the current landscape of employment related screening practices and interventions in healthcare settings, more research should be done to assess application in a general population.

Interventions to address employment-related risks

Article Population Intervention Outcomes

(2019) 69 English- or Spanish-speaking adult women, <20 weeks gestation and currently working, receiving prenatal care from a public hospital in San Francisco, CA between March and August 2011. Qualified staff reviewed Pregnancy and Work Questionnaire within 24 h of completion, and referred selected participants to the UCSF occupational and environmental medicine clinic for additional follow-up. A blended care ‘Pregnancy and Work’ program, consisting of a training session for professionals and an mHealth application to provide pregnant women and their obstetrical caregivers with advice on work adjustment. The percentage of women receiving advice from their midwife or obstetrician to adjust their work was 9% in the intervention group vs 2% in the control group at 16 weeks of pregnancy (RR 5.64), and 39% vs 18% at 24 weeks of pregnancy (RR 2.18)
