Topical Pharmacologic Interventions Versus Active Control, Placebo, or No Treatment for Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis: Findings From a Cochrane Systematic Review

Abstract

PURPOSE:

To summarize key findings from a Cochrane systematic review of the effectiveness and safety of topical pharmacologic interventions compared with active control or placebo for epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (EKC).

DESIGN:

Systematic review.

METHODS:

We included randomized controlled trials that compared antiseptic agents, virustatic agents, or immune-modulating topical therapies with placebo or an active control. We adhered to Cochrane methods for trial selection, data extraction, risk of bias evaluation, and data synthesis.

RESULTS:

Ten randomized controlled trials with 892 participants with acute or chronic EKC were included. Eight trials compared interventions with artificial tears or saline (n = 4) or with steroids (n = 4); two 3-arm trials contributed data to both comparisons. Estimates suggested that compared with tears, after povidone-iodine (PVP-I) alone (2 studies, 409 participants) more participants with acute EKC had resolution of symptoms (risk ratio [RR] 1.15 [95% confidence interval {CI} 1.07-1.24]) and signs (RR 3.19 [95% CI 2.29-4.45]) within 10 days. In 2 trials comparing treatments with steroid alone or steroid with levofloxacin, fewer eyes treated with PVP-I or polyvinyl alcohol iodine (PVA-I) plus steroid developed subepithelial infiltrates within 21 days (RR 0.08 [95% CI 0.01-0.55]; 69 eyes). No treatment was shown to improve resolution of infiltrates.

CONCLUSIONS:

Low- to very low–level certainty of evidence suggested that PVP-I or PVA-I with steroid may confer some benefit in acute EKC, but imprecision from small sample sizes, the potential risk of bias from inadequate reporting or trial design, and variability in participant selection, outcome measurement, and reporting limit the amount and quality of evidence.

Viruses cause about 80% of all cases of acute conjunctivitis.1-6 Human adenoviruses (HAdVs) are believed to account for 65% to 90% of cases of viral conjunctivitis,7 or 20% to 75% of all causes of infectious keratoconjunctivitis (KC) worldwide, making them the most common cause of viral conjunctivitis.6,8,9 Epidemic KC (EKC) is a highly infectious subset of conjunctivitis caused by HAdVs and, to a much lesser extent, by Coxsackievirus and Enterovirus. Large outbreaks of adenoviral EKC have occurred on military installations and at medical centers. In a 6-month period in Chicago, 401 patients, staff members, and physicians at the Illinois Eye and Ear Infirmary developed adenoviral EKC.10 EKC is accompanied by severe conjunctival inflammation, watery discharge, and light sensitivity and can lead to chronic complications, such as scarring from corneal subepithelial infiltrates (SEIs), conjunctival membranes, symblepharon,11,12 and dry eye syndrome. SEI can develop a few weeks after the acute phase of EKC and often is associated with long-term symptoms of glare and impaired quality of vision.

There is lack of consensus on the efficacy of any treatment to alter the clinical course of EKC, which has at least 2 distinct clinical and pathophysiological phases: the acute phase, in which viral replication and ocular surface inflammation are prominent, and the chronic, or delayed phase, which manifests later and is clinically evident solely as corneal SEIs. Many clinicians offer only supportive care in the form of lubrication and cold compresses to provide symptom relief during the acute phase. The goals of pharmacotherapy for either phase are to lessen symptom severity, to shorten the duration of signs and symptoms, to restore comfort and visual function, and, for the acute phase, to decrease development of sequelae (eg, SEIs, membranes, symblepharon) associated with long-term morbidity. Successful treatment must be balanced against long-term dependence on or adverse effects from pharmacotherapy.13 Although not a commonly reported outcome measure, an important goal of therapy is prevention of transmission of EKC to an unaffected fellow eye.

Some clinicians offer topical corticosteroids for highly symptomatic patients, especially those with SEIs or membranes. Other treatments have included virustatic agents, such as cidofovir and cyclosporine A (CsA),14,15 interferon B,16-19 and tacrolimus.20 It is unclear whether these therapies are more effective than no treatment or artificial tears. Treatment typically costs more than vehicle, artificial tears, or no treatment and may induce drug allergy, sensitivity, or toxicity to the eye or prolong viral shedding.21,22 However, rapid resolution of signs and symptoms restores vision-related quality of life and ability to return to work. The main objective of this summary of our Cochrane systematic review is to report the comparative safety and effectiveness of topical pharmacologic interventions compared with placebo, active control, or no treatment, based on the best currently available evidence.23

METHODS

Adhering to the methods in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, 24 we included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in our review. A summary of methods is provided below; details can be found in the full Cochrane systematic review.23 Eligible trials compared antiviral agents (ganciclovir and trifluridine), corticosteroids, antiseptic agent (PVP-I or equivalent), calcineurin inhibitors (CsA and tacrolimus), without regard for dosage or duration of treatment, with control interventions. Control treatments included placebo, vehicle, artificial tears, saline, no treatment, or corticosteroids. We excluded studies of participants with adenoviral conjunctivitis that was not specified as EKC unless there was an EKC subgroup that had been randomized and for which outcomes had been reported separately. In our search, we included trials that enrolled patients in any phase of EKC. We included studies that enrolled both adult and pediatric patients.

SEARCH METHODS:

We searched the electronic databases CENTRAL (which contains the Cochrane Eyes and Vision Trials Register), Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid MEDLINE In-Process and Other Non-Indexed Citations, Ovid MEDLINE Daily, Ovid OLDMEDLINE, EMBASE, PubMed, Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature Database, the metaRegister of Controlled Trials (www.controlledtrials.com), ClinicalTrials.gov, and the World Health Organization (WHO) International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (www.who.int/ictrp/search/en). We did not impose any date or language restrictions in the electronic search for trials. We last searched the electronic databases on April 27, 2021. The Cochrane systematic review includes our detailed search strategy for each electronic database.23

TRIAL SELECTION:

Pairs of authors independently reviewed titles and abstracts to identify citations that definitely or possibly were eligible based on our inclusion criteria. The final eligibility decision was based on independent review by 2 authors of the full text of articles; disagreements were resolved by discussion.

OUTCOMES OF INTEREST:

The primary review outcomes considered mainly the acute phase of EKC, including decreasing the mean time (measured in days) from initiation of treatment until resolution of signs or symptoms and/or increasing the proportion of patients with resolutions of signs or symptoms by 7 days after treatment.25 In trials where many signs and symptoms were evaluated individually, conjunctival injection or “redness” was chosen as the primary sign and ocular discomfort or pain as the primary symptom.

Secondary outcomes focused mainly on the chronic, or delayed, phase, including the proportion of participants in whom SEI had developed by day 21 after treatment initiation; the proportion of participants in whose eyes SEI had disappeared by day 21 after treatment initiation; and the proportion of participants who discontinued medication for SEI without rebound signs and symptoms by day 21 after initiation of treatment. Additional outcomes included the proportion of participants with evidence of adenoviral eradication based on results of negative cell culture immunofluorescence assay, polymerase chain reaction, or other methods by day 14 after treatment initiation; the proportion of initially unaffected fellow eyes that developed signs/symptoms of infection within 7 days of onset of signs/symptoms in the first eye; the proportion of participants who experienced adverse events including severe ocular discomfort, redness, or discharge, which might be signs of toxicity; intraocular pressure elevation ≥22 mm Hg; and costs of interventions.

DATA COLLECTION AND ASSESSMENT OF TRIALS FOR RISK OF BIAS:

For all outcomes, we recorded the available data for each outcome closest to the time points specified. Within pairs of review authors, authors independently extracted and then cross-verified data from the included studies regarding trial characteristics, methods, participants, interventions, outcomes, and funding sources. We used the web-based software Covidence to store and manage the data.26 We compared participant and design characteristics among the trials to judge clinical and methodologic heterogeneity in Review Manager.27 We attempted to contact study investigators by electronic mail when necessary. We also sought complete trial information from pharmaceutical websites wherever possible. We did not impute any outcome data. Review authors also worked in pairs independently to evaluate the included studies for potential sources of bias based on guidelines in the Cochrane Handbook.28 We judged each study to have been at low, high, or unclear risk (whenever the information provided was insufficient to make an assessment) of bias of each type and documented reasons for these assessments. We resolved discrepancies through discussion.

DATA SYNTHESIS AND ANALYSIS:

We ascertained whether the design of each included trial and whether investigators randomized at the participant or eye level. We estimated the difference in means (“mean difference”) for comparison of continuous outcomes. We calculated summary risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

We calculated the I2 statistic (%) to determine the proportion of variation in outcomes due to statistical heterogeneity; we considered a value >50% to suggest substantial heterogeneity.24 We compared outcomes for topical pharmacologic interventions vs inactive controls (placebo, artificial tears, saline, vehicle, or no treatment), for topical pharmacologic treatments vs active controls, and for steroid-containing treatments vs inactive controls. We performed meta-analysis when clinical and methodological heterogeneity was acceptable. When <3 studies were included in an analysis, we used a fixed-effect model as advised in the Cochrane Handbook.29 We explored comparisons between treatments and controls and by subgroups to explain observed heterogeneity in effect estimates from individual studies whenever sufficient data (≥2 studies in ≥1 subgroup) were available. When the quality of the available data from a study prevented meaningful analysis, we omitted the study from quantitative analyses and reported the data in a narrative format. All analyses were performed in RevMan Web.27 We also assessed the certainty of evidence for each review outcome following the GRADE approach.30 A priori, we judged the design of each included study to confer a high certainty of evidence and downgraded certainty to moderate, low, or very low when there was evidence of high risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, or imprecision.

RESULTS

The electronic searches yielded 4981 records, of which we screened 4166 after removing duplicates (Figure 1). We obtained and screened 101 full-text reports that were potentially relevant. We included in this review 10 RCTs, published in 14 reports. Another 4 trials (6 records) were either “ongoing” (in progress) or “awaiting classification” pending receipt of additional information from the trial investigators or sponsors.

DESCRIPTION OF INCLUDED STUDIES:

All included studies were single-center studies, conducted in Asia (n = 4), the Middle East (n = 3), Europe (n = 2), and Africa (n = 1). The 10 studies enrolled 892 participants and reported outcomes for 834 (93.5%) participants; a median number of 71 (interquartile range [IQR] 28-82) participants were enrolled per study. All 10 included studies were parallel-group RCTs: in 6 RCTs, 1 eye of each participant was treated; in the other 4, both eyes of some or all participants were assigned to the same intervention (Table 1). We analyzed data from studies that had assessed outcomes in both eyes separately in the absence of information on intraperson correlation. No trial was judged to have low risk of bias across all criteria (Figure 2). We judged 6 of the 10 trials to have had unclear or high-risk of bias in ≥5 of the 6 domains assessed. Reporting bias (selective reporting bias) was the domain for which we judged the largest number of trials to have been deficient.

Patients purely in the acute phase were recruited in 5 trials,31-35 in which patients were followed over time for SEI development and resolution. Three trials recruited only patients with existing SEIs of varying duration, ranging from “fresh”36 to 2 to 4 weeks.37,38 Two trials recruited patients with SEIs and with acute EKC.39,40

COMPARATIVE ANALYSES:

Control treatments were divided into 2 pharmaceutical categories—artificial tears (or saline) and steroid (or steroid-containing agents). We compared any treatment vs artificial tears or saline in comparison 1, which included PVP-I 5% vs saline,31 PVP-I 0.6% vs hyaluronate tears,39 ganciclovir 0.15% ointment vs nonpreserved artificial tears,34 and a combination of topical steroid and neomycin vs polyvinylpyrrolidone artificial tears. We compared any treatment with steroid-based control treatment in comparison 2, which included tacrolimus 0.03% ointment vs dexamethasone 0.05% ointment,37 CsA 0.5% vs fluorometholone (FML) 0.1%,38 CsA 0.05% vs loteprednol 0.5%,36 and a combination of FML 0.1% and PVA-I (at a concentration equivalent to PVP-I 0.055%) vs a combination of FML 0.1% and levofloxacin 1.5%.33 The investigators of one of the 3-arm trials compared PVP-I 1.0% plus dexamethasone 0.1% drops with dexamethasone 0.1% drops or hypromellose artificial tears (hydroxypropyl methylcellulose 0.3%),32 all administered 4 times per day. In the other 3-arm trial, the comparisons were of trifluridine 1% vs dexamethasone 0.5% vs artificial tears (PVA 1.4% w/v).35 We included comparative data from these 2 trials in comparisons 1 and 2, separately.

EFFECTIVENESS AND SAFETY OF INTERVENTION:

Comparison 1: Any treatment vs artificial tears or saline

Six trials contributed data to comparisons with artificial tears or saline for ≥1 outcomes of interest (Table 2). In terms of resolution of signs or symptoms, 2 trials compared trifluridine 1%35 or ganciclovir 0.15%34 with artificial tears but reported inconsistent findings: Ward and associates35 found no differences in mean duration of time with symptoms/signs using trifluridine whereas Tabbara and Jarade35 found a significantly shorter duration of conjunctivitis in the ganciclovir arm.34 We did not combine the results because the antiviral mechanisms are different and significant heterogeneity was detected between the effect estimates (I2 = 96%, P < .01).

The pooled RR from 2 other studies estimated that a 15% higher proportion of participants had symptom resolution by 10 days using PVP-I 5%31 or PVP-I 0.6%39 than artificial tears or saline (RR 1.15 [95% CI 1.07-1.24]). In addition, PVP-I–treated participants had resolution of signs after 7 to 10 days of treatment (RR 3.19 [95% CI 2.29-4.45]) 3 times more often than those in the control group. Although Kovalyuk and associates33 similarly reported 8-fold more eyes with symptom resolution by 7 days of treatment (RR 9.00 [95% CI 1.23-66.05])32, we did not combine this result with those of the 2 PVP-I trials31,39 because of statistical evidence that supported a potential difference in the patient characteristics, pharmacological mechanism, or in the study design and outcome measurements of participant-level vs eye-level (I2 = 75.4%, P = .04).

Four studies reported the proportion of participants or eyes in which SEI developed within a month of treatment;32,34,39,40 1 additional study that compared PVP-I 5% with saline assessed this outcome at 3 months.31 The combined estimated RR from 2 trials reporting participant-level data suggested a 76% reduction (RR 0.24 [95% CI 0.01-0.56]) in SEI development within 30 days of treatment with PVP-I 0.6% or ganciclovir 0.15% compared with artificial tears (Table 2).34,39 A post hoc sensitivity analysis that combined trials by treatment, rather than by treatment duration, found a participant-level beneficial effect of PVP-I on SEI development (Figure 3).31,39

In 2 trials that enrolled participants with SEI and examined resolution of SEI after 21 days of treatment,39,40 the CI was consistent with no difference between artificial tears and PVP-I 0.6%39 or dexamethasone 0.1% plus neomycin40 in SEI resolution (RR 1.34 [95% CI 0.88-2.04]).

Adverse effects

Severe discomfort of interventions and elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) were 2 reported adverse effects. There was no evidence of a difference between trifluridine 1%35 or dexamethasone 0.1% plus neomycin40 vs artificial tears for either outcome. None of the 6 trials had measured or reported outcomes related to viral eradication.

Comparison 2: Any treatment vs a steroid-containing treatment

Six studies compared a topical intervention with a steroid alone or a steroid-containing control treatment (Table 2). Two studies offered inconsistent results regarding symptom/sign recovery within 7 to 8 days: PVP-I 1.0% plus dexamethasone 0.1% resulting in 9 times more eyes than dexamethasone 0.1% alone (RR 9.00 [95% CI 1.23-66.05])32 and PVA-I plus FML 1.0% being no different from levofloxacin 1.5% plus FML 1.0% in resolving signs (RR 1.62 [95% CI 0.32-8.18]).33

The combined RR from 2 studies suggested that PVP-I 1% plus dexamethasone 0.1%32 or PVA-I plus FML 0.1%33 had reduced the development of SEI within 21 days by 92% compared with dexamethasone 0.1% or FML 0.1% plus levofloxacin (RR 0.08 [95% CI 0.01-0.55]), with a similar reduction (88%) at day 30 (RR 0.12 [95% CI 0.02-0.65]).

Two groups of investigators evaluated the effects of CsA on resolution of SEI of varying duration at the time of treatment initiation.36,38 Single-study estimates were consistent with no treatment effect in facilitating SEI disappearance after adequate treatment.36 The CI of the pooled estimates from 3 trials (RR 1.04 [95% CI 0.94-1.14]) reporting the outcome of rebound SEI when treatment was discontinued at 3 months36 or 6 to 7 months37,38 was consistent with no difference between tacrolimus or CsA and steroid in preventing SEI rebound (Table 2).

Adverse effects

Severe treatment intolerance (burning, redness, foreign body sensation) was >4 times as common with CsA 0.5% or tacrolimus 0.03% as with artificial tears (RR 4.64 [95% CI 1.15-18.71]).37,38 Two trials monitored for adverse events of high IOP after treatment initiation,35,37 with 1 trial observing no incidents of IOP elevation35 and the other no significant differences in incidence of high IOP between the 2 comparison arms.37

DISCUSSION

In this review of 10 randomized controlled trials in which a total of 892 participants with EKC were treated and followed, we sought to compare the effectiveness of topical treatments relative to either artificial tears or steroid-containing agents in outcomes indicative of ameliorating the acute signs and symptoms of EKC and preventing and treating its sequelae, including SEIs and the spread of EKC to the fellow eye. Because EKC is marked by a delayed, chronic clinical phase evident as SEIs and other complications, we also compared treatments for patients with SEIs in terms of SEI resolution and lack of rebound after treatment cessation. To the best of our knowledge, this systematic review is the only review to date of outcomes from RCTs that exclusively enrolled patients with EKC, in contrast with narrative reviews of studies that enrolled patients with all types of adenoviral conjunctivitis presenting with a wide range of severity or included observational studies.41,42 The current review revealed that PVP-I was favored over artificial tears in resolving the signs and symptoms of acute EKC as well as lowering the risk of SEI development. Either PVP-I or PVA-I combined with steroid was favored over steroids in lowering the risk of development of SEI in patients with acute EKC. In patients enrolled with SEIs, treatments to eradicate SEIs and prevent rebound, however, were no different from either tears or steroids. Clinical and methodological diversity in studies, such as method of measuring outcomes, time at which participants were recruited and assessments were made, treatment duration and regimen of various concentrations (eg, of PVP-I and steroids) as well as inadequate sample sizes and high risk of bias limit the reliability of estimates of quantitative comparisons of effects between interventions and controls and thus the certainty of evidence for most outcomes. Greater methodologic rigor should be incorporated in the design and conduct of future comparative studies.

PVP-I and PVA-I (used in Japan) are inexpensive ophthalmic antiseptic agents with a low adverse effect profile other than mild irritation. The single report of ganciclovir shortening the clinical course of conjunctivitis and reducing the risk of SEI development compared with artificial tears came from a small study (n = 18 subjects) with findings summarized only in a meeting abstract.34 The available literature on ganciclovir and adenovirus has been limited to in vitro studies, animal studies,43 and human clinical trials that enrolled patients without EKC44-47 or clinical trials of unknown status.48 Ganciclovir would not be expected to be effective against adenovirus given that it needs to be phosphorylated by a viral kinase to the active monophosphate form and adenoviral genomes do not encode a kinase.

In all studies included in this systematic review, EKC was a clinical diagnosis at study entry. Few studies provided information regarding the time between onset of infection (gauged by the emergence of signs or symptoms) and allocation to a treatment arm. Therefore, in most studies, the length of this period was unclear. Treatment responses likely varied among participants enrolled at different phases of the clinical course of EKC (eg, “fresh” cases of SEI vs those with long-standing SEI;36-38 patients with acute EKC and patients with SEIs in the same trial),39,40 making evidence not only difficult to compare across studies but also resulting in possibly misleading comparisons and effect estimates. Future investigators should strive to reduce the variability of the length of time between onset of infection and study enrollment. Clinical acumen, improved diagnostic testing, and education of the public about EKC would help facilitate recruitment and enrollment in future clinical trials of participants at the earliest sign or symptom to reduce baseline variability.49,50 Unless or until that capability becomes widely available, an optimal time origin for aligning participants across studies of patients with acute EKC might be the onset of keratitis signs and symptoms, which typically include red eye, tearing, foreign body sensation, pain or discomfort.51 Treatments for SEI may be best analyzed if trials enroll patients in the acute phase and follow them for development of SEIs, rather than patients with SEIs of variable duration or a combination of patients with acute phase EKC and patients with SEIs. Adenoviral typing is not widely available but may be associated with disease severity and additionally may influence response to treatment. Investigators also may choose to assess outcomes at time points other than 7 days and 21 days, which were chosen for this review based on consideration of work furloughs related to worker infectivity and typical duration of EKC symptoms.

Although the results of active (treatment)-controlled trials and placebo-controlled trials may be quite different, in this review we did not identify any true “placebo”-controlled trials, ie, a placebo being identical to the active test intervention but lacking activity. Rather, steroids or artificial tears are commonly considered the standard of care for patients with acute EKC, especially as the latter alleviates symptoms of irritation and discharge without risk of adverse effects and may be considered an effective control.52 Evidence generated from this review may represent actual clinical practice more closely than that from any placebo-controlled study. The concentration of intervention medications such as PVP-I, CsA, and steroids (as well as type of steroids) varied widely, which also may have contributed to high heterogeneity in a few subgroups. Investigation into any dose-dependent effect of active comparators may be important in future reviews.

Several studies included in our review had implemented scoring systems for symptoms or signs of conjunctivitis32 or of SEI,37 none of which had been validated. Development of standardized scoring systems would allow valid comparisons of interventions and also promote more nuanced outcome measures than resolution of signs and symptoms and disappearance of SEI. In addition, study authors assessed outcomes at time points often different from those we had specified in the review protocol. Planned subgroup analyses to explore effects of different types of therapies were not feasible for all review outcomes because few trials reported the same outcomes or measured and reported them in the same manner.

In conclusion, because EKC can have major economic repercussions from loss of work53 or school days and can cause chronic visual disturbance, shortening the duration of signs and symptoms of the acute phase of EKC during which patients can transmit EKC to the fellow eye and to other people should be the primary outcome of interest in clinical trials of topical pharmacologic interventions for EKC. In this review, we found evidence of very low certainty that ganciclovir 0.15% or PVP-I had achieved these clinical benefits more so than artificial tears. The evidence that PVP-I or PVA-I compared with steroid had resulted in development of SEI in a much smaller proportion of participants with acute EKC is of very low certainty, but eradication of SEI once present was not different with either artificial tears or (long-term) steroid use. Furthermore, some therapies have been associated with adverse effects. Therefore, prevention of SEIs in patients with acute EKC may be more important than the eradication of SEIs. Certainty of evidence to determine best treatment(s) for EKC would be improved by rigorous design of future trials, reporting of all outcomes assessed, analysis of data appropriate to trial design, better diagnostic capability (thus providing some uniformity in pretherapeutic windows), agreement on outcome measures important to patients and physicians, and standardization and validation of a scoring system for signs and symptoms of acute EKC and SEI. Costs of therapy, evidence of adenoviral eradication within a specified window of time after treatment initiation, and risk of infecting the fellow eye—none of which was described in any study included in this systematic review—also should be assessed and reported in future trials.
