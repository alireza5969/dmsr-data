Abstract

Background:

Data heterogeneity and methodological errors hinder the ability to draw clinically meaningful conclusions from studies using the BREAST-Q Reconstruction Module patient-reported outcome measure (PROM). In this systematic review we evaluate the quality of BREAST-Q Reconstruction Module administration in relation to the BREAST-Q Version 2.0 User’s Guide and the reporting of key methodology characteristics. Secondarily, we describe a framework for improving the quality of BREAST-Q data analysis and reporting.

Methods:

We conducted a systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Ovid HAPI databases to identify articles on the BREAST-Q Reconstruction Module to assess post-mastectomy breast reconstruction outcomes. We registered the protocol prior to study implementation on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/c5236) and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. Data on mode of BREAST-Q administration, time horizon justification, and sample size calculation were collected.

Results:

We included 185 studies in the analysis. Errors in BREAST-Q administration were identified in 36 (19.5%) studies. Appropriate administration of the BREAST-Q could not be determined in 63 (34.1%) studies due to insufficient reporting. Time horizon for the primary outcome was reported in 71 (38.4%) studies, with only 17 (9.2%) reporting a sample size calculation.

Conclusions:

We identified important yet actionable shortcomings in the BREAST-Q literature. Researchers are encouraged to review the BREAST-Q User’s Guide in the design phase to mitigate errors in PROM administration and reporting for future trials using the BREAST-Q Reconstruction Module. Adhering to these guidelines will allow for greater clinical utility and generalizability of BREAST-Q research.

INTRODUCTION

The BREAST-Q Reconstruction Module is a validated, reliable, and responsive patient-reported outcome measure (PROM).1, 2 Developed in 2009, it has become the gold-standard PROM for evaluating patient satisfaction and health-related quality of life in breast reconstruction patients. The BREAST-Q has been recommended by the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) to be a standard-of-care PROM for breast cancer patients,3 and the FDA has approved the BREAST-Q as a Medical Device Development Tool.4 As a research instrument, the BREAST-Q has been used to investigate the impact of a variety of patient and surgical characteristics, including comorbidities, adjuvant therapies, reconstruction type, and reconstruction timing, on short- and long-term patient-reported outcomes.5–8 If properly applied and interpreted, BREAST-Q research findings can significantly improve current guidelines and clinical practice in breast reconstruction by incorporating the patient perspective.

Despite the large volume of the BREAST-Q literature for breast reconstruction, recent systematic reviews reveal the challenges of drawing clinically impactful conclusions based on available research data.9–15 Liu et al. recently noted that 40 of the 54 studies analyzed were weaker level III or level IV evidence (Oxford Evidence-Based Medicine Levels of Evidence), indicating that most of the BREAST-Q literature had methodological weaknesses, such as a lack of longitudinal analysis, retrospective design, inadequate control for confounders, and low response rates.9 In addition, few systematic reviews on breast reconstruction were able to conduct meta-analyses of BREAST-Q outcomes studies, with the largest meta-analysis including BREAST-Q data from only 10 studies.14 While a large number of studies were initially included for review, high risk of bias, high heterogeneity, or inadequate data reporting eventually eliminated most included studies before analysis.10–15 These issues significantly limit our ability as a field to appropriately interpret patient-reported outcomes, to synthesize BREAST-Q data from multiple studies, and to apply this information to improve patient care.

There is a clear need for standardized methodology when performing and reporting BREAST-Q studies, such that study data can be aggregated and applied to clinical practice. In this systematic review, we examine the characteristics of existing BREAST-Q research implementation for post-mastectomy breast reconstruction in an effort to identify methodological gaps that can weaken conclusions drawn from these data. Specifically, the primary objective of this systematic review was to evaluate the quality of BREAST-Q PROM administration in relation to the published BREAST-Q Version 2.0 User’s Guide.16 The secondary objective examined the reporting of key methodology characteristics in the included studies, such as outcome time horizon, sample size calculation, response rates, and funding source reporting.

METHODS

In accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, we conducted a systematic review of studies that utilized the BREAST-Q to evaluate outcomes following post-mastectomy breast reconstruction. Our protocol, which includes our search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and data extraction form, was registered prior to study implementation on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/c5236).

Search Strategy

A medical librarian constructed a systematic electronic search strategy for published studies of post-mastectomy breast reconstruction patients that used BREAST-Q as an outcome measure. We searched the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Ovid HAPI databases (see Appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 1, for exact search terms and criteria) on May 22, 2020, and we used the Covidence systematic review management software (Covidence.org, Melbourne, Australia) to import and manage identified studies.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Publications were included if 1) any scale of the BREAST-Q Reconstruction Module was used as an outcome measure, 2) the study population included post-mastectomy breast reconstruction patients, 3) the study was published in 2009 (the year the BREAST-Q was developed and made available) or later, and 4) the study design was a randomized controlled trial, cohort study, case-control study, cross-sectional study, case series with more than 10 patients, or economic analysis. Of note, studies that involved breast-conserving therapy patients but not post-mastectomy breast reconstruction patients were not included in this review. Publications were excluded if they were 1) a letter to the editor, abstracts or conference proceedings, cadaver study, animal study, or systematic review, or 2) articles written in a language other than English.

Study Screening and Data Extraction

Two reviewers (J.C. and L.G.) independently and in duplicate screened each study by title and abstract based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any conflicting decisions were discussed by the two reviewers, with two other reviewers (J.N. and S.V.) available for resolution of any remaining conflicts. The same two reviewers (J.C. and L.G.) then reviewed the full text of every article independently; any conflicting decisions were discussed and resolved by consensus between the two reviewers. Any remaining conflicts were reviewed with J.N. and S.V. and consensus was achieved through discussion. A standard data extraction form was created using Microsoft Forms (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA) to assess the methodological characteristics of each study. Data extraction was initially performed independently and in duplicate on approximately 10% of studies until consistency was achieved. The remaining data extraction was performed independently with data checking. All data extraction was performed by J.C. and L.G.

Outcomes

The BREAST-Q Reconstruction Module measures satisfaction and health-related quality-of-life for post-mastectomy breast reconstruction patients.1 This module has six main scales: Satisfaction with Breast, Satisfaction with Care, Satisfaction with Outcome, Physical Well-being, Psychosocial Well-being, and Sexual Well-being. Additional scales and subscales in the module include Adverse Effects of Radiation, Satisfaction with Implants, and Satisfaction with Information, among others. According to the BREAST-Q Version 2.0 User’s Guide (qportfolio.org/breast-q),16 each scale of the BREAST-Q Reconstruction Module is administered and scored independently. There is no overall BREAST-Q score; each BREAST-Q scale provides an independent score. After the Likert-scale questions in each scale are scored, the raw summed score is converted into a 0–100 Q-score using the Q-Score software. For a scale to be scored, a patient must independently answer at least 50% of the questions either on paper or electronically. No questions may be changed, added, or removed, as this will nullify the psychometric properties of the BREAST-Q.

The primary outcome of this systematic review was to identify common errors in BREAST-Q administration in relation to the BREAST-Q User’s Guide.16 This was reported as a dichotomous outcome, and reasons for suboptimal administration were recorded. Studies that did not provide any information on how the BREAST-Q was administered were labelled “insufficient reporting of methodology.”

Secondary outcomes included an evaluation of whether studies reported the outcome time horizon and provided a rationale or justification for any reported time horizon. Time horizons were defined as the pre-specified time point at which study outcomes are assessed, in this case when the BREAST-Q scale(s) was administered.17 Other secondary outcomes were the reporting of a priori sample size calculations, response rates, and funding sources. As this systematic review evaluated the reporting and methodology characteristics of included studies rather than trial outcomes, assessments of risk of bias within or across included studies was not performed. No subgroup analyses were performed.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data collected. All data analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.0.2, readxl, tidyverse) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.2, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

RESULTS

A PRISMA flow diagram of the search and selection process is shown in Figure 1. Of the 751 titles and abstracts initially screened, 185 articles were included for final analysis (Appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 1). The majority (n=160 [86.5%]) of BREAST-Q articles were published after 2015 (Figure 2). Most of the BREAST-Q articles included in this analysis were either cross-sectional (35.1%) or cohort studies (prospective: 29.2%, retrospective: 29.2%) (Figure 3).

Table 1 shows BREAST-Q specific methodology characteristics. We found errors in BREAST-Q administration or insufficient reporting of administration methods in over half of the studies analyzed (n=99 [53.5%]). Of studies with errors in BREAST-Q administration (n=36), the most common reasons for improper administration included the addition, deletion, or alteration of scale items (n=8 [22.2%]),18–25 presenting raw scores rather than the converted Q-score (n=14 [38.9%]),26–39 averaging individual scales into one score (n=6 [16.7%]),20, 34, 40–43 and patients not completing the BREAST-Q themselves directly (i.e. completed through a telephone interview or with help from caregivers or interpreters) (n=7 [19.4%]).44–50

Most studies did not provide a time horizon (n=114 [61.6%]) for their primary outcome. Of the 71 articles that referenced a time horizon, only three (4.2%) justified their selection.51–53 Approximately one-third of the 185 included articles (n=69 [37.3%]) did not report a funding source nor explicitly state that “no funding was received.” Most studies (n=168 [90.8%]) did not report a sample size calculation. Only 11 (5.9%) of the included studies described a sample size calculation well enough for it to be reproducible.7, 42, 54–61 These methodology characteristics are presented in Table 2. Finally, 41.6% (n=108) of studies did not report their response rate for BREAST-Q administration (Figure 4). Of studies reporting a response rate, response rates ranged from 23% to 100%.

DISCUSSION

As the most utilized and published PROM in breast reconstruction over the last decade,62 it is imperative to critically examine the BREAST-Q literature to identify potential areas for improvement in methodology. The results of our systematic review highlight key areas for improvement in the existing BREAST-Q literature, specifically BREAST-Q administration, time horizon reporting, and sample size calculation (Figure 5).

The systematic review and meta-analysis is one of the highest levels of evidence, synthesizing data from multiple independent studies, increasing the sample size and providing a precise estimate of the effect of an intervention. However, the results are dependent on the quality of the data input: i.e. the included studies. While the field of breast reconstruction research has expanded and embraced patient-reported outcomes, particularly the BREAST-Q, our examination of the BREAST-Q literature identified gaps in three specific and critical methodologic areas within BREAST-Q research. It is vital that BREAST-Q administration and BREAST-Q study data reporting be of sufficient quality to allow researchers to pool results in the form of a systematic review and meta-analysis to be able to inform clinical practice guidelines and improve patient care.

Following the BREAST-Q User’s Guide

If changes are made to BREAST-Q scales, such as adding or changing items or averaging scores across scales, the validity of this PROM and any subsequent analyses can no longer be assured. In our study, we either identified errors in BREAST-Q administration or could not confirm appropriate BREAST-Q administration in over half of studies. The most common errors involved not transforming raw scores into BREAST-Q scores using the Q-Score and adding, removing, or altering questions within a scale.

It is crucial to convert the raw questionnaire data into summary scores for each BREAST-Q scale. This can easily be done, and the Q-Score program is provided free of charge on the BREAST-Q website (http://qportfolio.org/score-breast-q-breast-cancer-2/). Converting to Q-Score is critical as it allows the ordinal-level data to become linearized by means of item calibrations.1, 63 It is impossible to interpret study findings and compare study results with other BREAST-Q–based research if the Q-Score conversion is not undertaken.

The usefulness of the BREAST-Q in evaluating post-mastectomy breast reconstruction outcomes comes from its rigorous developmental methodology and psychometric validation in breast reconstruction patients.1, 2, 64 If the content of the BREAST-Q is altered through changing the wording of a question or adding or removing items to a scale, the psychometric properties of the scale are nullified. Additionally, data from these adapted versions of the BREAST-Q cannot be compared to or aggregated with findings from other BREAST-Q studies.

We encourage researchers to adhere to the instructions in the BREAST-Q Version 2.0 User’s Guide (http://qportfolio.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/BREAST-Q-USERS-GUIDE-V2.pdf) for the appropriate use and scoring of the BREAST-Q to ensure the validity of their study results.16 We also encourage electronic administration of the tool, which has been shown to improve data quality.64 Importantly, BREAST-Q outcome measures should be completed by the patient alone to avoid interview bias, which may artificially inflate scores.64–66 For clinical practices with a large proportion of non-English speakers, surgeons may request translations of the BREAST-Q through qportfolio.org; at present, there are translations for the BREAST-Q available in approximately 50 languages.

We also recognize that there is room for the BREAST-Q to grow in terms of its implementation, and this may explain some of the common administration errors identified in this study. Future work on the BREAST-Q can focus on improving its usability in administration and scoring, especially for clinical practice. More can also be done in terms of its interpretation, such that BREAST-Q scores have clinical meaning and can be used to guide clinical decision making. Incorporation of these changes can improve research quality and the utility of the BREAST-Q as a clinical tool.

Presenting an Appropriate Time Horizon

Time horizons are pre-determined time points at which outcomes are assessed for the study population. In plastic surgery, choosing appropriate time horizons is crucial as outcomes often take time to stabilize; unclear time horizons may lead to inaccurate assessment of outcomes or inappropriate outcome comparisons between study groups.17 Our systematic review identified a particularly low proportion of studies reporting a time horizon, with only 38.4% of studies provided a time horizon for their BREAST-Q administration, of which only 4.2% provided a rationale for their selection. The chosen time horizon has important implications for the interpretation of study results. Furthermore, failure to provide a time horizon increases heterogeneity when attempting to pool study results in a meta-analysis.17 Moreover, the results of these observational studies cannot be appropriately applied in clinical practice if readers cannot apply the stated results in the appropriate context.17 This is especially important for BREAST-Q studies as previous longitudinal analysis has demonstrated significant changes in BREAST-Q scores over time.6 Accurate interpretation and comparison of BREAST-Q findings, therefore, rely on implementing and reporting time horizons. In an ideal study, even one with a retrospective design, cohorts would have a similar period of examination or follow-up to reduce the potential for time-related biases.67

We recommend investigators justify their choice of time horizon in the methods section of their manuscript.51–53 When designing a study, researchers should review the relevant literature and obtain expert opinion on the intervention’s anticipated recovery time and outcome stabilization to identify an appropriate time horizon. A detailed 2-stage approach to choosing an appropriate time horizon has been proposed by Mowakket et al.17

Calculating the Sample Size Prior to Study Implementation

Our systematic review of the BREAST-Q literature noted a paucity of studies reporting a sample size calculation. Sample size calculations ensure a study is adequately powered to detect a difference between two populations.18 An underpowered study, with an insufficient sample size, is likely to overestimate the treatment effect to demonstrate a statistically significant result. As such, a primary statistical analysis may demonstrate a statistically non-significant result simply due to an inadequate sample size.19 Conversely, overpowered studies waste resources and can be deemed unethical if they are prospective in nature.19,20 By presenting a sample size calculation, readers can be confident that the study was sufficiently powered to detect a true difference between groups. For BREAST-Q scores, a minimal important difference (MID) of four points per scale has been established,68 although this is likely a conservative estimate. At Memorial Sloan Kettering, we now utilize this MID value, along with the standard deviation previously established in our breast reconstruction population, to determine adequate sample size for our BREAST-Q studies. Future investigators should make use of the BREAST-Q MID to perform appropriate sample size calculations before commencing their study.7, 55, 69

Limitations

Firstly, we present a focused analysis of articles using the BREAST-Q Reconstruction Module, which limits that extrapolation of the results of this study to other BREAST-Q modules (i.e. augmentation and reduction/mastopexy). However, studies using other BREAST-Q modules will benefit from adherence to the published guidelines16 on the appropriate use and scoring of the BREAST-Q. Secondly, we did not conduct a search of the grey literature; therefore, these results do not account for the unpublished literature. Lastly, the authors acknowledge that this analysis does not evaluate other measures of article quality, such as “reporting quality” and “risk of bias,” that should be considered by readers when performing a critical appraisal of the published literature.

CONCLUSION

The BREAST-Q continues to be increasingly employed in outcomes research; however, there are some limitations in the existing post-mastectomy reconstruction literature that reports BREAST-Q data. Fortunately, many of these limitations have actionable solutions. Future research utilizing the BREAST-Q should focus on improving methodology to address issues of BREAST-Q administration, time horizon reporting and justification, and sample size calculation to ensure that the collected and presented data are psychometrically valid, generalizable, and adequately powered. The findings from this systematic review can be used to strengthen future post-mastectomy breast reconstruction study design, thus allowing for greater clinical utility and generalizability of BREAST-Q research.
