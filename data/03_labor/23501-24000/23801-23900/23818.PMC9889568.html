Interventions to Reduce Hospital Readmissions in Older African Americans: A Systematic Review of Studies Including African American Patients

Sanjay Bhandari[1][2]

Aprill Z. Dawson[1][2]

Zacory Kobylarz[1]

Rebekah J. Walker[1][2]

Leonard E. Egede[1][2]

[1] Department of Medicine, Division of General Internal Medicine, Medical College of Wisconsin, 8701 Watertown Plank Rd, Milwaukee, WI 53226, USA

[2] Center for Advancing Population Science, Medical College of Wisconsin, 8701 Watertown Plank Rd, Milwaukee, WI 53226, USA

Author notes:

Correspondence to: [✉] Leonard E. Egede legede@mcw.edu

Abstract

Objectives

This systematic review aims to summarize interventions that effectively reduced hospital readmission rates for African Americans (AAs) aged 65 and older.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines were followed for this review. Studies were identified by searching PubMed for clinical trials on reducing hospital readmission among older patients published between 1 January 1990 and 31 January 2020. Eligibility criteria for the included studies were mean or median age ≥ 65 years, AAs included in the study, randomized clinical trial or quasi-experimental design, presence of an intervention, and hospital readmission as an outcome.

Results

There were 5270 articles identified and 11 were included in the final review based on eligibility criteria. The majority of studies were conducted in academic centers, were multi-center trials, and included over 200 patients, and 6–90% of participants were older AAs. The length of intervention ranged from 1 week to over a year, with readmission assessed between 30 days and 1 year. Four studies which reported interventions that significantly reduced readmissions included both inpatient (e.g., discharge planning prior to discharge) and outpatient care components (e.g., follow-ups after discharge), and the majority used a multifaceted approach.

Conclusion

Findings from the review suggest successful interventions to reduce readmissions among AAs aged 65 and older should include inpatient and outpatient care components at a minimum. This systematic review showed limited evidence of interventions successfully decreasing readmission in older AAs, suggesting a need for research in the area to reduce readmission disparities and improve overall health.

Introduction

The older adult US population is rapidly growing. Adults aged 65 and older made up 16% of the population (52.4 million) in 2018 and are projected to grow to 21.6% of the population by 2040 [1]. Older adults experience several health challenges such as increased healthcare needs due to a higher burden of chronic diseases including diabetes and heart disease [2]. Eighty percent of older adults have at least one chronic disease, and about 70% have two or more chronic conditions [3]. Older adults also utilize a relatively large volume of health care services due to their increased healthcare needs, with US adults aged 65 or older representing 45% of the top 10% of healthcare utilizers [4]. In addition, healthcare expenditures among older adults are disproportionately higher than their younger counterparts [5]. While national health expenditures are projected to increase from 17.7% in 2018 to 19.7% of gross domestic product in 2028 with an annual rate of over 5%, Medicare is expected to experience the fastest spending growth of 7.6%5. Due to the growing population of older adults, higher prevalence of comorbidities, and increased healthcare utilization and expenditures, older adults pose a high burden on the healthcare system [6].

About 17% of US adults aged 65 and older are hospitalized annually with at least one overnight hospital stay, compared to the national average of 7.4% [7]. About 5.5% of older adults are hospitalized two or more times, compared to 1.2% of adults aged 18–44 years and 2.7% of adults aged 45–64 years [7]. Hospital readmission is associated with poor patient outcomes (i.e., increased length of stay, mortality) and costs Medicare about $26 billion annually [8–10]. It has garnered significant attention in recent times as a possible target for reform and has been linked to Medicare reimbursement since the Hospital Readmission Reduction Program (HRRP) was introduced [11]. Under HRRP, hospitals with higher 30-day readmission rates for HRRP-targeted disease conditions (heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pneumonia, coronary artery bypass graft, and elective primary total hip arthroplasty, and/or total knee arthroplasty) are financially penalized by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) [11]. Therefore, reducing readmissions among older adults is significant and will not only result in improved clinical outcomes such as quality of care but will also help to reduce healthcare system costs.

Evidence suggests there are racial differences in readmission rates, with older African Americans having higher rates of hospital readmissions compared to their White counterparts [12–15]. For example, a 2020 study including a cohort of over 30,000 patients from 2010 to 2018 found a significantly higher percent of African Americans (29.4%) experienced either a heart failure (HF) readmission or death within 30 days of hospital discharge compared to Whites (23.0%) [16]. An additional study of more than 270,000 adult patients with diabetes found African Americans had a significantly higher risk of 30-day readmission than members of other racial/ethnic groups [17]. While overall readmission rates have decreased over time with the implementation of HRRP [18], racial differences in readmission persist [12, 19–22]. Reducing the rate of readmission for ethnic minorities remains a priority set by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) [10]. Several risk factors for readmission and interventions designed to reduce readmission have been described in literature. Risk factors include acuity of admission (emergency versus elective), length of hospital stay, comorbidities, and biological markers such as hemoglobin and sodium levels [23–25]. Commonly described interventions to reduce readmissions include medication reconciliation, patient education, scheduling appointments before discharge, self-management, patient-centered discharge instructions, timely follow-up, home visits, and post-discharge telephone follow-ups [26–30]. However, there remains a paucity of evidence focused on reducing readmissions in older African Americans and effective strategies for this high-risk group. Therefore, to address this gap in literature, we conducted a systematic review to identify successful interventions to reduce hospital readmissions among older African Americans.

Methods

Data Sources, Search Strategy, and Eligibility Criteria

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed for this review [31]. The literature search conducted in the PubMed database included articles published between 1 January 1990 and 31 January 2020, focusing on available literature in the last two decades. The initial literature search was conducted on May 27, 2021. The PICOS (participants, interventions, comparisons, outcomes, study design) approach was followed for describing eligibility criteria for studies included in the review (Moher et al. 2009). Inclusion criteria was as follows: Participants: (1) Mean or median age of study participants was 65 years and older, (2) study included participants identified as African American or Black; interventions: (1) any intervention designed to reduce readmissions, including interventions occurring prior to discharge, post-discharge, or interventions bridging the transition from inpatient to outpatient care; comparisons: (1) control, (2) usual care, (3) other interventions; outcomes: (1) hospital readmission, not limited to any specific time-frame; study design: (1) clinical trials (randomized controlled or quasi-experimental). Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) no inclusion of African American/Black participants, (2) obstetric/gynecological, psychiatric or cancer diagnosis, as the focus of the review focus was on internal medicine patients with common medical diagnosis, (3) interventions using drugs, devices, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, brachytherapy, or surgery, and (4) observational studies (cross-sectional, prospective, or retrospective cohort, case–control).

A reproducible search strategy was used to identify studies based on the prespecified PICOS eligibility criteria. Search terms were identified based on search strategies used in previous systematic reviews for older adults [32–34], hospital readmissions [29, 30, 35], and African Americans [36–38]. Older adults were defined as a person aged 65 years and older [39]. A hospital readmission was defined as an admission in the hospital occurring within a specified time frame after discharge from the index hospitalization [40]. Table 1 shows search terms and structure of the search strategy used to identify manuscripts for the review.

Study Selection and Data Collection

The sequence of steps used to identify eligible articles is shown in Fig. 1. Titles and abstracts were reviewed to ensure they met the eligibility criteria. Articles meeting eligibility criteria, including those with titles and abstracts insufficient to make an eligibility determination, proceeded to full-text review. For example, if the titles and abstract did not include details about the participants’ race or did not clearly exclude African Americans, the full text of the article was reviewed. Full-text reviews were completed by two independent reviewers (SB, AZD) which on further assessment for eligibility were advanced to be included for final analysis. Disagreements were resolved by consensus after discussion among the reviewers.

The following data was collected as the studies were being reviewed: author, year, state, type of location (i.e., hospital, skilled nursing facility, etc.), population description, percentage of AAs, mean or median age of participants (years), number of participants, details of intervention, settings (outpatient, inpatient), follow-up period, personnel involved, results (significant, non-significant), and readmission timeframes. Quality of the included manuscripts was assessed by two independent reviewers (SB, AZD) using National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute’s (NHLBI) study quality assessment tool for controlled intervention studies [41].

Results

Study Selection

The initial database search produced 5270 studies (Fig. 1). After abstract and title screening, 5147 studies were excluded, and remaining 123 studies were assessed for eligibility via full-text screening. Of the 123 studies, 11 studies met eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative review. Ten studies were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and 1 was quasi-experimental in design [42–52].

Study Characteristics

All studies were US-based, with 3 occurring in the West (California), 1 in the Southwest (Texas), 2 in Southeast (Georgia, North Carolina), 1 in Northeast (Pennsylvania), and 2 in Midwest (Missouri, Michigan); 2 studies were based in more than 1 region (Table 2). Majority of studies took place in academic centers, were multi-center trials and included over 200 patients. Six studies [42, 44, 46, 49, 50, 52] included patients with heart failure, whereas remaining 5 studies [43, 45, 47, 48, 51] were not disease-focused. None of the studies exclusively included African American participants; the percentages of African American participants ranged from 6 [42] to 90% [48]. Of the 11 studies included in the review, one study included 90% African American patients [48], two studies included more than 50% African American patients [50, 52], three studies included 25–50% African American patients [43, 46, 47], four studies included 20–25% African American patients [44, 45, 49, 51], and one study included 6% African American patients [42].

The mean or median age of patients in the studies ranged from 66 to 80 years of age (Table 2). While the majority of studies report age as part of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, there were a number of studies that did not report this information and either only provided a mean or in a few cases the age range of patients included in the study. Of the 11 studies included in the final review, almost half of the studies reported being aged 65 and older [46, 47, 52] or aged 70 and older [45] as part of the inclusion criteria. Three studies enrolled patients who were aged 60 and older [43, 48, 50], and one study enrolled patients aged 50 and older. Lastly, there were two studies that enrolled patients with a diagnosis of heart failure, a condition with a significantly higher prevalence among older adults compared to younger adults, that did not report age inclusion criteria [42, 44]. The mean ages and standard deviations for each study were 72 + 11 and 67.9 + 12.6 [42, 44]; and 83% of adults in the intervention group and 86% of adults in the usual care group were aged 60 and older in the study by DeBusk et al. [42].

Results from the study quality assessment were as follows: six studies were scored as good quality (i.e., adequate method of randomization, concealment of treatment allocation, use of intention-to-treat analysis, etc.) [42–44, 46, 50, 51], four studies as fair quality (i.e., had lower adherence to treatment, higher drop-out at the end-point, etc.) [45, 47, 49, 52], and one study as poor quality (i.e., method of randomization not adequate, study subjects and providers not blinded, sample size not enough to detect the difference, etc.) [48].

Interventions to Reduce Hospital Readmissions

The length of interventions in the studies ranged from 1 week to more than 1 year; three studies had interventions lasting one to more than or equal to two weeks [43, 45, 48, 51], two studies had interventions lasting 3 months [47, 50], one study had an intervention that lasted 1 month [46], three studies had interventions lasting 6 months [44, 49, 52], and one study had an intervention that lasted for more than 1 year [42] (Table 3). A variety of health care professionals (nurses [42, 43, 48, 49, 52], therapists [50, 51], care coordinators [45], pharmacists [45], or nurse practitioners [43–47]) delivered the interventions (Table 3).

Follow-up visits were held as early as 2 days to 1 year post-intervention; and the outcome of the hospital readmission was assessed between 30 days and 1 year (Table 3).

Most of the interventions included both inpatient and outpatient care components (Table 3). The intervention used in the study by Soran et al. [52] was based on home monitoring device which would detect any change in heart failure signs and symptoms and transmit information to the primary physician. Two studies [50, 51] used intervention based on rehabilitation, aimed to address functional impairment in frail patients (Table 3). The remaining studies used interventions that targeted parts or entireties of multi-dimensional components, which included counseling/educating, medication reconciliation, discharge planning, making follow-up appointments, telephone follow-ups, home visits, assessing and addressing needs on follow-ups, and coordinating with the outside providers (Tables 3 and 4). Three studies included home-visits as a part of post-discharge follow-up planning [46–48], and all studies, excluding two [50, 51], included follow-up telephone calls as a part of intervention (Tables 3 and 4).

Majority of the studies [42–50] included both inpatient (e.g., medication reconciliation, making follow-up appointments, patient education) and outpatient components (e.g., telephone follow-ups, home visits) (Table 4). Of the 11 studies included in the review, one study included 90% African American patients [48], two studies included more than 50% African American patients [50, 52], three studies included 25–50% African American patients [43, 46, 47], four studies included 20–25% African American patients [44, 45, 49, 51], and one study included 6% African American patients [42].

Interestingly, the study that included 90% African American patients did not find significant differences in hospital readmissions between the intervention and control groups [48]. The intervention used for this study provided patients with medication self-management assistance, a patient-centered medical record maintained by the patients to facilitate information transfer from the inpatient to outpatient setting and a list of signs and symptoms of worsening condition along with instructions on what to do if their condition worsens [48]. Lastly, telephone calls instead of home visits were provided for patients with caregiver support and those who demonstrated engagement based on a subjective assessment completed by the interventionist. Given the known challenges faced by many African Americans with literacy, health literacy, and increased social risk factors, it may have been important to provide home visits to all study participants instead of those identified by the care coaches as having difficulty with medication management and poor caregiver support.

Of the two studies that included more than 50% African American patients, only one had statistically significant differences in hospital readmissions [50]. The intervention used in the study by Reeves et al. [50] included the provision of a tailored exercise prescription that was adapted to individual functional deficits in four domains (balance, strength, mobility, endurance) and administered by trained interventionist using specific milestones for progression. The individualized, tailored approach appears to have been beneficial in meeting the individual needs of all patients including ethnic minorities [50].

Two of the three studies that included 25–50% African American patients had statistically significant results [46, 47]. The intervention in the study by Naylor et al. [46] included advanced practice nurse (APN)-initiated discharge planning and home follow-up protocol. The follow-up procedures included APN visits every 2 days in the inpatient setting, at least two home APN visits, and APN 24/7 telephone availability [46]. Similarly, in the second study by Naylor et al. [47], the intervention included APNs discharge planning and home follow‐up protocol. Once again, the APNs visited the patient during the index hospitalization, APNs conducted at least 8 home visits, and were available via telephone 24 h a day, 7 days a week [47]. In both studies, patients and their caregivers were provided with a comprehensive discharge plan and home follow-up protocol that was designed for high-risk older adults; in addition, the APN in collaboration with the patient’s physician was able to individualize patient management within the confines of the protocol [46, 47].

The intervention described in the study by Koehler et al. [45] was delivered by individuals with advanced nursing certifications and a targeted and individualized care bundle with information on specific health conditions, emphasizing home self-care and contingency plans if problems arose. Clinical pharmacists that were also part of the team focused on medication reconciliation and education [45].

Unfortunately, only one study reported conducting subgroup analyses by race/ethnicity and age [49]. In the study by Ong et al., results overall showed there was no statistically significant difference in 180-day all cause readmission; and there was not a statistically significant difference by the following subgroups: race/ethnicity, age, sex, New York Heart Association classification.

While four of the 11 studies reported a significant reduction in readmission rates and included both in-hospital and after-hospital care components, some differences existed among these studies. First, patient population varied by study with two studies including patients with heart failure [47, 50], a study with patients hospitalized for various medical or surgical reasons [46], and a study with patients with common medical diagnoses such as heart failure, intracranial hemorrhage, or cerebral infarction, pneumonia, septicemia, renal failure, etc. [45]. Second, only one study included a majority (56%) of African American participants [50]; the percentage of African Americans included in other studies ranged from 19.5 to 45% [45–47]. Third, intervention components varied across all studies and ranged from a multi-domain physical rehabilitation in frail patients with HF [50] to discharge planning [45–47], medication reconciliation [45], patient education [45], and follow-ups after discharge, including telephone follow-ups [45–47] and home visits [46, 47]. Fourth, the length of intervention ranged from 1 week [41] to 3 months [43] post discharge from the index hospitalization. Finally, although the health personnel involved in the reported interventions primarily included advanced nurse practitioners [45–47], care-coordinators [45], clinical pharmacists [45], exercise physiologists [50], and physical therapists [50], there was support and collaboration from different individuals such as outside providers, reflecting the need of multidisciplinary teamwork [53]. Since none of these studies exclusively focused on or performed subgroup analysis on older African Americans, it is difficult to isolate or identify successful interventions that primarily targeted older African Americans.

Discussion

Results from this review showed four out of eleven interventions had statistically significant reductions in readmission rates. All interventions resulting in reductions in readmission included both inpatient (discharge planning prior to discharge) and outpatient care components (follow-ups post discharge), and the majority used a multifaceted approach (medication reconciliation, patient education, home visits, etc.). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to summarize available evidence on the success of interventions for older African American patients in reducing hospital readmissions, and findings show there remains limited evidence on interventions focused on older African Americans.

Overall, studies that included moderate to high (20–56%) percentages of African Americans enrolled and had statistically significantly lower rates of readmission overwhelmingly used individualized, tailored approaches to meet the individual needs of study participants and their caregivers and were delivered by advanced practice providers or individuals with advancing nursing certifications [45–47, 50]. While studies included diverse populations, we believe these findings from studies that included moderate to large percentages of older African Americans on interventions that successfully reduced readmissions among older are important to share and consider from both clinical and research perspectives. Three of the four studies with statistically significant results included the following components as part of the intervention [45–47]: (1) multidisciplinary care team that included a pharmacist and a care coordinator with advanced nursing certification at a minimum), (2) intervention delivery initiated during the index hospitalization and carried out for a minimum of 1-week post discharge; (3) patient-centered health education specific to the individual’s condition, with information on signs and symptoms requiring medical attention; (4) individualized care plan along with continuity of care and care coordination that included medication reconciliation, ordering or confirmation of necessary medication and medical supplies. The fourth study that describes an intervention to address physical limitations in older adults with heart failure used an intervention that was initiated post-discharge, included home-visits, and much like the other efficacious interventions, individualized exercise prescriptions tailored to meet the needs of each patient [50].

There are a few noteworthy implications of this review. First, there were varying proportions of African American participants across the studies; therefore, it is difficult to speak to the efficacy of interventions developed solely for older African Americans. While the interventions mentioned in the studies can possibly be applied to an older African American population, there is a need of studies focused on older African Americans due to disproportionately higher rates of readmissions, high number of comorbidities, lower socioeconomic status, and more clinically complex circumstances warranting more focused care for this population compared to their White counterparts [54–56]. Secondly, majority of the studies were focused on patients with heart failure. The studies did not particularly focus on other diseases which are known to sustain higher readmission rates with worse hospital outcomes, including various HRRP (acute myocardial infarction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pneumonia, coronary artery bypass graft, and elective primary total hip arthroplasty and/or total knee arthroplasty in addition to heart failure) [11] and other non-HRRP-targeted conditions such as diabetes [17] and renal disease [57, 58]. Thirdly, none of the studies addressed social risk or cultural factors. Social risk factors form an important target for interventions aimed at improving health outcomes in disadvantaged populations [10]. Since social risk factors are linked to higher risk of readmission [59, 60], and form an important area to address among African Americans, future research should consider incorporating addressing social risk factors in strategies to reduce higher readmission rates among older African Americans. Strategies might include connecting older African Americans with community-based resources on housing and food security, transportation, employment, financial support, and encouraging social support through community connections10. Furthermore, evidence suggests cultural factors play a role in reducing healthcare disparities and readmission and promoting self-care [59, 61–63]. Therefore, older African Americans might benefit from culturally tailored interventions that account for cultural norms and beliefs. Finally, the future interventions potentially need to incorporate both inpatient and outpatient components (e.g., discharge planning, medication reconciliation, patient education, post-discharge follow-ups such as telephone calls and home visits) with the support from a multidisciplinary team. The component which will contribute to the largest reduction in readmission risk and appropriate “dose” of intervention (days, weeks, or months) for older African Americans is something the future trials need to elicit.

There are several limitations to our study, many of which stem from the diversity of interventions and the lack of trials conducted with our target population. First, since most of the tested interventions included multifaceted components, we cannot determine which individual component or groups of components were drivers of the reduction in readmission. Second, the majority of the studies had less than 50% older African Americans, so the real impact of interventions on African Americans cannot be ascertained. Third, the lack of homogeneity of follow-ups (as early as 2 days to 1 year), length of intervention (a week to over a year), and timeframe of assessment of readmission outcome (30 days to 1 year) limit the concrete comparisons between these studies. Fourth, it should also be noted that the studies included in this review also assessed other outcomes such as functional recovery, costs, patient satisfaction, mortality, or quality of life. Therefore, some of the reported interventions might have demonstrated effectiveness for African Americans in terms of other outcomes, which were not reported in our review.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this systematic review found four out of eleven clinical trials in which older African Americans participated were effective in reducing readmissions. Characteristics of successful interventions incorporated both inpatient and outpatient components including inpatient discharge planning, medication reconciliation, patient education, and follow-ups after discharge. There is limited evidence from randomized clinical trials on reducing hospital readmissions which are primarily focused on older African Americans. Additional research is needed to explore focused interventions reducing higher readmission rates among this vulnerable population which might include, but not limited to, incorporating the need for addressing social risks and cultural tailoring of interventions.
