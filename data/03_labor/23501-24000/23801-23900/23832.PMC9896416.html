Pharmacology Management in Improving Exercise Capacity of Patients with Fontan Circulation: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

Abstract

Background: The Fontan procedure is currently the mainstay therapy for single functional ventricles. However, with prolonged follow-up duration, various complications have been observed that seriously influence the quality of life of patients.

Objectives: The aim of this meta-analysis is to compare the effectiveness of pharmacologic agents in improving exercise capacity in patients with Fontan circulation.

Methods: This meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) statement and has been registered in the International Prospective Register for Systematic Reviews database with registration no. 282352. Quality assessments of the included studies were assessed using the Strengthening Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Results: Twelve studies met the predetermined inclusion criteria and were included in this meta-analysis. This meta-analysis found that treatment with bosentan significantly improved New York Heart Association Functional Class (NYHA FC) in Fontan patients (standard mean difference -0.59, 95% CI -0.94 – -0.25; P=0.0008; I2 = 1%). However, the use of bosentan (P=0.66) and sildenafil (P=0.13) did not show a significant improvement in the maximum rate of oxygen consumption (VO2 max).

Conclusion: This meta-analysis shows that people with Fontan circulation may benefit from using bosentan as it decreases postexercise heart rate and improves NYHA FC and 6-minute walking test results. Therefore, indirectly improving exercise capacity. Nonetheless, considerable work is required to strengthen our knowledge in improving the exercise capacity of Fontan patients.

INTRODUCTION

1

The Fontan procedure is currently the mainstay therapy for single functional ventricles, where systemic and pulmonary circulations are placed in series to guarantee blood flow into the lungs passively. Many studies have shown that the Fontan procedure has a promising short-term efficacy, and it has been confirmed as a safe and effective surgical procedure. Since the development of the Fontan technique, the 10-year survival rate of postsurgery has risen from only 50% and to its current rate of 90% [1-3].

However, with prolonged follow-up duration, many complications have been observed that seriously influence the quality of life of patients.

Compromised cardiac output, elevated central venous pressures, and loss of pulsatile pulmonary blood flow all contribute to the reduction of exercise capacity. There is subtle evidence that drugs that improve myocardial performance and decrease pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) could improve the long-term outcome of Fontan patients as well as optimize circulatory efficiency. However, there are currently no studies that compare the efficacy of these various pharmacologic agents in improving the exercise capacity of Fontan patients [4, 5]. Thus, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to evaluate the efficacy of these different pharmacologic agents in improving various parameters that reflect on the exercise capacity of Fontan patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

2

Search Strategy

2.1

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement [6]. We searched 11 electronic databases, which include PubMed, Cochrane, MEDLINE, ProQuest, EBSCO, Science Direct, Springer, BMJ, Lancet, PloS One, and Google Scholar, with a combination of keywords and MeSH terms as follows: Fontan, bosentan, sildenafil, vasodilator, vasodilating agent, and exercise capacity. Studies were searched from the date of their inception to October 31, 2020, with no restrictions on language. Hand searching of the bibliographies from the included studies was also performed.

Study Criteria

2.2

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) cohort study or clinical trial (randomized or nonrandomized), (ii) participants underwent a Fontan operation, and (iii) participants used a vasodilating agent as the intervention. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) participants treated with any non-pharmacological therapy, (ii) case reports, reviews, editorials, and (iii) irretrievable full-text articles.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

2.3

Three authors (BM, C, and MS) screened the titles and abstracts for all searches independently and excluded all irrelevant articles. The full texts of the selected articles were then retrieved and read independently. Extraction of data included the first author, year of publication, study design, study location, the total number of participants, participants’ baseline characteristics (including gender, type of Fontan circulation, ventricular morphology, age at administration, weight, Body Mass Index (BMI) or Body Surface Area (BSA), a drug used as the intervention, type of tests used to assess exercise capacity, duration of follow up, the dosage and mode of administration of the drug, and the results of each test performed (New York Heart Association functional class (NYHA FC) assessment, 6-minute walk distance test (6 MWD), and cardiopulmonary exercise testing), which included: pulmonary vascular resistance, heart rate, blood pressure, rate of oxygen consumption (VO2), maximum rate of oxygen consumption (VO2 max), oxygen saturation (SpO2), as well as any adverse events noted during each study. Risks of bias assessments of the included studies were assessed independently by three authors (BM, C, and MS) according to the STROBE statement [7]. All disagreements were resolved by discussion between the authors.

Statistical Analysis

2.4

Statistical analyses were performed using REVMAN (version 5.4; Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK). Standardized mean difference (SMD) scores were the primary summary measure, allowing for comparison of the effect sizes of sildenafil and bosentan across NYHA, VO2, and SpO2 measures. All meta-analyses were conducted using a random-effects model, such as variation in the true effect size between studies caused by clinical (for example, CHD complexity, type of Fontan procedure, comorbidities) or methodological (such as the design of the study, risk of bias) factors. For each reporting method, separate analyses were then performed for sildenafil and bosentan, for each parameter: NYHA, VO2, and SpO2. For articles in which the number of control participants was not reported, a conservative approach assuming the number was equal to that of the patient group was used.

We used both the eyeball test and the I2 statistic to assess the heterogeneity of the studies included in the meta-analysis. The I2 statistic was used as an estimate of the percentage of total between-study variance, with I2 more than or equal to 50%, indicating substantial heterogeneity. For all statistical analyses, significance was set at p<0.05. Additional predictors not assessed in the meta-analyses were captured using narrative synthesis.

RESULTS

3

Search Results

3.1

The PRISMA flow diagram for the screening and selection process of the included studies in this systematic review and meta-analysis is shown in Fig. (1). The initial systematic searches yielded 6514 potential studies from all databases explored, from which 6412 studies were excluded for irrelevant titles. One hundred and two studies were screened for authenticity and duplication, leaving 17 studies. All 17 abstracts were screened, and full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Finally, all 17 studies which fulfilled the criteria for inclusion were included in the qualitative synthesis, while 12 studies were included in the quantitative analysis or meta-analysis.

Study Characteristics

3.2

This systematic review and meta-analysis included single and multi-centre studies published in 2009-2017 with study designs of prospective or retrospective cohorts, randomized or nonrandomized, controlled or uncontrolled trials. The included studies were Italy, the United Kingdom, the United States of America, Denmark, Belgia, China, Germany, and Japan. The total number of participants in the included studies was 408 patients who went through Fontan surgery of various types: atriopulmonary connection, intracardiac (lateral tunnel), extracardiac total cavopulmonary connection, right atrium-pulmonary artery connection, left atrium-left pulmonary artery connection, right ventricle-pulmonary artery connection, and right atrium-right ventricle connection. Single ventricular morphologies of the patients include tricuspid atresia, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, hypoplastic right heart syndrome, mixed ventricular morphology, double-inlet of right or left ventricle, double-outlet of right or left ventricle, D-transposition of the great artery (TGA), TGA with pulmonary stenosis, straddling atrioventricular valve, unbalanced atrioventricular septal defect and pulmonary atresia with the intact ventricular septum. In most studies, the patients underwent exercise test procedures such as NYHA FC assessment, 6MWD, cardiopulmonary exercise test (upright cycle ergometry, supine bicycle exercise, electronically braked cycle ergometer), modified Bruce protocol, and treadmill exercise, except in one study by Giordano et al. (2015) [8]. Detailed characteristics of the included studies and patients were summarized in Table 1.

The included studies evaluated the outcomes of Fontan patients with pharmacological management. Seven studies administered bosentan orally with different doses. Three studies administered bosentan starting from 62.5 mg and uptitrated to 125 mg twice a day [9-11], one study administered 125 mg bosentan twice a day [12], one study administered 0.5 mg/kg followed by 1 mg/kg bosentan twice a day [13], and one study administered bosentan according to patients’ body weight starting from 7.8125 mg (≤10 kg) to 62.5 mg (>30 kg) twice a day, where the dose was then doubled [14]. Five studies administered sildenafil orally and one study administered sildenafil orally as well as using a nasogastric tube (NGT). Two studies administered single doses of 50 mg of sildenafil [15, 16], one study administered sildenafil at a single dose of 0.7 mg/kg body weight (25-50 mg) [17], one study administered 0.5 mg/kg of sildenafil and increased to 1-2 mg/kg [18], one study administered 20 mg sildenafil three times a day, continued by washing out, and then crossed over to placebo [19], and one study administered 0.35 mg/kg sildenafil through NGT and then orally every 4 hours [8]. Other drugs used in the included studies were tadalafil, iloprost, nitric oxide, and ambrisentan. Tadalafil was administered orally at the dose of 1 mg/kg (max 40 mg) daily for 6 weeks [20]. One study administered 2.5 micrograms of iloprost using a standard nebulizer [21]. Nitric oxide was continuously supplied through a face mask with a supplemental room airflow of 5 litres per minute for 8-10 minutes [22]. Ambrisentan was administered orally with a dose of 5 mg daily [23]. Fig. (2) shows the quality assessment of the included studies. In our meta-analysis, the studies were homogenous based on the overlapping confidence interval in the eyeball test as well as the low values of the I2 statistics (0-1%).

Post-exercise Outcome

3.3

Seventeen studies reported postexercise outcomes before and after the administration of bosentan, sildenafil, tadalafil, iloprost, nitric oxide, and ambrisentan (Table 2). Post-exercise outcomes reported in the studies include NYHA FC assessment, 6MWD, heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), VO2, VO2 max, and SpO2. NYHA FC improved significantly after the administration of bosentan (5 studies; standard mean difference -0.59, 95% CI -0.94 – -0.25; P=0.0008; I2 = 1%; Fig. (3a) [9-11, 13, 14]. Administration of sildenafil and tadalafil also improved NYHA FC significantly in Mori et al. (2016) and Sabri et al. (2016), respectively [18, 20]. 6MWD improved after the administration of bosentan and the changes were significant in Derk et al. (2014) [10]. The difference in the result of the 6MWD between the bosentan group and the control group was also significant in Shang et al. (2016) [14]. However, Ovaert et al. (2009) reported decreased 6MWD after administration of bosentan [24]. Mori et al. (2016) reported an increase in 6MWD results after administration of sildenafil [18].

All studies using drugs other than bosentan reported higher postexercise HR after administration of drugs. Only one study by Rhodes et al. (2013) reported a significant decrease in postexercise HR between the intervention group receiving iloprost and the control group [21]. Studies using bosentan mostly reported lower postexercise HR after administration of bosentan, except in the adolescent group of Agnoletti et al. (2017) [13]. The differences between SBP and DBP in all studies were not significant between the intervention and control groups before and after administration of the drug. VO2 was increased significantly after the administration of bosentan in Agnoletti et al. (2017) in both children and adolescent groups [13]. VO2 max also increased after administration of bosentan (4 studies; standard mean difference 0.07, 95% CI -0.25 – 0.40; P=0.66; I2 = 0%; Fig. (3b) [9, 11, 12, 24] and sildenafil (3 studies; standard mean difference 0.24, 95% CI -0.07 – 0.55; P=0.13; I2 = 0%; Fig. (4a) [15, 17, 19], although both changes were not significant. Administration of bosentan increased postexercise SpO2 in Derk et al. (2014) and Ovaert et al. (2009) [10, 24]. On the contrary, SpO2 in Agnoletti et al. (2017) decreased after the administration of bosentan in the children group [13]. Post-exercise SpO2 increased after administration of sildenafil (5 studies; standard mean difference 0.11, 95% CI -0.14 – 0.37; P=0.39; I2 = 0%; Fig. (4b) [15-19]. There were no significant changes in postexercise SpO2 after administration of the remaining drugs.

Resting Outcome

3.4

Nine studies compared the outcomes of Fontan patients at rest before and after administration of bosentan, sildenafil, tadalafil, or nitric oxide [8, 13, 15-17, 19, 20, 22, 24]. The endpoints reported in these studies were HR, SBP, DBP, and SpO2, which are shown in Table 3. HR endpoints in all studies were not significantly different before and after administration of drugs in both the treatment and control groups. SBP was lower after administration of bosentan in both children and adolescents in the study by Agnoletti et al. (2017), but it was significant only in the adolescent group [13]. Patients in a study conducted by Giardini et al. (2008) also had significantly lower SBP after treatment compared to before administration of sildenafil, while there were no significant changes in control group [17]. SBP was significantly lower after sildenafil administration in the study by Hager et al. (2014) [15]. However, there was no significant difference before and after administration of sildenafil in both the treatment and control groups. Administration of tadalafil also lowered SBP significantly in the study by Sabri et al. (2016) [20], but SBP was higher after administration of nitric oxide in the study by Latus et al. (2016) [22] even though it was not significant.

There were no significant changes in DBP before and after the administration of bosentan. On the other hand, the administration of sildenafil lowered DBP significantly in the study by Giardini et al. (2008) [17], while no significant change was found in the study by Goldberg et al. (2011) [19]. Sabri et al. (2016) and Latus et al. (2016) reported no significant difference in DBP after administration of tadalafil and nitric oxide, respectively [20, 22]. Administration of bosentan in the studies by Agnoletti et al. (2017) and Ovaert et al. (2009) showed lower and higher SpO2, respectively, although both changes were not significant [13, 24]. Tadalafil and nitric oxide increased SpO2 in the studies conducted by Sabri et al. (2016) and Latus et al. (2016), but the increases were not significant [20, 22]. SpO2 changes were not significant in all studies, but most studies reported an increase of SpO2 after the administration of bosentan, sildenafil, tadalafil, or nitric oxide, except Agnoletti et al. (2017), who reported lower SpO2 after administration of bosentan [13].

Adverse Events

3.5

Adverse events reported in bosentan studies were headache, fatigue, flushing, palpitations, peripheral edema, dyspnea on exertion, and anemia with leukocytopenia. Adverse events found in a study conducted by Bowater et al. (2012) were headachess in three patients that resolved fully by the first month of follow-up and mild leg edema in one patient that resolved within the first month of treatment [9]. Schuuring et al. (2013) also reported headaches in three patients and peripheral edema in one patient. One patient experienced dyspnea on exertion and another patient had anemia with leukocytopenia [12]. Two patients experienced mild transient fatigue in a study by Ovaert et al. (2009) [24], while one patient also experienced fatigue and another patient experienced fatigue and palpitation in a study by Derk et al. (2014) [10]. Flushing was experienced by six patients in the study by Hebert et al. (2015) [11]. There was no adverse event experienced by the patients in the control group and in both treatment and control groups in the study by Shang et al. (2016) [14].

Adverse events reported in sildenafil studies were mostly headaches which were experienced by one patient in the study by Giardini et al. (2008) [17], and nine patients in the treatment group of the study by Goldberg et al. (2011) [19]. Goldberg et al. (2011) also reported other adverse events in the treatment group, such as flushing (5), dizziness (2), nausea/vomiting (2), abdominal pain (1), kidney stones (1), photosensitivity (1), rash (10), and other events (11) [19]. Dizziness was experienced by one patient in a study by Mori et al. (2016). Two studies reported no adverse events in patients receiving sildenafil [8,16].

There were some adverse events experienced by patients receiving tadalafil in the study by Sabri et al. (2016), such as headache (7), transient priapism (10), and leg pain (8), and back pain (5) [20]. Ten patients receiving iloprost in the study by Rhodes et al. (2013) experienced mild throat/chest discomfort [21]. Cedars et al. (2016) reported fatigue (1), diarrhea (1), and abdominal pain (1) in patients receiving ambrisentan [23]. There were no adverse events reported from nitric oxide administration in the study by Latus et al. (2016) [22].

DISCUSSION

4

Pharmacology Agents in Patients with Fontan Circulation

4.1

Sildenafil, a potent phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) inhibitor, is primarily used for treating erectile dysfunction. However, its role in treating pulmonary hypertension is growing. PDE5 degrades cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP). High concentrations of cGMP are found in the corpora cavernosum and the pulmonary arteries [25]. Patients with Fontan circulation show dysfunction of the endothelium, where there is decreased nitric oxide (NO) production [13]. Normally, NO stimulates guanylate cyclase in the cell, which results in increased production of cGMP, causing the relaxation of smooth muscle in the arteries. Sildenafil inhibits PDE5, the predominant phosphodiesterase in the lungs, thus inhibiting the degradation of cGMP, making it work longer. Widening of the arteries in the lungs will normalize pulmonary vascular resistance and therefore improve cardiac output, oxygen consumption, and exercise capacity. For the treatment of pulmonary hypertension, sildenafil is given orally at a dosage of 5-20 mg thrice daily, where a dosage above the maximum dose is not proven to be of higher efficacy [26].

Adverse events reported with the use of sildenafil include headache, flushing, dyspepsia, and nasal congestion [27]. However, these side effects were mild and mostly well-tolerated by Fontan patients [17-19]. In an open-label study consisting of 27 patients randomized to a single oral dose of sildenafil or no treatment, sildenafil improved pulmonary blood flow at rest and peak exercise, leading to a 9% increase in VO2 max. Hager et al. (2014) reported an acute increase in exercise capacity following a single dose of 50 mg sildenafil [15]. A similar result was reported by Giardini et al. (2008), where both studies have increased VO2 max by 9.4±5.2% and 5.1±8.0% respectively [17]. Nonetheless, our meta-analysis showed no favorable significance of sildenafil in terms of improving VO2 max in patients with Fontan circulation (p=0.13, 95% CI). Patients with Fontan circulation possess subtle pulmonary vascular changes that could not be reliably detected in regular evaluations, which may partially explain these results. Another reason is that patients in worse conditions might benefit more from sildenafil, whereas patients with optimal conditions could hardly profit. This may explain the findings of the study by Goldberg et al. (2011), which failed to show a significant effect on peak exercise capacity since they included a relatively healthy cohort patients without significant complications or comorbidities [19]. Thus, we speculate that sildenafil benefits those with diseased pulmonary vasculature, and not all Fontan patients are affected by these subtle pulmonary vascular changes.

Tadalafil, like sildenafil, is a competitive inhibitor of PDE5. The main difference between sildenafil and tadalafil is the long half-life of tadalafil (17.5 hours), which allows for once-daily dosing (while sildenafil requires thrice-daily dosing) [28]. A study by Forgue et al. (2005) showed that tadalafil given to healthy patients orally in single doses of 10 mg and 20 mg was well tolerated without any serious adverse events. The most reported adverse events were headache, back pain and myalgia [29]. In a study by Sabri et al. (2016), tadalafil was given to subjects orally at a dosage of 1 mg/kg (with a maximum dose of 40 mg) daily for 6 weeks. Even though the dosages differ in both these studies, no serious side effects were reported as well, where the common side effects reported were leg pain, back pain, headache and transient priapism [20]. This indicates that tadalafil is mostly well tolerated and safe to use with minimal side effects.

Bosentan is a nonselective endothelin receptor antagonist and is currently approved for treating pulmonary arterial hypertension. Endothelin-1 (ET-1) is a pulmonary vasoconstrictor, in which its receptors (endothelin A and endothelin B) are found to be overexpressed in the pulmonary vasculature of Fontan patients, where the binding between the two causes hypertrophy and vasoconstriction of the smooth muscles, as well as inflammation and fibrosis of the pulmonary vasculature. The blockade of these receptors aims to balance the upregulation of ET-1 and, therefore, lower PVR [9, 30]. Studies have shown that an oral dose of 125 mg twice daily is as effective as a higher dose of 250 mg with fewer side effects [31, 32]. Adverse events associated with the use of bosentan include headaches, increased liver enzymes, flushing, leg edema, anemia, and fatigue [9-12, 24, 30, 32]. Even though these side effects were well tolerated, there were concerns about using bosentan for Fontan patients, as their high systemic venous pressure puts them at risk of hepatic dysfunction [9]. However, studies have shown that the increase in liver enzymes was not significant [9-11]. Even so, patients treated with bosentan should still have their liver function monitored monthly [32].

In a study by Agnoletti et al. (2017), all patients treated with bosentan showed an improvement in NYHA FC [13]. Bowater et al. (2012) prospectively investigated the efficacy of bosentan in improving the NYHA FC of Fontan patients with NYHA FC > II and found that 6 patients showed improvement in NYHA FC from functional class III to II [9]. As Fontan circulation does not have a ventricular are pulmonary pump, blood flow through the pulmonary vasculature is passive. Therefore, both a transpulmonary gradient and low PVR are essential in maintaining adequate ventricular preload and cardiac output, which in turn, increases exercise tolerance. Our meta-analysis showed that only Bowater et al. (2012) and Hebert et al. (2015) showed a significant change in NYHA FC (p=0.0008 for overall effect) after bosentan administration [9, 11].

Ambrisentan, like bosentan, is a potent endothelin receptor antagonist. The difference between the two lies in their selectivity towards endothelin receptors, where bosentan nonselectively blocks both endothelin A and endothelin B receptors, while ambrisentan selectively blocks endothelin A. Endothelin A receptors are found in high concentrations in the pulmonary vasculature smooth muscles and have a selective affinity towards ET-1 [33]. Ambrisentan has a half-life of 15 hours, which allows for once-daily dosing, and is rapidly absorbed into the bloodstream. A dose of 5-10 mg of oral ambrisentan is generally well tolerated, where commonly reported adverse events were consequences of systemic vasodilation, which include headache, peripheral edema, anemia, and nasal congestion [34]. As an example of its use in Fontan patients, a dose of 10 mg ambrisentan showed no difference in the rate of adverse events when compared to placebo [23].

Iloprost is an analog of prostacyclin, which has vasodilatory and antithrombotic effects. Prostacyclin stimulates adenylate cyclase, which generates 3′,5′-adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), which leads to the inhibition of thrombocyte aggregation. Increased intracellular cAMP inhibits myosin kinase, which causes vasorelaxation [35]. Iloprost is administered via inhalation using a nebulizer (as enteral or intravenous administration lacks pulmonary selectivity and causes various systemic side effects), with a dose of 5 μg. Due to its selectivity, it significantly reduces pulmonary arterial pressure without reducing systemic arterial pressure. Thus, it is currently used for treating patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension. Common side effects include headache, jaw pain, syncope, flush, and cough [36]. A study by Rhodes et al. (2013) where iloprost was given to patients with Fontan circulation also reported mild throat/chest discomfort [21].

Like all other vascular beds, NO plays a role in the regulation of resistance in the pulmonary vasculature. NO stimulates guanylate cyclase to form cGMP, which then activates cGMP-dependent kinases and myosin phosphate, leading to a release of calcium and relaxation of the pulmonary vasculature [37]. When inhaled, NO shows a pulmonary selectivity for reducing PVR without systemic effects, which may be explained by its short half-life (3-30 seconds) where NO only dilates the vessels surrounding the alveoli [38]. Treatment with NO generally starts at 5-20 ppm as it is reported that 2-80 ppm is the range of the effective dose for pulmonary vasodilation. The dosage is increased to 80 ppm if no reaction is observed [39]. Latus et al. (2016), in a study involving patients with Fontan circulation, administered NO by continuous inhalation of 40 ppm through a face mask with an airflow at 5 l/min for 8-10 minutes [22]. Frostel et al. also found that the inhalation of NO at 40 ppm selectively reduces pulmonary resistance [40]. The major clinical issue with NO is its potential to rapidly react with oxygen to form nitrogen dioxide, a pulmonary irritant that may cause pulmonary edema [41]. The reactivity between NO and hemoglobin (Hb) also poses a problem, where the binding of both forms nitrosyl-Hb, which rapidly oxidizes into met-Hb, resulting in tissue hypoxia [42]. However, the toxicity of inhaled NO is dose-dependent, where a low dose of NO appears to be safe without side effects [22, 38, 39, 42]. Thus, it is important to administer NO at its lowest effective dose and monitor nitrogen dioxide levels to minimize its toxicity.

Exercise Parameters in Fontan Circulation

4.2

6MWD is the distance that a patient can quickly walkthrough on a flat, hard surface in 6 minutes, which represents the level of functional exercise for daily physical activity of the patient [43]. Derk et al. (2014) reported that 6MWD improved by a mean of 73 m after administration of bosentan in Fontan patients [10]. Shang et al. (2016) also reported higher 6MWD results in the bosentan group compared to the control group during a 2 year follow-up [14]. Improvement of 6MWD was also found in Bowater et al. (2012), although statistical significance was not reached [9]. On the contrary, Ovaert et al. (2009) found that 6MWD decreased after administration of bosentan [24]. These different results may result from the small sample size, and the different baselines of the included Fontan patients. Despite these different results, most studies reported an improvement of 6MWD after administration of bosentan, which may improve the exercise capacity of Fontan patients. Interestingly, Derk et al. (2014) found that there was a greater improvement of 6MWD in patients with worse NYHA functional classes, but the reason for this finding is unknown and should be explored further in future studies [10].

Cardiac output augmentation in normal individuals is achieved by increasing HR and stroke volume, which in turn is determined by increased contractility and preload. On the contrary, exercise-induced increase in cardiac output is primarily achieved by increasing HR. Claessen et al. (2019) reported that the HR increase in Fontan patients ceases at a lower peak exercise value, suggesting chronotropic limitation secondary to inadequate filling of the systemic ventricle and inappropriate contraction of the ventricle, and impaired cardiac output augmentation. Thus, the reduced heart rate reserve (HRR) and the difference between peak exercise and resting HR may be the consequence of impaired exercise hemodynamics and not a primary electrical phenomenon. Claessen et al. (2019) also showed that an increased HR during exercise lowers cardiac output, resulting in reduced exercise performance, hypotension, and death. Therefore, it is believed that drugs resulting in relative bradycardia during exercise may improve exercise capacity by enhancing diastolic filling [44].

Although the changes were not significant, administration of bosentan in the included studies decreased postexercise HR, except in the adolescent group of Agnoletti et al. (2017), which showed higher post-exercise HR [13]. Therefore, bosentan may be able to increase exercise capacity by decreasing post-exercise HR in Fontan patients. Rhodes et al. (2013) also reported significantly lower postexercise HR in the iloprost group compared to the control group. This effect may also increase exercise capacity in Fontan patients after administration of iloprost [21].

There was no significant change in postexercise SBP and DBP before and after administration of drugs. However, administration of bosentan resulted in significantly lower resting SBP in Agnoletti et al. (2017) and Giardini et al (2008) [13, 17]. Agnoletti et al. (2017) reported lower resting SBP in both children and adolescents, which was only significant in the adolescent group [13]. Administration of sildenafil in Hager et al (2014) and tadalafil in Sabri et al. (2016) also showed a significant decrease in resting SBP [15, 20]. It was suggested that this effect may contribute to increased exercise capacity in Fontan patients as these patients have increased systemic vascular resistance after Fontan operation and low cardiac output. However, previous studies with systemic vasodilators showed no effect on exercise capacity in Fontan patients [45, 46].

LIMITATION

Most of the studies included in this meta-analysis had no placebo control group and are cross-over studies. Since patients with Fontan circulation are rare, the number of patients in this meta-analysis is still very low for the number of variables and limits the robustness of the study. Secondly, even if all measurements were taken during the baseline evaluation, we could not rule out the possible role of associated comorbidities in our results.

CONCLUSION

Fontan procedure has given higher survival rates for patients born with single ventricles. However, as the cohort ages, the physiological burden becomes increasingly evident. This meta-analysis shows that patients with Fontan circulation may benefit from using bosentan as it decreases postexercise HR and improves NYHA FC and 6MWD results; therefore, indirectly improving exercise capacity. Nonetheless, considerable work is required to strengthen our knowledge in improving the exercise capacity of Fontan patients.

## INTRODUCTION

The Fontan procedure is currently the mainstay therapy for single functional ventricles, where systemic and pulmonary circulations are placed in series to guarantee blood flow into the lungs passively. Many studies have shown that the Fontan procedure has a promising short-term efficacy, and it has been confirmed as a safe and effective surgical procedure. Since the development of the Fontan technique, the 10-year survival rate of postsurgery has risen from only 50% and to its current rate of 90% [1-3].

However, with prolonged follow-up duration, many complications have been observed that seriously influence the quality of life of patients.

Compromised cardiac output, elevated central venous pressures, and loss of pulsatile pulmonary blood flow all contribute to the reduction of exercise capacity. There is subtle evidence that drugs that improve myocardial performance and decrease pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) could improve the long-term outcome of Fontan patients as well as optimize circulatory efficiency. However, there are currently no studies that compare the efficacy of these various pharmacologic agents in improving the exercise capacity of Fontan patients [4, 5]. Thus, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to evaluate the efficacy of these different pharmacologic agents in improving various parameters that reflect on the exercise capacity of Fontan patients.
## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Search Strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement [6]. We searched 11 electronic databases, which include PubMed, Cochrane, MEDLINE, ProQuest, EBSCO, Science Direct, Springer, BMJ, Lancet, PloS One, and Google Scholar, with a combination of keywords and MeSH terms as follows: Fontan, bosentan, sildenafil, vasodilator, vasodilating agent, and exercise capacity. Studies were searched from the date of their inception to October 31, 2020, with no restrictions on language. Hand searching of the bibliographies from the included studies was also performed.

### Study Criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) cohort study or clinical trial (randomized or nonrandomized), (ii) participants underwent a Fontan operation, and (iii) participants used a vasodilating agent as the intervention. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) participants treated with any non-pharmacological therapy, (ii) case reports, reviews, editorials, and (iii) irretrievable full-text articles.

### Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Three authors (BM, C, and MS) screened the titles and abstracts for all searches independently and excluded all irrelevant articles. The full texts of the selected articles were then retrieved and read independently. Extraction of data included the first author, year of publication, study design, study location, the total number of participants, participants’ baseline characteristics (including gender, type of Fontan circulation, ventricular morphology, age at administration, weight, Body Mass Index (BMI) or Body Surface Area (BSA), a drug used as the intervention, type of tests used to assess exercise capacity, duration of follow up, the dosage and mode of administration of the drug, and the results of each test performed (New York Heart Association functional class (NYHA FC) assessment, 6-minute walk distance test (6 MWD), and cardiopulmonary exercise testing), which included: pulmonary vascular resistance, heart rate, blood pressure, rate of oxygen consumption (VO2), maximum rate of oxygen consumption (VO2 max), oxygen saturation (SpO2), as well as any adverse events noted during each study. Risks of bias assessments of the included studies were assessed independently by three authors (BM, C, and MS) according to the STROBE statement [7]. All disagreements were resolved by discussion between the authors.

### Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using REVMAN (version 5.4; Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK). Standardized mean difference (SMD) scores were the primary summary measure, allowing for comparison of the effect sizes of sildenafil and bosentan across NYHA, VO2, and SpO2 measures. All meta-analyses were conducted using a random-effects model, such as variation in the true effect size between studies caused by clinical (for example, CHD complexity, type of Fontan procedure, comorbidities) or methodological (such as the design of the study, risk of bias) factors. For each reporting method, separate analyses were then performed for sildenafil and bosentan, for each parameter: NYHA, VO2, and SpO2. For articles in which the number of control participants was not reported, a conservative approach assuming the number was equal to that of the patient group was used.

We used both the eyeball test and the I2 statistic to assess the heterogeneity of the studies included in the meta-analysis. The I2 statistic was used as an estimate of the percentage of total between-study variance, with I2 more than or equal to 50%, indicating substantial heterogeneity. For all statistical analyses, significance was set at p<0.05. Additional predictors not assessed in the meta-analyses were captured using narrative synthesis.
