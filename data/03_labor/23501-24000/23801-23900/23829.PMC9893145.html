Safety and Efficacy of Bioresorbable Vascular Scaffolds in Coronary Bifurcation Lesions: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Ahmed Alsinbili[1][*]

Sean O’Nunain[2]

Ceri Butler[2]

 

[1] Department of Internal Medicine, Addenbrooke's Hospital, Cambridge, England;

[2] Brighton and Sussex Medical School, Brighton, England

Author notes:

Abstract

Introduction: Coronary bifurcation lesions (CBL) are one fifth of all coronary lesions and they do not have an optimal strategy for stenting yet. Bioresorbable scaffolds (BRS) are novel inventions proposed to be the optimal solution. The aim of this systematic review was to assess the role of BRS in treating CBL by comparing it to dedicated bifurcation stents (DBS).

Methods: A systematic review was conducted following the PRISMA guidelines, searching databases such as ScienceDirect, EMBASE, MEDLINE, NIH, TRIP, PUBMED, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The risk of bias was assessed by MINORS and modified Cowley’s criteria. Q statistic was used for heterogeneity testing and a meta-analysis was conducted using the “meta” package in the R software application.

Results: Fourteen studies were included with an average follow-up period of twelve months. Almost 80% of the participants were male (p-value= 0.148) and around two-thirds were smokers. Meta-analysis was performed for myocardial infarction (MI), target lesion revascularisation (TLR), major adverse cardiac events (MACE), and stent thrombosis (ST). These showed statistically non-significant differences, with a slight trend favouring BRS except with stent thrombosis.

Conclusion: There is a lack of randomised trials on the topic, which may be an area for further research. But the results showed favourable yet statistically insignificant outcomes for BRS except for ST, an issue that can be addressed with technological advancement.

INTRODUCTION

1

The unique characteristic of bioresorbable scaffolds (BRS) is their complete dissolution in a period of time, which allows restoration of arterial normal anatomy, pulsatility, vasomotor reactivity, and the risk of restenosis [1]. They also have the potential to eliminate the risk of very late thrombosis. Moreover, they may require a shorter period of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), thereby lowering the risk of secondary bleeding [1]. The use of BRS in bifurcation lesions (CBL) has been understudied due to the high rate of exclusion of the complex lesions from conducted trials.

Several trials have studied the use of BRS in coronary artery disease, where their effect has been assessed against the most used option (second-generation drug-eluting stents) [2]; some studies have shown short-term increases in stent thrombosis (ST) and myocardial infarction (MI), while other studies have shown non-inferiority [3]. In a meta-analysis of 147 trials comparing all contemporary stents and scaffolds released in 2016, there was a lower rate of stent thrombosis and MI observed in DES than in BRS, but no difference was found when it came to all-cause mortality [4]. This difference in stent thrombosis did not exist when BRS was compared to classic bare-metal stents [5]. Recent trials have shown no difference between BRS and everolimus-eluting stents (EES) in clinical outcomes, reopening the door for a possible wider implementation of BRS in the future [6].

Currently, no stent has proven to be superior in treating bifurcation lesions, which allows for trying novel modalities, such as dedicated bifurcation stents (DBS) and BRS [7].

No randomized trial with clinical endpoints has compared BRS to other stents in CBLs, which is a gap in the evidence body, given that bifurcation lesions have to gain the most from BRS and that they constitute 20% of all coronary lesions [8]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether the use of a bioresorbable scaffold is safe and effective in relation to coronary bifurcation lesion management. This was assessed by studying the clinical outcomes of BRS compared to DBS. The sought outcomes included myocardial infarction (MI), death (D), cardiovascular death (CD), revascularisation, stent thrombosis (ST), and major adverse cardiac events (MACE).

The Absorb BRS is the only scaffold supported by randomised evidence to date, and the results show a concerning incidence of late stent thrombosis [9]. Currently, there are other manufacturers developing scaffolds, such as the Magmaris scaffold, which reported no scaffold thrombosis at 12 months of follow-up (n=123) [10]. As of May 2017, five BRS were approved in Europe: Absorb, Desolve, Fantom, ART Pure, and Magmaris. Technical challenges with BRS use include strut thickness reaching up to 170µm in Igaki-Tamai and ART scaffolds [11], which can result in poor deliverability, causing vessel trauma and, subsequently, platelet deposition. Moreover, strut thickness can generate higher shear stress and, therefore, cause platelet activation [12]. Hernandez and Lee argue that advances in research will overcome these limitations, and accordingly, BRS technology remains worthy of pursuit [1].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

2

This review was conducted following the PRISMA guidelines and the Cochrane guidelines for systematic reviews [13]. The study protocol was approved by the dissertation panel at Brighton and Sussex Medical School on 30th of November, 2018.

A multisource investigation was conducted to gather the data. First of all, an online review of published material was made through Science Direct, HDAS (EMBASE), HDAS (MEDLINE), HDAS (CINAHL), PUBMED, NIH, TRIP resources, Cochrane review clinical trials, European heart journal through Oxford University Press, Google Scholar, and ClinicalTrials.gov during December 2018. This was followed by scoping of existing references in the identified studies. Afterwards, an investigation of grey literature, such as conference abstracts or unpublished trials, has been done. The study authors were contacted on 18th December 2018 through researchgate.com and their published emails. Finally, a consultation with a specialist in the field at Brighton and Sussex University Hospitals was conducted to enquire about yet unidentified publications.

A systematic approach was followed, with the eligibility criteria being based on the PICO system [14]. Firstly, the included studies must have been designed to specifically address the treatment of bifurcation lesions. Regarding patients, no restrictions were placed on patient selection; other than that, the studies involved ethically robust inclusion criteria. Experimental studies (in-vitro, in-vivo) and animal studies were excluded because the outcomes sought were strictly clinical. Regarding intervention, the use of BRS for the main branch, whether or not it is used for the side branch was necessary; studies that used multiple different treatment options in the intervention were excluded. The comparison group needed to provide an approved method of treatment with an available body of evidence and comparable study designs to provide a second arm for review.

The studies only published in English were included along with those that involved a minimum follow-up time of six months, thereby providing enough time for reviewing the safety of the intervention. Moreover, only studies published in or after 2013 were considered.

The choice of the comparison group was made according to the availability of well-designed studies following the eligibility criteria and a study design similar to the BRS arm.

The study selection process followed the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Then, data from the included articles were extracted between 20th February 2019 to 25th February 2019 by the reviewer and sent to two reviewers for appraisal. For eligibility, a form was designed based on the Cochrane form for RCTs and non-RCTS [13].

Two methods for assessment of the risk of bias were implemented. The first assessment employed the widely used MINORS criteria [15], followed by the application of Cowley’s criteria [16] (Table 1).

A narrative synthesis of the outcomes is presented in a tabular pattern discussing different aspects of the data, followed by a meta-analysis using proportion as a measure of effect and comparison. Both fixed effects and random effects forms of meta-analyses effects were assessed. The analysis method used was the generalised linear mixed effect model (GLMM), which is appropriate for a proportion-based meta-analysis [17]. The results of the included studies are presented in the form of a forest plot, funnel plot, and Baujat plot to explore heterogeneity. Publication bias was also investigated graphically using funnel plots and Begg’s method based on rank correlation measure [18, 19]. P-values at a 0.05 level of significance were considered for the two-tailed test (p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant). Meta-analyses were performed using the “meta” package in the R software application.

RESULTS

3

1887 resources were identified, of which 81 abstracts were screened with the implementation of the eligibility criteria; the full-text review of 17 articles was assessed, and in the end, eight studies were selected in the bioresorbable scaffolds arm. Reasons for exclusion included design issues, duplication, animal studies, literature reviews, case studies, studies not specific to bifurcation lesions, or not involving bioresorbable scaffolds (Fig. 1) [12, 20, 21].

Eight single-arm trials examining BRS and seven single-arm trials examining DBS involving 1742 patients combined (1038 patients in the BRS group and 704 patients in the DBS group) with a total number of lesions of 1865 (1159 in the BRS group and 706 in the DBS group) were included (Table 2). The average follow-up period was 12 months. All reported centres were in European countries (4 trials did not clearly report on their centres). The Absorb scaffold was used in all studies in the BRS group; the DBS group used different stent brands: BiOSS Expert, Axxess system, Tryton, and BioSS LIM.

Baseline Features of Patients

3.1

The mean age of the two groups was 60 years for BRS and 65.6 years for the DBS group. Both groups reported more male participation (83.6% for BRS and 82.1% for DBS). The prevalence of diabetes approached one-quarter of participants, with sparse reporting on insulin dependence. Six out of 10 patients suffered from hypertension in both groups, and a quarter to a third of the participants reported active smoking. The DBS group had a stronger family history of CAD. Both groups had similar rates of CKD participants, and around a third of participants had a history of prior primary coronary intervention. A larger proportion of DBS patients presented with stable angina rather than an acute coronary syndrome (Table 3).

Baseline Features of the Lesions

3.2

Almost two-thirds of the lesions were bifurcations of LAD and diagonal arteries (LAD/D), and only a minority was in the distal main stem (4% - 6%), although the site of the bifurcation was not frequently reported. Syntax scores were higher in the DBS group (21.96 vs. 16.84). DBS patients were twice as likely to have multivessel disease in the DBS group, and they had more true bifurcation lesions (72.8% vs. 40.12%) as well as a higher prevalence of Medina 1.1.1 lesions (55.8% vs. 24.05%) (Table 4).

Procedural Features

3.3

There was much more utilisation of main-branch (MB) pre-dilation for the BRS. The length of the main branch involved procedurally was noticeably longer for the BRS. Both groups reported comparable MB diameters, and both groups reported comparable figures for pre- and post-dilation for the side branch (SB) (Table 5).

Clinical Outcomes

3.4

The DIVERGE study in the DBS group contributed 42.9% of the whole group. BRS group reported six cardiac deaths (0.67% vs. 1.01%). Two of the patients who died in the BRS group were confirmed to have stopped dual antiplatelet therapy against medical advice.

BRS patients reported less myocardial infarction (1.89% vs. 3.2%) but higher stent thrombosis incidence (1.97% vs. 0.57%). Overall, DBS patients suffered more MACE (6.73% vs. 5.84%) (Tables 6 and 7).

A meta-analysis was attempted for MI, TLR, MACE, CD, and ST, which showed no statistically significant difference between the two treatment groups in all tested outcomes. Meta-analysis failed for cardiac death due to the low number of cardiac deaths, which made the statistical analysis unfeasible (Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

4

Patients’ Characteristics

4.1

The five years’ difference in age between the DBS (65yrs) and BRS (60 years) groups can potentially cause some difference in the results relating to the general status of patients as they age, although both groups fall under the same category of coronary risk based on the Framingham study [22]. In a study comparing 2-years outcomes between patients younger than 65 years and those older, there was no difference found in cardiac mortality and TLR [23].

The DBS group showed lower smoking rates and higher hypercholesterolemia rates, which in theory may affect the study results, but as the Framingham study showed, both smoking and elevated cholesterol profiles tend to have a lower effect on IHD with aging and no independent effects after the age of 65, which annuls the effect of these two specific risk factors [22].

The BRS group reported lower levels of smoking, a family history of CAD, and dyslipidaemia, which may reflect that this group, in general, had a more selective approach towards the patients enrolled. Although with regards to major risk factors, such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension, the averages were very similar, thus supporting the homogeneity assumption discussed in the meta-analysis.

DBS patients had a prior history of MI twice as that in the BRS group (33.52% vs. 17.68%), but, as a presenting symptom for intervention, BRS reported higher unstable angina/NSTEMI rates (35.86% vs. 23.56%) and less stable angina (50.43% vs. 71.02%) which, according to published literature, could translate into a more favourable outcome in the DBS group [24].

Lesions’ Characteristics

4.2

The DBS group had more true bifurcation lesions; although these lesions have a lower angiographic success rate, previous literature does not show a statistically significant correlation with worse clinical outcomes [25].

Both groups showed a similar prevalence of combined LAD/D and distal LMS lesions (BRS: 70.66%, DBS: 67%). These lesions are usually the culprit of stent thrombosis and other MACEs [26].

Clinical Outcomes

4.3

The study at hand shows a statistically non-significant advantage of using BRS over DBS in all clinical outcomes except stent thrombosis. This advantage is not expected and thus warrants further research. The statistically non-significant elevated risk of stent thrombosis is in concordance with previously reported studies on scaffold use [27].

When the results of the six major trials of bifurcation lesion stenting (Table 8) were weighed and compared to the outcomes of the two arms of the current study, the same trends were observed, with BRS failing to show advantage only in relation to stent thrombosis.

The issue of scaffold thrombosis (ST) was first noted in the ABSORB randomised controlled trial, which reported nine cases of late ST [10]. This is thought to be due to the fact that BRS have low radial strength [28] and thus, are prone to fracture during post dilation [29, 30]. Another contributing factor is the strut thickness, which can be as high as 170µm [11]. It is postulated that thicker struts generate higher shear stress and, therefore, cause platelet activation [1]. Hernandez and Lee argued that advances in research would overcome these limitations [1].

Capodanno et al. discussed the subject of scaffold thrombosis, especially at bifurcations, where scaffold thrombosis carries ten times the risk of in-hospital mortality compared to scaffold thrombosis in non-bifurcation lesions; thus, recognising the potential of BRS, Capodanno recommends avoiding the use of BRS in bifurcation lesions altogether [31, 32].

Limitations

4.4

The lack of randomised controlled trials is one of the main challenges in this research. The use of non-randomised trials is uncommon in systematic reviews despite its representativeness of real-world conditions and outcomes. There is a clear risk of bias at the outcome level due to the different methodologies implemented in the studies regarding patient selection, as some studies enrolled patients and others recruited from registries. Attention was given to enrolment from the same registry and the time of enrolment; subsequent removal of studies was made when a clear probability of duplication existed [22, 33]. Moreover, the rate of drop-out in the BRS group was remarkable, and that could affect the validity even though reporting on the reasons for drop-out was made. Moreover, the review was conducted by one reviewer, followed by the revision of two appraisers, but the effect of this might be limited based on the type of collected data, which is numerical, objective, and standardised (Table 1) [34-36].

Finally, the design of a systematic comparative review has not yet been researched in-depth, and it may cause a limitation in comparability and reproducibility. Although it is a good design to reflect real-world conditions, it is still evolving and not completely regulated.

CONCLUSION

Patients who had coronary bifurcation lesions and were treated with bioresorbable scaffolds displayed statistically similar outcomes to those treated with dedicated bifurcation lesions, with a tendency for BRS to have a lower incidence of MI, MACE, CD, and a higher incidence of stent thrombosis. Further randomised controlled trials are recommended to deeply investigate this area. Moreover, the inclusion of bifurcation lesions in major studies is advised as they constitute 20% of all coronary lesions. While the risk of stent thrombosis is a major issue occurring with scaffolds, new models with thinner struts provide hope for a better ST profile.
