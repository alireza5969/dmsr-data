Bisphosphonate Therapy for Treating Osteonecrosis in Pediatric Leukemia Patients: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Background:

Despite improved outcomes in children with leukemia, complications such as osteonecrosis are common. We conducted a systematic review to investigate the role of bisphosphonates in reducing pain, improving mobility, and stabilizing lesions in pediatric leukemia survivors.

Methods:

Using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, we searched the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, and ClinicalTrials.gov databases. Five of 221 articles retrieved met our inclusion criteria.

Results:

Bisphosphonates, especially when combined with dietary calcium and vitamin D supplements and physical therapy (“supplements/PT”) were associated with improved pain and mobility in 54% and 50% of patients, respectively. A significantly greater proportion of patients treated with bisphosphonates (83%) reported mild/moderate pain or no pain compared with those with supplements/PT alone (36%) (P < 0.001). Sixty-six percent of patients treated with bisphosphonates achieved improved/full mobility compared with 27% of those treated with supplements/PT alone (P = 0.02). However, 46% of patients showed progressive joint destruction despite bisphosphonate therapy. No adverse events were reported, except for acute phase reactions to intravenous therapies.

Conclusions:

Bisphosphonates, when combined with supplements/PT, were associated with less pain and improved mobility, but not prevention of joint destruction in pediatric leukemia patients with osteonecrosis.

INTRODUCTION

For children with leukemia, outcomes have been steadily improving, with an estimated 5-year event-free survival rate of approximately 84%.1,2 However, long-term complications such as osteonecrosis are increasingly common, with varying incidence (0.9% to 29%) depending on patient characteristics, such as age at diagnosis.3–7 Osteonecrosis is typically diagnosed within 3 years after commencing chemotherapy. Although it is sometimes asymptomatic, osteonecrosis causes pain, immobility, and decreased quality of life in as many as 84% of affected patients.3,4,8,9 Osteonecrosis poses a particular problem for children because it can lead to joint collapse, degenerative joint disease, and subsequent arthroplasty at a young age.8,10 Risk factors for the development of osteonecrosis in the setting of pediatric leukemia include adolescence (>10 years old) at cancer diagnosis, high body mass index value, and female sex.3,4,7,11

Depending on the severity of the lesion, treatments for osteonecrosis range from nonoperative and bisphosphonate therapies to surgery (e.g., core decompression, autologous bone grafting, and joint arthroplasty).12–17 The data supporting the use of bisphosphonates in the treatment of osteonecrosis are largely based on results of nontraumatic osteonecrosis of the hip for causes other than hematologic malignancies in adults.18,19 Literature regarding the utility of bisphosphonate treatment in survivors of pediatric hematologic malignancies is limited to small case series, possibly because of concerns about conducting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in this vulnerable population. However, some authors suggest that bisphosphonate treatment may remain a useful modality in the overall treatment of these patients.12–14,20 We conducted a systematic review of the relevant medical literature in an effort to investigate the potential role of bisphosphonate therapy in reducing pain, improving function, and stabilizing lesions in survivors of pediatric leukemia.

METHODS

Search Strategy

On August 9, 2018, we conducted a systematic search of the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, and ClinicalTrials.gov electronic databases for relevant articles using the following search terms: (‘leukemia’), (‘bone necrosis’ or ‘avascular necrosis’ or ‘aseptic necrosis’ or ‘osteonecrosis’ or ‘bone infarct’), and (‘bisphosphonate’ or ‘diphosphonate’ or ‘pamidronate’ or ‘ibandronate’ or ‘zoledronic acid’ or ‘alendronate’). All identified articles were screened for inclusion, and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines were followed. Full literature search methods and terms are included in the Supplemental Digital Content.

Study Selection

We included original research articles published in English that investigated bisphosphonate therapy for the treatment of osteonecrosis in survivors of pediatric leukemias. We excluded duplicate publications, case reports, letters, conference notes, and abstracts without accompanying manuscripts. Two authors independently screened the titles and abstracts of potential studies and subsequently reviewed the full text of potentially relevant studies to determine eligibility. Any discrepancies regarding the inclusion or exclusion of articles were resolved via joint review. Six patients with lymphoma and 2 patients with benign hematologic malignancies were excluded from 1 included study.16

Of 221 studies retrieved, we selected 22 for further review on the basis of their titles and/or abstracts. Five of these studies were included in our analysis (Fig. 1). No relevant RCTs investigated the use of bisphosphonates for improving pain and mobility in pediatric leukemia patients with osteonecrosis. In total, 1 observational, 1 interventional, and 3 retrospective cohort studies describing 66 unique patients were included (Table 1).

Data Collection

The primary outcomes of interest were pain, mobility, and radiographic changes after treatment. From each study, we extracted the following: 1) study characteristics (author names, publication year, sample size, follow-up period); 2) patient characteristics (sex, age at oncologic diagnosis, joints affected); 3) treatment parameters (type of bisphosphonate, duration of treatment); and 4) patient outcomes (pain/mobility at diagnosis and after treatment, radiographic parameters). Duplicate patient data across studies were removed by matching patient characteristics. For duplicate patients, we used outcome data from the most recent publication.

Study Characteristics

All studies included children previously treated for leukemia who subsequently developed osteonecrosis. Duration of follow-up ranged from 6 to 72 months but was not specified in 2 studies. Patient age and sex did not differ significantly between treatment groups or among studies (1-way ANOVA, Fisher exact; P > 0.05) (Table 2). Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) scores ranged from 9 to 11 points (maximum of 16) for noncomparative studies, and 15 points (maximum of 24) for comparative studies (Table 1). Two studies used zoledronic acid, 2 used pamidronate, and 1 used alendronate and/or pamidronate as bisphosphonate interventions (Tables 1 and 2). Two studies used dietary calcium and vitamin D supplements and physical therapy (herein, “supplements/PT”), in addition to bisphosphonates, and compared outcomes of patients treated with both versus those of patients treated with supplements/PT alone (Table 1). Two studies included pain and mobility assessments at baseline (at osteonecrosis diagnosis), in addition to follow-up assessments. Across all 5 studies, median patient age at cancer diagnosis was 13 years (range, 2.7–17), median time to osteonecrosis diagnosis was 1.1 years (range: 0.2–3.3), and median duration of bisphosphonate treatment was 13 months (range unavailable) (Table 2).

Critical Appraisal

We appraised the methodologic quality of all eligible studies using the MINORS criteria. MINORS requires that at least 8 criteria are rated (12 criteria if assessing a comparative study). A maximum of 2 points were assigned per criterion: 0, not reported; 1, reported but inadequate; 2, reported and adequate. The criteria are as follows: reporting of the aim, patient inclusion, data collection, appropriate endpoints, unbiased assessment, appropriate follow-up period, loss to follow-up, and level for statistical significance. For comparative studies, the MINORS criteria additionally assess the adequacy of control groups, contemporary groups, baseline equivalence of groups, and statistical analyses (Supplemental Digital Content).

Statistical Analysis

Our primary analysis assessed pain and mobility outcomes for survivors of pediatric leukemia treated with bisphosphonates and/or supplements/PT, relying on each study’s definitions of outcomes. If pain outcomes were labeled “pain-free and/or reduced pain with decreased analgesic requirement,” we included them in the “mild/moderate pain” category to limit exaggeration of the effect. Outcomes after initial alendronate treatment alone were analyzed for patients who were first treated with alendronate and later switched to pamidronate. In 1 study, all but 2 patients were treated with bisphosphonates.15 Because these patients were unidentified, we included them in the bisphosphonate group and performed statistical analyses with inclusion and exclusion of this study.

Duplicate patients were removed in an effort to limit bias. Two studies had an incomplete overlap in their patient cohorts, so we conducted statistical analyses with and without each study, as well as with and without the duplicate patient data. There were no significant differences in total outcomes; therefore, we excluded the 11 duplicate patient entries from further analysis. In addition, 1 study included 6 patients with lymphoma and 2 patients with Fanconi anemia and sideroblastic anemia, whom we excluded from our results.16 Data from 9 patients in the 2013 study by Padhye et al.16 were removed because they duplicated data reported by the same group in 2016.15

Because we anticipated some variance between studies, we used the Fisher exact test and 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to identify any differences in patient characteristics. To examine the association between bisphosphonate or supplements/PT and patient outcomes, we used the Wilcoxon rank-sum and Kruskal-Wallis tests. The methods of the included studies were considered too varied to conduct a meta-analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using Stata, version 15.1, software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). Significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

In the studies with pain, mobility, and radiography assessments at baseline and follow-up, bisphosphonates, especially when combined with supplements/PT, were associated with improvement of pain (54% of patients) and mobility (50% of patients) relative to baseline (Table 3). Although pain and mobility outcomes were favorable, 46% of patients in these studies showed progressive joint destruction and/or collapse, despite bisphosphonate therapy (Table 3). Two studies reported that patients with osteonecrotic lesions in the knee typically remained radiographically stable with zoledronic acid therapy; whereas, those with involvement of the hip showed progressive joint destruction.15,16

When comparing bisphosphonate treatment with supplements/PT alone, patients treated with bisphosphonates showed better pain outcomes, with a greater proportion of patients reporting mild/moderate pain or no pain at last follow-up (83%, P < 0.001) (Table 4). In 1 study, all 17 patients treated with pamidronate had improved pain and function/range of motion.12 At final clinical assessment, 66% of patients treated with bisphosphonates achieved improved or full mobility compared with 27% of those treated with supplements/PT alone (P = 0.02) (Table 4).

The largest proportion of pain-free outcomes was in patients treated with pamidronate (P < 0.001); whereas, the proportion of full/improved mobility outcomes was larger in patients treated with zoledronic acid (P = 0.02) (Table 5). However, these results may be confounded by the longer median duration of pamidronate treatment compared with that of alendronate.

No adverse events were reported, with the exception of acute phase reactions, which were commonly seen with bisphosphonate therapy and were noted in at least half of the patients treated with pamidronate in 2 studies.13–16

DISCUSSION

The English-language medical literature was systematically reviewed to evaluate pain and mobility outcomes in survivors of pediatric leukemias who were treated for osteonecrosis with bisphosphonate therapy. Bisphosphonates were associated with improved pain and mobility when used with supplements/PT, although they did not appear to prevent radiographic progression of osteonecrosis and joint collapse.

Bisphosphonates have historically been used as antiresorptive agents for treating conditions such as osteoporosis to prevent bone loss and fractures,21,22 as well as decreasing the rate of skeletal-related events in patients with osseous metastatic disease. Although several studies have shown that bisphosphonates may be effective for treating osteonecrosis,23–25 most analyses excluded survivors of pediatric malignancies. As a result, there are scant data evaluating the role of antiresorptive therapy in pediatric leukemia survivors, a group known to have a very high rate of skeletal involvement at a young age. Our findings suggest that bisphosphonates may be useful as part of the treatment of osteonecrosis in this challenging clinical scenario, with 54% and 50% of patients reporting improved pain and mobility, respectively.

However, 46% of patients showed progressive joint destruction despite bisphosphonate therapy. Although this high rate of degeneration in the setting of favorable pain control appears contradictory, a unifying biologic explanation could be possible. Because of the potential role of osteoclast-mediated netrin-1 in promoting aberrant innervation in subchondral bone, any reduction in osteoclast function may impair netrin-1 production.26 Such a scenario could explain the improvement in pain, as well as the progression of joint degeneration.

When comparing bisphosphonate types, zoledronate and pamidronate are administered intravenously, whereas alendronate is given orally and therefore subject to reduced absorption and difficulties with adherence. Padhye et al.15,16 noted that osteonecrotic lesions of the knee responded more favorably to zoledronic acid therapy than did those of the hip in patients with both knee and hip involvement. This finding suggests that zoledronic acid may stabilize osteonecrosis of the knee but may not distribute as well to the osteonecrotic femoral head even if used at an earlier stage of osteonecrosis.15,16 One study reported a reduction in the volume of lesions in all 17 patients but showed no significant difference between those treated with supplements/PT versus bisphosphonate therapy.12 The same study reported that all 6 patients treated with pamidronate had improvements in pain, whereas 7 of 8 patients treated with supplements/PT alone continued to report severe pain.12 It is unclear whether these results are related to differences in the involved joints. Hips, knees, and ankles constituted 89% of affected joints (Table 6), which may reflect a bias toward treating more commonly for the weightbearing joints of the lower extremity.

Despite our inclusion of results from alendronate treatment alone for patients who were switched from alendronate to pamidronate in 1 study, all patients who were subsequently treated with pamidronate showed improvements in pain and mobility.12 Leblicq et al.13 reported improvement in pain for 77% of 13 patients treated with pamidronate for 1–4 months, whereas none of the patients treated with supplements/PT had improvements in pain. Patient outcomes varied by study, which may reflect inherent differences in the treating institutions where these studies were conducted.

The main adverse reaction reported in the included studies was an acute phase reaction after the first bisphosphonate injection, although this was reported only in patients treated with pamidronate and zoledronic acid. However, in 1 study, 2 patients also presented with new lesions (1 in the shoulder and 1 in both knees) recognized during pamidronate treatment.14 Another study reported 5 patients who sustained fractures of nonosteonecrotic sites during treatment for osteonecrosis, although 3 of these patients had not received bisphosphonate therapy and 1 of 2 other patients treated with bisphosphonates sustained the fracture before bisphosphonate treatment.12 Despite its well-recognized association with bisphosphonates,27 there were no reports of osteonecrosis of the jaw in any patients in the included studies.

Although the methodologic quality of the included studies was deemed acceptable, our analysis was subject to limitations. First, the small number of available studies and lack of relevant RCTs presented a major limitation to the overall analysis. Because of the lack of RCTs, our analyses depend on subjective measures of pain and mobility in primarily retrospective and observational studies, which may be subject to recall bias. Second, the methods of the included studies were too heterogeneous to conduct a meta-analysis, thus rendering the results not fully comparable. Third, some patients required additional interventions while on bisphosphonate therapy, such as bone grafts, chondroplasty, and osteotomies, which may confound our findings,13,15,16 although this reflects typical treatment in clinical practice. Despite these limitations, our study offers insight into the potential utility of bisphosphonate therapy as part of the treatment of osteonecrosis in survivors of pediatric leukemias.

Bisphosphonates, when combined with supplements/PT, were associated with improved pain and mobility in survivors of pediatric leukemias with resulting osteonecrosis. These subjective clinical improvements may be accompanied by a continued risk of joint destruction and collapse.
