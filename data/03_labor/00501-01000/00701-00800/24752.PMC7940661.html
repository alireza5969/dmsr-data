Differences in Outcomes of Chemoradiation in Women With Invasive Cervical Cancer by Human Immunodeficiency Virus Status: A Systematic Review

Sidrah Shah[a]

Melody Xu[b]

[a] University of Texas Southwestern Medical School, Dallas, Texas

[b] Department of Radiation Oncology, University of California, San Francisco, California

Author notes:

Correspondence to: [*] Corresponding author: Surbhi Grover, MD, MPH; Surbhi.grover@pennmedicine.upenn.edu.

Abstract

Purpose:

Cervical cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer death among women worldwide, and women living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) carry the highest burden of disease. Chemoradiation (CRT) is the current standard treatment for locally advanced cervical cancer, without specific treatment modifications based on HIV status. This systematic review evaluates existing literature reporting differences in outcomes between HIV+ and HIV− women with invasive cervical cancer treated with CRT.

Methods and Materials:

Searches were conducted through Pubmed, Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library. Two researchers independently conducted article selection; articles were selected by title, then abstract, and then by full text content. Data were extracted using a structured form.

Results:

Thirteen articles were included in the analysis, all of which were either retrospective or prospective cohort studies published between 2012 and 2018, and most of which were conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa. Treatment outcomes included treatment response, survival, toxicities, and quality of life. The majority of studies (8 of 13) reported no differences in treatment outcomes by HIV status. Out of 8 studies that assessed survival, 6 reported no significant difference based on HIV status. All 4 studies assessing treatment response found no significant differences based on HIV status. Among 6 studies primarily assessing treatment toxicity, 3 showed no differences based on HIV status. Factors affecting treatment outcomes, such as treatment selection bias, pretreatment hemoglobin levels, and antiretroviral therapy administration, were not systematically accounted for.

Conclusions:

The majority of studies analyzed showed no differences in treatment outcomes, including overall toxicity, treatment response, or mortality, on the basis of HIV infection status. These results suggest CRT should continue to be the treatment of choice for locally invasive cervical cancer regardless of HIV status. Further study is required to more precisely account for other variables that influence treatment outcome.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in 28 countries around the world and is the leading cause of cancer death among women in 42.1 Of the countries most affected by cervical cancer, the majority are concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, and all are low- and middle-income countries.1 The disparity in the incidence and mortality of cervical cancer between high income countries and low- and middle-income countries are largely due to higher rates of cervical cancer screening implementation in high income countries, as well as higher rates of vaccination against human papillomavirus (HPV), a virus known to be associated with almost all cases of cervical cancer.2–5 An additional factor that is inextricably linked to the concentration of cervical cancer in Sub-Saharan Africa is the high rates of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection in the region.6

Women living with HIV are 4 to 5 times more likely to develop invasive cervical cancer than their HIV− counterparts.7 This is likely due to increased life expectancy on antiretroviral therapy (ART)1,6 and the effects of HIV on the immune system, making it more difficult to clear HPV infections and more likely for women to develop dysplasia and eventually cancer.6,8 This theory is supported by studies demonstrating a longer length of HPV infection among HIV+ individuals, as well as the overall higher rates of HPV-associated cancers among people living with HIV.6,8 Given that many resource-limited countries face dual epidemics of cervical cancer and HIV, it is important to understand the effects of resource-intensive standard treatment recommendations such as chemoradiation (CRT) and brachytherapy in this specific population.9

Guidelines established on the basis of numerous randomized trials recommend CRT as the treatment of choice for early stage bulky and locally advanced invasive cervical cancer.10 Compared with radiation therapy alone, CRT has been shown to improve both overall and progression-free survival in this population, and may also reduce local and distant recurrence.10 However, increased acute toxicity rates among patients receiving CRT compared with those treated with radiation alone must be considered carefully in resource-limited settings where the cost of managing these toxicities may outweigh the benefits of intensifying treatment.11 Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend that cervical cancer treatment not be modified solely on the basis of HIV status.12 Furthermore, the differences in outcomes of patients with cervical cancer treated with CRT in the ART era continues to be debated.

There is currently no systematic review of existing literature comparing the clinical outcomes of CRT between HIV+ and HIV− women with invasive cervical cancer. This systematic review seeks to present the results of previously published studies that report on these outcomes and assess for differences based on HIV status.

Methods and Materials

Study design

A literature review protocol in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses checklist was developed.13 Retrospective cohort studies, prospective cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies were eligible for this study. Case reports, case series, qualitative studies, news articles, textbook chapters, and reports or summaries from conferences were excluded owing to study design. Only articles written in English were included.

Search strategy and data sources

Searches were conducted in Pubmed, Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library using a combination of MeSH terms and keywords to catch indexed and nonindexed articles. The full electronic search strategy used in Ovid MEDLINE is included in Appendix E1 for reference. Articles were first eliminated by title, then abstract, then by the full text of the article itself. The searches were conducted on February 26, 2019.

Participants and study outcomes

The population being investigated was women with invasive cervical cancer undergoing CRT, and studies investigating this population were included regardless of age, ethnicity, geographic location, comorbidities, or population size. The primary outcomes of interest were mortality/survival and treatment response, and the secondary outcomes were toxicities and quality of life. Outcomes in the population of interest were compared based on the HIV status of the participants. To this end, only articles that stratified CRT outcomes by HIV status in women with invasive cervical cancer were included. Articles that did not look at both HIV+ and HIV− populations were excluded. Articles were included as long as some participants underwent CRT, even if a subset of the population did not, to allow for more articles to be analyzed.

Study selection

Two researchers independently conducted each phase of article selection based on these categories: study outcome, study type, population, and other. Articles eliminated by study outcome included those finding molecular pathways involved in a process, assessing screening/prevention methods or behavior/knowledge/beliefs, determining cost-effectiveness/feasibility, reporting prevalence/incidence of disease or risk factors for cancer, or describing the effects of noncancer treatments. Articles eliminated by study type included case reports, qualitative studies, news articles, textbook chapters, and reports or summaries from conferences. Articles eliminated by population included patients without invasive cervical cancer, only patients who did not receive CRT, only men, only HIV+ patients, or only HIV− patients. The category of “other” encompassed duplicate articles or those that were too ambiguous or broad.

To select by title, each researcher independently read each title and labeled it based on their decision to include, exclude, or discuss further. Excluded articles were designated to 1 of the aforementioned exclusion criteria. Once all titles had been analyzed, all articles that were labeled for inclusion or further discussion were retained. Those that were labeled for exclusion by both researchers were excluded. The remaining articles, which were labeled for exclusion by 1 and for further discussion by another, were further discussed until both researchers agreed on whether or not they should be included in the following phase.

Selection by abstract was conducted in a similar manner. Selection by text was performed by both researchers reading the article together and discussing its eligibility for inclusion in the review.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from the final articles using a structured form (Appendix E2). Information taken from each article included author, date of publication, study design, country, study size, HIV+ and HIV− cases, number of patients receiving CRT, treatment differences between HIV+ and HIV− patients, median radiation and brachytherapy doses, number of chemotherapy cycles, treatment type, median CD4 count and viral load of HIV+ patients, median stage of cancer diagnosis, median age, treatment outcomes, treatment completion rate, patient demographics, percentage of HIV+ patients on ART, study limitations, and study conclusions.

Data analysis

The information from the data collection form was first analyzed through qualitative synthesis. The number of articles that did and did not find statistically significant differences between the HIV+ and HIV− populations in the study were noted, and the details of each study were compared to better understand the differences between each study. A meta-analysis was not performed owing to the heterogeneity of the included studies.

Results

Search results

The search was conducted on February 26, 2019, and yielded 1360 results. An additional article was added after review of the references of 1 of the included articles. After removing duplicates, 842 articles remained. Elimination by title yielded 73 articles for abstract review. After elimination by abstract, 41 articles were kept, and the full text was reviewed. After the final round of elimination, 13 articles remained for inclusion in analysis (Fig 1).

Description of studies and qualitative synthesis

Table 1 describes the general characteristics of each included study. Seven of the final 13 papers analyzed were prospective studies, and 6 were retrospective. No randomized controlled trials were found addressing the topic. Most of the studies were conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa with dates of publication ranging from 2012 to 2018. Only 4 of the 13 studies exclusively included patients undergoing CRT for cervical cancer (Table 1), and 2 did not specify the proportion of patients treated with CRT. The studies are ordered by date of publication.

Overall, 6 studies reported some statistically significant differences in treatment outcomes (mortality, treatment response, or toxicities) between HIV+ and HIV− patients. However, of these 6, 1 also reported that differences in mortality were no longer significant when the HIV+ cohort was restricted to only those on ART.14

Clinical and demographic characteristics of the participants

Table 2 describes the clinical and demographic characteristics of the patients in each study, grouped by whether or not they found significant differences in treatment outcomes based on HIV status, similar to Table 1. Six studies reported that HIV+ patients were statistically significantly younger than HIV− patients by approximately 1 decade. Five studies included only patients treated with curative intent.14–18 The remaining studies included patients treated with palliative intent or did not specify treatment intent.19–26 The percentage of HIV+ patients on ART varied significantly, with only 3 studies reporting 100% of participants on ART before starting treatment.15,16,25 Treatment completion rates likewise varied significantly, with no studies reporting a 100% CRT completion rate.14,17–19,21,23,25,26

Treatment response and survival

Table 3 describes treatment response and mortality outcomes, which were assessed by a total of 9 studies. Eight studies reported survival outcomes, and only 2 of these found statistically significant differences based on HIV status. Dryden-Petersen et al19 studied 327 patients, with 84% of HIV+ patients receiving CRT compared with 73% of HIV− patients. Although treatment response outcomes were not found to be different by HIV status, HIV+ patients still experienced higher mortality rates at a median follow-up period of 19.7 months (50.7% HIV+ compared with 41.7% HIV+).19 Notably, the study found that the effect of HIV infection on mortality was attenuated by increasing CD4 counts. However, even HIV+ patients with near-normal CD4 counts still experienced higher risk of mortality after treatment.19 Mangena et al23 studied 98 patients, of which 74.5% of HIV+ patients and 72.3% of HIV− patients received CRT. Median survival was found to be 21 months for HIV+ patients and 32 months for those who were HIV− (P = .05).23 Of note, HIV+ patients in this cohort experienced more treatment interruptions than their HIV− counterparts.23 Patients receiving palliative treatment were also included, although the percentage of these patients was not significantly different between the HIV+ and HIV− groups.23 Four studies assessed initial treatment response based on HIV status, and none found significant differences between HIV+ and HIV− patients.15,18,19,21

Toxicities and adverse effects

Table 4 presents 6 studies that assessed treatment toxicities based on HIV status, and 1 that assessed quality of life. Toxicity was defined differently by each study. For example, Simonds et al17 graded toxicity using the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group guidelines, whereas Vendrell et al26 only evaluated neutropenia. The most common toxicities were hematologic, specifically neutropenia and anemia. Among the 6 studies, 3 studies found no significant difference in treatment toxicities based on HIV status.19,22,25 Factors including subjectivity in reporting and assessing for toxicities, significant differences in treatment and other characteristics such as age between HIV+ and HIV− patients, and lack of controlling for these variables in statistical analysis affected the studies that did find significant differences based on HIV status.17,23,26 One study assessed quality of life and found significant differences based on HIV status in numerous domains, including physical function, pain, fatigue, appetite loss, nausea and vomiting, diarrhea, cognitive function, emotional and social health, and sexual well-being.20 However, this study had higher loss-to-follow-up rates among the HIV+ cohort compared with the HIV− cohort (56% and 34%, respectively; P value not reported), inherent subjectivity in analyzing toxicity based on self-reported questionnaires, and failure to control for other statistically significant demographic differences found between the cohorts such as age and treatment modality.20

Treatment intent

Table 5 presents treatment outcomes of the 5 studies that included only patients treated with curative intent.14–18 Of these studies, 3 assessed survival, 2 assessed treatment response, and 1 assessed treatment toxicity. Only the study assessing treatment toxicity found significant differences between HIV+ and HIV− patients, with HIV+ patients being significantly more likely to develop acute hematologic toxicities, and patients with grade 3 or 4 toxicities were twice as likely to be HIV+.17 The remaining 4 studies found no significant differences in treatment outcomes based on HIV status.

Pretreatment hemoglobin levels

Only 3 studies took pretreatment hemoglobin levels into account in their statistical analysis.15,16,22 These studies did not find significant differences in treatment outcomes between HIV+ and HIV− participants. One other study included participants’ pretreatment hemoglobin levels in data collection but did not account for them when statistically analyzing treatment outcomes between HIV+ and HIV− cohorts.23 This study found that HIV+ participants were more likely to have a pretreatment hemoglobin of less than 11 g/dL, and therefore experienced more treatment interruptions than their HIV− counterparts, which in turn negatively affected prognosis.23

Discussion

This review assessed 13 studies published within the last 8 years investigating the differences in treatment outcomes of patients with invasive cervical cancer who underwent CRT treatment based on HIV status. There was significant heterogeneity among the analyzed studies, precluding the performance of a meta-analysis. We found that, among studies that stratified for outcomes based on receival of CRT, there were no significant differences in mortality rate or treatment response between HIV+ and HIV− patients with invasive cervical cancer.

Notably, treatment selection differences by HIV status were present in 9 of the 13 analyzed studies, yet not accounted for in their statistical analysis. This is a significant factor to consider, as radiation therapy alone is known to have fewer toxicities and inferior treatment outcomes compared with CRT.11 Studies that included patients who were not treated with CRT were included in this analysis to allow for more articles to be analyzed. Because none of the studies with significant differences in treatments between HIV+ and HIV− patients took this into account, it is difficult to postulate how much of the differences in outcomes may be explained by differences in treatment selection.

Only 3 of the 13 reviewed studies had all HIV+ patients on ART before starting CRT, and all 3 of these studies found that there was no difference in outcomes between the 2 groups. These findings suggest that HIV+ patients being treated for both HIV and cervical cancer are less likely to have differences in toxicity and clinical outcomes compared with their HIV− counterparts. This explanation is further underlined by the findings of Simonds et al,17 which initially indicated that HIV+ patients have a significantly higher overall mortality rate than HIV− patients. However, when analysis was restricted to the 5-year survival of HIV+ patients on ART, these findings were no longer statistically significant. Nevertheless, correlating survival with the median CD4 count of the HIV+ population in each study does not produce a clear pattern, with some studies finding a significant difference in toxicity and survival outcomes based on HIV status despite reporting higher CD4 counts compared with other studies that did not find a significant difference (Table 2). For example, Ferreira et al21 studied an HIV+ population with a median CD4 count of 263 and did not find a significant difference in mortality rate based on HIV status, while Dryden-Peterson et al19 did find a significant difference with a median CD4 count of 397.

Differences in treatment intent were likewise notable between studies. In studies that included patients receiving palliative treatment, results were not stratified by treatment intent.19,21–23 Therefore, it is difficult to estimate based on these studies the role inclusion of palliative treatment has on outcomes. Four additional studies did not specify treatment intent, making analysis similarly difficult to conclusively interpret.20,24–26 However, when these studies were excluded from analysis to reduce heterogeneity in patient populations (Table 5), 4 of the 5 remaining studies assessed treatment response and overall survival and did not find significant differences based on HIV status.14–16,18 In the fifth study, Simonds et al17 assessed treatment toxicities in patients being treated with curative intent and found that HIV+ patients were significantly more likely to develop acute hematologic toxicities, although gastrointestinal, renal, skin, and weight toxicities were not significantly different between both groups. Therefore, stratifying studies by treatment intent indicated that the inclusion of patients receiving palliative treatment may explain in part the differences in treatment outcomes found in some studies between HIV+ and HIV− patients.

An additional factor only accounted for by 3 of the included studies is pretreatment hemoglobin levels.15,16,22 Hemoglobin levels have historically been considered an important prognostic factor in cervical cancer, though recent evidence suggests otherwise, and in all 3 of these studies, no significant difference in treatment outcomes or toxicity were found between HIV+ and HIV− populations.27–30 A fourth study, which demonstrated significantly poorer survival among HIV+ participants, included participants’ hemoglobin levels but did not account for them in the final statistical analysis of survival.23 Notably, this study did show that their HIV+ participants were more likely to be anemic, and that this may have contributed to their overall poorer survival compared with their HIV− counterparts because of increased treatment interruption for blood transfusions.23 The absence of patient stratification by hemoglobin levels may therefore potentially account for toxicity or clinical outcome differences among other studies in this analysis.

Strengths and limitations

Conclusions about survival and toxicity varied by study; factors such as treatment selection bias, varied levels of ART administration, pretreatment hemoglobin, and lack of clear stratification by treatment intent may account for some of these differences. Limitations of this analysis are the inclusion of patients who did not undergo CRT in most of the articles analyzed, the inconsistencies in outcome types reported, and the lack of uniformity in statistical analysis performed by each study. The variability in the HIV control subset analysis is not accounted for in many of these conclusions. Furthermore, the majority of studies were conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa, limiting their validity in other settings. The strengths of this study include the exhaustiveness of the search through the use of 6 databases and the inclusion criteria with which articles were chosen.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this review analyzed treatment outcomes of CRT in women with invasive cervical cancer based on HIV status. Thirteen studies were found, with the majority of studies demonstrating no significant differences in treatment or toxicity outcomes based on HIV status. Therefore, this review suggests that the standard of care for cervical cancer ought to be concurrent CRT regardless of the HIV status of the patient. Given the small number of studies we found addressing this question, as well as the limitations of the analyzed studies, further study is necessary to define the specific outcomes of CRT on HIV+ and HIV− women with invasive cervical cancer, controlling for variables such as age, stage, treatment modality, CD4 count, viral load, hemoglobin levels, and treatment intent.
