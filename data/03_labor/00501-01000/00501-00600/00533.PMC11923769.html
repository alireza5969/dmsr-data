Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis Occurring in Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Systematic Review

Abstract

Background

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare and aggressive syndrome of excessive cytokine requiring prompt recognition and aggressive therapy.

Aims

We aimed to systematically characterize HLH in moderate-to-severe inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).

Methods

We performed a systematic review of the literature (PubMED; EMBASE) and FDA Adverse Event Reporting System in accordance with the PRISMA statement. Use of biologics was used as a surrogate definition for disease severity (consistent with usual and contemporary clinical management), to enable identification of rare HLH cases with the highest fidelity.

Results

58 cases of HLH occurring in IBD patients are known (mean age: 26.0 years, 70% male, 83% with Crohn’s disease, mean disease duration 7.0 years). 34.5% of patients were undergoing induction therapy at HLH diagnosis. All cases occurred on patients exposed to anti-TNF agents, but cases with anti-integrin or anti-IL-12/23 exposure were reported. 2/3 of cases did not report prior AZA/6MP exposure. Underlying opportunistic infection or lymphoma was found in > 80% of cases. Survival was 70% if promptly recognized and treated. Five patients restarted biologics after HLH resolved, and one patient developed recurrent HLH.

Conclusions

HLH is rare among IBD patients exposed to biologic therapy. Most cases had an identifiable infection or malignancy at the time of diagnosis as well as history of immunomodulator use. Risk factors may include younger age, male gender, presence of Crohn’s disease, and induction phase of treatment. Our study is not intended to assess risk of HLH with specific IBD therapies.

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare immune overactivation disorder of macrophage and cytotoxic T cell activation causing excessive systemic inflammation. HLH is defined by the presence of at least five of the following eight criteria: fever, splenomegaly, cytopenias, hypertriglyceridemia, evidence of hemophagocytosis, low or absent natural killer cell activity, elevated ferritin, and elevated soluble CD25 [1]. Secondary HLH is the most common form of HLH and is often related to an underlying infection or malignancy leading to autoimmunity [2]. Without prompt recognition and aggressive treatment, HLH is invariably fatal [3]. In gastroenterology, individuals with moderate-to-severe inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) are already inherently immunocompromised by the milieu of corticosteroids, biologics, and immunomodulators necessary to induce and maintain disease-free remission and prevent long-term complications. However, clinically appropriate IBD management lays the groundwork for a low but real risk for opportunistic infections such as Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and cytomegalovirus (CMV), as well as risk for lymphomas—all of which can precipitate HLH [4, 5]. In practice, several clinical questions remain unanswered due to the paucity of systematic analyses on this condition. How common is HLH in an IBD population? In what setting should HLH be considered on the differential for IBD patients under typical management, so that it is recognized in an appropriate timeframe to enable prompt treatment? What is the expected outcome of treatment for HLH in an IBD population?

The aim of this study was to characterize HLH occurring among individuals with moderate-to-severe IBD based on systematic review of the literature and the FDA Adverse Events Reporting System (FAERS).

Methods

Literature Search

The conduct of this study adhered to the PRISMA statement [6]. A literature search was conducted (PubMED, EMBASE; 1950–May 2017) to identify case reports and case series of patients with HLH in an IBD population published in abstract form or as a peer-reviewed full manuscript. Our PubMED search string was as follows: (“Colitis, Ulcerative”[Mesh] or “ulcerative colitis” OR “Crohn Disease”[Mesh] OR Crohn* OR IBD OR “inflammatory bowel”) AND (adalimumab OR infliximab OR certolizumab OR natalizumab OR vedolizumab OR golimumab OR biologic OR immunosuppressive OR immunomodulator) AND ((“Lymphoproliferative Disorders”[Mesh] OR lymphoproliferative OR lymphoma OR hepatosplenic) OR (hemophagocyt* OR haemophagocyt* OR “Lymphohistiocytosis, Hemophagocytic”[Mesh]) OR “macrophage activation”). Our EMBASE search string was as follows: ‘crohn disease’/exp OR ‘crohn disease’ OR ‘ulcerative colitis’/exp OR ‘ulcerative colitis’ AND (‘infliximab’/exp OR infliximab OR ‘adalimumab’/exp OR adalimumab OR ‘certolizumab’/exp OR certolizumab OR ‘golimumab’/exp OR golimumab OR ‘natalizumab’/exp OR natalizumab OR ‘vedolizumab’/exp OR vedolizumab) AND (hlh OR ‘hemophagocytic syndrome’/exp OR hemophagocyt* OR haemophagocyt* OR hepatosplenic OR ‘lymphoproliferative disease’/exp OR ‘lymphoproliferative disease’ OR ‘macrophage activation syndrome’). We then conducted a search of the FDA Adverse Events Reporting System (FAERS) which catalogs all reported adverse events that FDA has received for FDA-approved therapies. The use of this reporting system is voluntary for patients and providers, but is a legal requirement for drug manufacturers if they receive a report from the healthcare professional or consumer. FAERS is actively maintained by a dedicated team of investigators at FDA who contact patients and caregivers about clinical history and outcomes when adverse events are reported. The strength of FAERS for this study was its inherent capability of capturing all known events for rare conditions such as HLH with a high level of accuracy and fidelity in an IBD patient population already undergoing on-label treatment for IBD as standard of care. It is important to understand that our study design was not appropriate to evaluate risk of HLH on IBD treatments, as FAERS is not an appropriate resource to conduct a drug risk assessment. Our FAERS search terms included cases related to relevant biologic license application numbers (125057; 125544; 103772; 761054; 125289; 125433; 125160; 125476; 125104; 125261; 761044) referenced to the preferred term “Histiocytosis haematophagic”.

Inclusion Criteria and Data Extraction

Our inclusion criteria were the following: (1) patients must have been diagnosed with IBD, (2) patients must have developed HLH, and (3) patients must have been treated with infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab, golimumab, natalizumab, vedolizumab, or ustekinumab. Given our focus on assessing HLH occurring during usual IBD treatment in line with practice guidelines, we did not assess the patients with moderate-to-severe IBD who were not receiving biologics as part of their care.

Demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, location, disease type (CD or UC), disease phenotype, and associated complications. Exposure to IBD treatments (AZA/6MP, methotrexate [MTX], infliximab [IFX], adalimumab [ADA], certolizumab [CER], golimumab [GOL], natalizumab [NAT], vedolizumab [VED], and ustekinumab [UST]) was categorized by the number of years exposed to therapy, time since last dose, and therapeutic goal (induction vs maintenance of remission). Active infections identified at time of HLH diagnosis were also noted. Treatment and outcomes related to HLH were recorded. FAERS includes all case reports that are included in the literature. In an effort to limit reporting duplicate cases of the same patient, case reports were combined if the age, sex, exposure, and outcome characteristics matched across multiple case reports. Exposure history was reviewed independently by two authors (ES and EC) with discrepancies addressed by consensus among authors.

EBV infection was diagnosed on the basis of a positive polymerase chain reaction, heterophile antibody, or IgM antibody. CMV infection was diagnosed on the basis of a positive polymerase chain reaction, IgM antibody, or intestinal biopsy.

Statistical Analysis

HLH cases were categorized by the current treatment regimen at time of diagnosis, as well as prior exposures to biologics and AZA/6MP. The following designations were used: combination therapy with AZA/6MP, biologic monotherapy with prior AZA/6MP exposure, biologic monotherapy without prior AZA/6MP exposure, and previous exposure to biologics. Within-strata mean, median, range, and standard deviation were calculated for continuous variables. Inter-observer variability in determining therapeutic exposure was calculated using Cohen’s kappa. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA (version 15, STATACorp, College Station, Texas).

Results

In reviewing a total of 2486 abstracts and 182 FAERS reports, we identified 58 unique cases of HLH in patients with IBD who received at least one dose of a biologic medication (Fig. 1). Fifty-two of the cases occurred in patients actively receiving biologic therapy, four were patients with previous biologic therapy, and the remaining two cases had unclear treatment exposure histories. There was high inter-reviewer agreement in assessing medication exposure history (κ statistic = 0.89). Table 1 shows patient demographics and clinical characteristics at the time of HLH diagnosis. The full list of case reports is found in Table 2.

Age and Sex of HLH Cases Among IBD Patients

The median age for patients on combination therapy (biologic and AZA/6MP) was 26.0 years spanning pediatric populations to a maximum of 64 years at time of diagnosis. Comparatively, the median age for patients on biologic monotherapy with prior AZA/6MP exposure was 24 years with an age range of 8–77 years. In contrast, the median age for patients on biologic monotherapy with no prior AZA/6MP exposure was 42.5 years with a wide range of ages spanning pediatric populations to 72 years. 70% of the 57 patients in whom sex was reported were male. 48 patients had CD (82.8%) and ten patients had UC (17.2%).

Disease Extent of HLH Cases Among IBD Patients

Disease extent was reported in 19 patients with CD: four patients had isolated ileal disease (21.1%), seven patients had ileocolonic disease (36.8%), six patients had isolated colonic disease (31.6%), and two patients had isolated upper GI disease (10.5%). The mean duration of IBD prior to HLH diagnosis was 7.0 years. The median duration of IBD was 4.5 years with an age range of 0.33–24 years.

Intent of Biologic Therapy (Induction or Maintenance) at the Time of HLH Diagnosis

At the time of HLH diagnosis, 20 patients (34.5%) were undergoing induction therapy with biologics, 30 patients (51.7%) were on their biologic for maintenance therapy, and four patients (6.9%) were not on biologics at the time of HLH diagnosis. The indication was unknown for five patients (8.6%). The distribution of indications for biologic therapy is shown in Fig. 2, stratified by concomitant immunomodulatory usage.

HLH Among IBD Patients Receiving Biologics, with Previous Exposure to AZA/6MP or Methotrexate

Of the 58 total cases, 39 (67.2%) reported at least some prior exposure to AZA/6MP with an average AZA/6MP exposure of 2.33 years prior to HLH diagnosis. 31 (79.5%) of the 39 cases were reported to be on combination therapy with a biologic agent at the time of HLH diagnosis, with a median AZA/6MP exposure of 3.5 years in this cohort. Among patients receiving combination therapy, the biologic was being used as an induction agent in 13 cases (42.0%) when HLH developed and for maintenance of remission in 17 cases (54.8%) with an uncertain indication in one case (3.2%). Of the remaining 8 cases, 4 (50.0%) were on biologics at the time of HLH diagnosis with a history of reported AZA/6MP use. The other 4 patients were taking AZA/6MP monotherapy (and reporting prior biologic use) at the time of HLH diagnosis, with an average AZA/6MP exposure time of 1.25 years in this cohort.

Six patients reported exposure to methotrexate, with four patients actively receiving methotrexate at the time of HLH diagnosis. The duration of exposure was reported for two patients, each receiving approximately one month of methotrexate before HLH diagnosis. All six patients had reported exposure to infliximab, two patients were exposed to adalimumab, one patient exposed to golimumab, and four patients had received prior azathioprine.

HLH Among IBD Patients Receiving Biologics, with No Prior Exposure to AZA/6MP

18 patients (31.0%) of the total 58 had reported biologic use at time of HLH diagnosis, but never reported any exposure to AZA/6MP. In one patient, the therapy at time of diagnosis was unknown. The average age of these patients was 40.1 years at the time of diagnosis, ranging from pediatric populations to 72 years. 14 patients had CD (77.8%) and four had UC (22.2%). Eleven patients were male (61.1%).

Nine patients were on infliximab therapy at the time of HLH diagnosis, with an average length of exposure of 1.1 years. One patient was on tacrolimus at the same time as infliximab. The minimum reported time of exposure to infliximab prior to HLH diagnosis was two weeks.

Eight patients were on adalimumab with an average drug exposure of six months prior to HLH diagnosis. One patient had a reported prior exposure to infliximab. The minimum time of exposure to adalimumab prior to HLH diagnosis was two weeks.

One patient had received ustekinumab for three months at the time of HLH diagnosis.

Stratification of HLH Cases by Biologic Class

Among anti-tumor necrosis factor agents, 41 of the 58 total patients (70.7%) were exposed to infliximab with an average exposure period of 1.16 years prior to HLH diagnosis. 23 cases (39.7%) reported exposure to adalimumab for an average duration of 0.34 years. One male patient with CD had a prior exposure to certolizumab for a duration of 3.75 years. This patient also had known prior exposure to both infliximab and adalimumab for unknown durations. At the time of his HLH diagnosis, he was receiving natalizumab for six months in combination with azathioprine.

Among anti- α4β7 integrin agents, one female patient with CD had reported vedolizumab exposure for a duration of four months prior to HLH diagnosis. She had also reported prior exposure to natalizumab for 1.17 years, as well as prior exposure to infliximab and azathioprine for unknown durations. At the time of her HLH diagnosis she was taking vedolizumab, methotrexate for a duration of six weeks, and cyclosporine. This patient along with the patient on certolizumab (previous paragraph) were the only two patients in the study who reported a previous exposure to natalizumab. Golimumab exposure of unknown duration was reported in one male patient with UC. The patient had a previous exposure to adalimumab for unknown duration as well. At the time of HLH diagnosis, the patient was on infliximab for unknown duration and had also been on methotrexate for two weeks.

One male patient with CD was exposed to ustekinumab (an anti-interleukin 12 and 23 agent) for three months years prior to HLH diagnosis. This patient did not report any history of exposure to other biologics or immunomodulators.

Opportunistic Infections and Malignancies as Identifiable Risk Factors for Developing HLH

Opportunistic infection or malignancy was found in > 80% of all known IBD patients with at least some biologic exposure who developed HLH (Fig. 3). 15 cases of HLH occurred in patients with active EBV infection, eleven were associated with active CMV infection, eight with active histoplasmosis, and seven with active tuberculosis (TB).

In patients whose biologic therapies were reported with active EBV infection, nine reported exposures to infliximab, 3 to adalimumab, and 1 to natalizumab. For CMV infection, six patients had exposure to infliximab and 3 patients had exposure to adalimumab. Six patients with histoplasmosis had exposure to infliximab and two patients had exposure to adalimumab.

Concomitant lymphoma was identified in an additional six HLH cases in whom an opportunistic infection was not present. Reported biologic exposures in this group included one patient on infliximab, three patients on adalimumab, and one patient on ustekinumab.

Outcomes for IBD Patients Who Developed HLH

Outcomes were reported in 50 patients (86.2%) of which 15 (30.0%) reported the outcome as death. The average survival time after HLH diagnosis was 21.2 days among individuals who died as a result of HLH, with the minimum survival time being 11.0 days and the maximum survival time being 61.0 days.

Discussion

HLH is a serious and often deadly condition characterized by immune overactivation which requires prompt recognition and emergent treatment. IBD patients are theoretically at risk, given that appropriate treatment leads to an immunocompromised and susceptible state toward developing opportunistic infections and malignancies often associated with secondary or acquired HLH. Despite the current era of upfront biologics and combination therapy necessary to prevent long-term complications of moderate-to-severe IBD in clinical practice guidelines, there is a paucity of systematic reporting on HLH in an IBD population in the literature.

We found a total of 58 cases of HLH occurring in patients who reported exposure to biologic therapies for their IBD, suggesting that HLH is thankfully a rare condition. In most cases, an opportunistic infection or lymphoma was identified. The survival rate was 70% if promptly treated. HLH cases were reported among all marketed classes of biologics spanning a variety of drug mechanisms, suggesting that HLH may not be related to biologics in themselves, but to the immunocompromised nature of moderate-to-severe IBD. Interestingly, a higher number of patients developed HLH during the induction phase of biologic therapy than might be expected compared to chronic maintenance treatment, consistent with a higher risk of opportunistic infection during periods of rapid immunosuppression, suggesting that HLH should at least be considered on the differential diagnosis especially during induction therapy.

HLH is associated with a high mortality rate and requires prompt recognition and treatment. Our study found that HLH occurred more often in males (70.0%) with a median age of 31 years and in patients with CD (82.8%). The median disease duration of IBD at time of HLH diagnosis was 4.5 years. Forty-one of the 58 patients (70.7%) in this review had underlying infections that have been well recognized in their association with HLH. EBV and CMV are the most common infectious etiologies of HLH, found in patients with IBD whom are being treated with immunosuppressive therapy. There have also been reported associations with histoplasmosis, mycobacterial infections, as well as other various microbes [7]. The pathogenesis of secondary HLH due to viral illness is thought to be due to the high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-a, IL-12, etc.) generated by clonal proliferation of virally infected T lymphocytes. The elevation in cytokine levels stimulates macrophage activation, which then leads to hemophagocytosis [8]. Despite the prevalence of EBV and CMV infections among individuals with HLH, the overall prevalence of HLH likely remains low even in a highly immunocompromised patient population. Thus, the value of routine CMV and EBV serologies even in high-risk populations remains uncertain [9, 10].

HLH can be rapidly fatal without specific therapy, thus early recognition and treatment is crucial. This was apparent in our analysis where the average survival time after HLH diagnosis in patients in who died, was just 21.2 days. The treatment usually includes etoposide, rituximab, dexamethasone, and eventually hematopoietic stem cell transplant [3]. In secondary HLH, treatment of the underlying cause (i.e., anti-fungal or anti-mycobacterial therapy, etc.) is also indicated. With effective treatment, survival rates at 5 years range from 50 to 70% consistent with current findings in the IBD population [11].

For patients who recover from HLH and any underlying infection, the safety of restarting immunosuppressive therapy is largely unknown. In our study, five patients were restarted on biologics after recovering from HLH. All were restarted on anti-TNF therapy with the exception of one patient who was switched from infliximab pre-HLH to ustekinumab post-HLH. One patient later developed recurrent HLH after resuming biologic therapy.

The primary strength of our study was the up-to-date search of the literature and FAERS database, which to our knowledge encompasses the largest cohort of reported HLH cases in patients with IBD who had either prior or current exposure to biologic therapy. While FAERS is a drug-based database of adverse events, this was appropriate for our study given that moderate-to-severe IBD is treated with on-label biologic therapy as standard of care in current practice. In contrast, treating patients with moderate-to-severe IBD with therapies other than biologics, such as chronic steroid therapy, immunomodulator monotherapy, or no therapy, is not standard of care in most circumstances. For rare conditions like HLH, the use of FAERS lends a high level of accuracy toward ensuring that included cases actually had HLH and that all known cases are likely identified. Furthermore, there is a paucity of literature on this topic. One systematic review on this topic was performed in 2013, however SONIC to support combination therapy was conducted only three years earlier in 2010 and UC-SUCCESS was published one year later in 2014 [12–14]. While identifying 50 case reports at that time, it is not clear whether cases review was verified by a second author or how duplicate cases were adjudicated. Furthermore, FAERS was not included which identified a significant number of cases which are unavailable in the literature in the current study.

There are several important limitations to consider when interpreting our study findings. While FAERS is maintained by FDA as a database intended to characterize adverse events on FDA-regulated drugs, our study can neither evaluate risk of HLH on any specific IBD treatment nor was it designed to do so. First, FAERS is insufficient for assessing causation of potential adverse events [15]. Second, our study did not include a control arm for immunomodulators, corticosteroids alone, or no treatment because these treatment strategies are no longer consistent with standard practice. Furthermore, a likely causative opportunistic infection or lymphoma was found in most cases and among a variety of biologic classes and mechanisms, suggesting that the rapidity of concentrated immunosuppressive agents necessary in controlling disease may be a more important driving factor toward developing HLH, compared to any specific agent. Finally, we used biologic therapy as a surrogate for moderate-to-severe IBD due to its consistency with clinical practice guideline recommendations toward managing IBD and enabling use of FAERS to identify rare HLH cases with high fidelity, understanding that rare events such as HLH do not lend themselves toward systematic assessments of disease severity using patient-reported outcomes, or serologic, endoscopic, or histologic markers typical of clinical trials. Specifically, FAERS is insufficient to characterize the baseline risk of HLH in IBD with or without biologic therapy because the use of this database requires that potential adverse events be tied to specific drugs (such as biologics) rather than to specific diseases like IBD. Clinical trials are insufficient to assess HLH risk on biologics due to their low power in assessing rare events. Claims datasets remain insufficient to assess HLH risk because the accuracy of HLH coding is likely affected by misdiagnosis (both over-diagnosis and under-diagnosis in practice). Due to these limitations of existing datasets in performing a baseline risk assessment for HLH, whether use of biologics or disease severity alter the risk of HLH remains unknown and cannot be informed by our assessment. Ultimately, given the number of total known HLH cases in an enriched population of IBD patients receiving biologic therapy, the annual risk of HLH in IBD overall is likely to be very low (< 0.1% or less) based on similar case report numbers and estimates of HSTCL risk in IBD [16].

In summary, HLH remains a possible but thankfully a rare event in managing moderate-to-severe IBD. Risk factors may include younger age, male gender, presence of Crohn’s disease, and induction phase of treatment. Prompt recognition, identification of an underlying cause, and emergent treatment are required to prevent death. Our findings suggest that HLH should be especially considered during early treatment for active moderate-to-severe IBD.
