Cutoff Points for Grip Strength in Screening for Sarcopenia in Community-Dwelling Older-Adults: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Background

Currently, different cutoff points for handgrip strength (HGS) have been used to estimate the prevalence of sarcopenia. In addition, the variability of equipment and protocols for this assessment can significantly influence the early detection of this important public health problem. Thus, this review aims to identify the different cutoff points for HGS adopted for older men and women in screening for sarcopenia.

Objectives

this review aims to identify the different cutoff points for HGS adopted for older men and women in screening for sarcopenia. Methods: In accordance with the PRISMA 2020 recommendations, which included published studies from the last 10 years, from 6 databases, in 3 different languages.

Results

19.730 references were identified, of which 62 were included for the review. All references analyzed used algorithms and definitions of sarcopenia already known in the literature. Of the studies found, 16 chose to develop cutoff values for HGS based on their own population. The variation in cutoff points was evident when compared between gender and regions of the world.

Conclusion

It has become evident that there is a variability of normative values for HGS in sarcopenia screening. In addition, this systematic review shows the difference in the cutoff points used between the consensuses and those developed for each population.

Introduction

Sarcopenia, a commonly used concept in geriatrics and gerontology (1), is defined as a musculoskeletal disorder characterized by a reduction in skeletal muscle mass and strength, which can lead to a progressive and generalized reduction in muscle function (2). Sarcopenia is a major clinical problem in public health (3), being associated with adverse health results. For example, sarcopenia increases the risk of falls and fractures, cardiac and respiratory diseases, impairs the ability to perform daily living activities, in addition to impairment in mobility and loss of independence (4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9).

Sarcopenia can be identified by assessing three criteria: 1) Low muscle strength, 2) Low muscle quantity/quality, and 3) Low physical performance (2). For the identification of these items, a wide range of tools can be used, among them, the handgrip strength (HGS) assessment is the recommended technique for measuring muscle strength and is the simplest method of assessing muscle function in clinical practice (10).

Low HGS is a powerful predictor of adverse outcomes and it correlates with the strength of the whole body (11). Therefore, HGS could be a reliable substitute for more complicated measures of the strength of the lower and upper limbs (12, 13). A literature review on the assessment of HGS has shown that there is variability in the choice of equipment and protocol (14). Additionally, different cutoff values are found for the HGS (10, 15). Due to this, different limits of cutoff points will lead to differences in the estimated prevalence of sarcopenia (16). Studies show that racial diversity, body size, lifestyle differences, and socioeconomic status are also important obstacles for standardizing measurements of HGS (13, 17).

The original European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) consensus (1) paper did not advise specific cutoff points for HGS, and disputes over cutoff points have hampered research and development in the field of geriatrics due to lack of study consistency. Currently, EWGSOP2 (2) has opted to provide recommendations for cutoff points for different parameters to increase harmonization of sarcopenia studies. Because the current EWGSOP2 recommendations focus on the European population (healthy young adults), it is important to identify which cutoff values for HGS are used to screen for sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults living in different places (15, 18).

Thus far, few reviews (14, 19, 20) have sought to identify the different methods and protocols for the assessment of HGS in sarcopenia screening; however, none of them has had the main focus on identifying the cutoff points that were used. In view of this gap in the literature, this systematic review aimed to summarize the cutoff points used to identify low muscle strength in community-dwelling older people in the screening for sarcopenia with HGS. In addition, this work will help us to answer two important questions: 1) «What cutoff points are adopted for older men and women?» and 2) “What is the prevalence of sarcopenia found in a specific population when using different cutoff points?”.

Methods

The protocol for this review was registered in the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42020187727) and followed the recommendations of PRISMA 2020 (21). The research question that conducted this review, which was based on the adapted PICO acronym, was: “Which cutoff points for low HGS are used in screening for sarcopenia in community-dwelling older people?”.

Eligibility criteria

Observational studies published in the last 10 years so that only those studies that used some of the consensus for the screening and diagnosis of sarcopenia were considered, and in 3 different languages (English, Portuguese and Spanish). Studies were selected whose main objective is to carry out the screening of primary sarcopenia in the older population, considering, during the assessment of muscle strength, the use of measurement of HGS through the manual dynamometer. In addition, studies that examined community-dwelling older people (≥60 years) of both genders were selected. Studies in which the study population had any chronic conditions (such as dementia, Parkinson's disease, stroke, diabetes mellitus, frailty, obesity, rheumatoid arthritis or physical impairments that may affect the measurement of HGS test) associated with sarcopenia were excluded. Studies that used other diagnostic methods for sarcopenia, and studies where the assessment of sarcopenia was not the main focus, were discarded. Studies such as randomized clinical trials, systematic reviews, congress abstracts, theses and dissertations were also excluded.

Search strategy and research information

The search was conducted in six databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Scientific Electronic Library Online (SCIELO), Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (LILACS), The Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) and Embase, considering the periods from January 2012 to August 2021.

The search strategy was developed using the Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) descriptors, considering the following standardized formula for all databases: (Elderly OR “older adults”) AND community-dwelling AND sarcopenia. Details of the search strategy can be found in the supplementary material 01.

Selection process

After searching the databases, the results were exported to Rayyan® (Qatar Computing Research Institute — Data Analytics, Doha, Qatar), a web-based software that facilitates collaboration between reviewers during the study selection process. Training exercises were carried out for members of the study group who were not familiar with the software.

First, duplicate references were excluded and then two independent reviewers (EKRS and PRSM) assessed possible eligibility by reading titles and abstracts. Afterwards, the full text was evaluated to confirm eligibility, also by two reviewers independently. Disagreements at any stage of the selection process were resolved by another reviewer (SGGF).

Data collection process

Data from the included articles were extracted by a reviewer and checked by a second examiner. At this stage, a standardized form was used in Excel® that contained information on author, year of publication, country of origin, study design, population characteristics, sample size, mean age of participants, sarcopenia diagnostic criteria, cutoff points used for handgrip strength and prevalence of sarcopenia.

Methodological quality

The quality of the studies was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute's (JBI) assessment tools and recommendations. This assessment aimed to analyze the methodological quality of the studies and determine the extent to which a study addressed the possibility of bias in its design, conduct and analysis. In this review, two checklists were considered, one for cross-sectional studies, containing 8 questions, and the second, containing 11 questions, was intended for cohort studies (22).

Each study was categorized according to the percentage of positive responses. The risk of bias was considered high when the study obtained up to 49% of the answers classified as yes, moderate when it obtained 50 to 69% of positive answers, and low when the study reached more than 70% of the score (23). The process was conducted by two independent reviewers (PRSM and SGGF). Disagreements were resolved by another reviewer (EKRS).

Characteristics of selected studies

62 studies were selected, of which 48 cross-sectional and 14 longitudinal studies. All studies were carried out with community older people with a mean age of at least 65 to 89 years old., comprising a sample of 73.798 participants. Among them, six studies were performed only with women (29, 35, 38, 50, 52, 77) and one with men only 36(Pereira et al., 2015). As for places of publication, most studies were published in Asia and South America (Figure 2).

Diagnostic criteria

All studies used sarcopenia algorithms and definitions already known in the literature. The EWGSOP1 (1Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2010) was used in 36 studies, while the AWSG1 (85) in 16 and the FNIH criteria (86) in 7. The updates of the EWGSOP (2) and AWSG (87) were used in 10 and 6 studies, respectively. Among these studies, nine used more than one consensus to define sarcopenia (27, 31, 39, 43, 50, 55, 64, 65, 71). More details can be found in table 1.

Cutoff points for HGS and development methods

After extracting data, the variation in cutoff values found in the studies was evident. Figure 03 summarizes the highest and lowest values established, according to region and gender. It is possible to see that all observed values are different from those established in the consensus, making it clear that such values were calculated using as a base the reference population of each location where the studies were carried out.

A total of 46 selected studies used cutoff points already available in the consensus to verify the low muscle strength of individuals, while 16 studies chose to use cutoff points based on their own population, which were developed using methods such as: first quartile (33, 55, 61, 65, 73), 20 or 25 percentile (29, 31, 38, 66, 72), −2.5 standard deviation of the mean (27) and regression tree -CTree- (43). Of these, 12 had lower cutoff values than what is established in the consensuses (1, 2, 85, 86, 87), for both men and women. Only the study by Bachettine et al., (27) found higher cutoff points when compared to the consensuses.

When analyzing the cutoff values according to sex, it was observed that in all the studies women had lower cutoff points than men, as expected. When comparing the studies that developed their own cutoff points and those that used those recommended by the consensus (1, 2, 85, 86, 87), we saw that the study by Fernandes et al., (31) established a value equal to those suggested in the EWGSOP2 and FNIH. The study by Shafiee et al., (59) identified values above the recommended in the EWGSOP2 and FNIH yet equal to those established for the AWGS1 and AWGS2. Nonetheless, women still had lower cutoff values than men (59).

Among the 36 studies that used the EWGSOP1 algorithm, 11 preferred to use the cutoff points for HGS based on body index mass (HGS/BMI) (16, 33, 34, 36, 43, 50, 66, 75, 76, 84), while the others opted for the method established by Fried et al., (88) (HGS/gait speed= 20 and 30kg). However, only the studies by Beaudart et al., (16), Moreira et al., (33) and Pagotto and Silveira (34) focused on comparing these two cut-off methods that are presented in the EWGSOP1 (HGS/BMI and HGS/GS) and it was observed that the prevalence of sarcopenia is lower when using muscle strength based on BMI.

Prevalence of sarcopenia

The prevalence of sarcopenia ranged from 2.4% to 35.05%. Observing this variable according to gender, 9 studies show that there is a higher prevalence among women, while 18 studies indicate a higher frequency of sarcopenia among men.

Eight studies (27, 31, 33, 55, 59, 66, 72) provided the prevalence of sarcopenia using the HGS cutoff points established by the consensuses and using those developed for specific populations. Of these, two studies (43 and 66) showed that the prevalence was higher when cutoff points specific for the study population were used, while the others studies (27, 33, 55 and 72) presented the opposite result, demonstrating that the prevalences were higher when the cutoff points established in the consensuses were used.

Only the study by Fernandes et al. (31) shows that even using a cutoff point that is specific to the population studied, the prevalence can still vary significantly depending on the decision-making algorithm that will be used. In this study, it was seen that when using the EWGSOP2 algorithm, with specific cutoff points for the population studied, the prevalence was higher in men, compared to the values found using the original cutoff point (2). When using the FNIH algorithm, the prevalence of sarcopenia for both genders was lower than that observed when using the original cutoff values of the consensus.

Methodological quality

When evaluating the methodological quality and risk of bias of the selected references, according to the study design, it is observed that most articles -32- have a low risk of bias, with percentages of positive responses ranging from 72.7 % to 100%. Meanwhile, 26 studies — of which 25 were cross-sectional — were classified as having a moderate risk of bias, and 4 studies (42, 45, 55, 67) were classified as having a high risk of bias, with scores ranging between 3 and 5, representing only 37.5% and 45.4% of positive criteria. Discussion

This review aimed to identify the cutoff points for HGS used in screening for sarcopenia in community-dwelling older people from different locations. The heterogeneity and variability of the cutoff points used to identify low muscle strength with the HGS became evident, and, consequently, sarcopenia as well. Similar to these findings, a systematic review carried out by Dodds et al., (20), which aimed to investigate differences in grip strength by region of the world using specific data as a reference standard and found considerable variations in cutoff points for HGS in all regions of the world, thus highlighting the need to establish cutoff values that are widely applicable for both men and women.

There are some explanations about the heterogeneity of cutoff values available in the literature. Previous studies show that grip strength varies according to gender, race, method used for measurement, body composition and population studied (52, 89, 90). In addition, the available consensuses contribute to this variability, since in a survey conducted by Lee and Gong (91) it was observed that each sarcopenia consensus behaves differently regarding the HGS reference values, presenting different statistical methods for the development of these cutoff values and different conceptual frameworks (71).

While the FNIH uses more advanced statistical methods (regression tree), like the one carried out by Choe et al., (43), the other consensuses make use of simpler analyses. The EWGSOP1, the most used among the studies found, presents two different ways to define the cutoff points for HGS: the first is based on the gait speed of 1.030 individuals aged 20–102 years living in Italy, identified through the ROC curve (3); the second method is established through the BMI quartiles of the older over 65 years old from the Cardiovascular Health Study (88). Therefore, it is possible to observe that within EWGSOP1consensual definition (1), different cutoff points are recommended, which could lead to differences in sarcopenia estimates. According to Beaudart et al., (16), the use of threshold values for HGS developed from gait speed for both genders (20kg and 30kg) can lead to an estimated prevalence of sarcopenia of 15%, while values dependent on the BMI lead to lower prevalence. This result is similar to what was found in this review.

In the update of the EWGSOP (2), grip strength was considered as the main parameter of sarcopenia, with changes in cutoff values, identified through the T-score of −2.5*standard deviation of a reference sample, setting now values lower than those used previously for both genders (27kg and 16kg). However, in clinical practice, it is important to know whether a single cutoff point can be applied in different contexts (20).

Analyzing the cutoff values according to continents, it was possible to observe different cutoff points in studies carried out in Asia. Although the AWGS have suggested values for the HGS, it is still common to find divergence even between countries of the same region. An example of this can be seen in the studies by Choe et al., (43), Moon et al., (54), Moon et al., (55), Kim et al., (51) and Lee et al., (52) that evaluated the presence of sarcopenia in Koreans, however only the studies by Choe et al., (43) and Moon et al., (55) used specific cutoff points for this population, developed using statistical methods different from other studies (regression tree and lower quantile), while the others chose to use the values already established in the literature (1, 85, 87).

We verified that countries located in developing regions have lower grip strength measurements, an example of which is the values found in Brazil (11kg and 16kg). Thus, strengthening the hypothesis that differences in ethnic, cultural, social and lifestyle, body size and composition (weight and height) influence the cutoff values. This can be seen in the study by Fernandes et al., (2021) who found cut-off values different from those developed in a context of medium and high income, even within the same country. In a study by Koopman et al., (92) which compared grip strength values in the Netherlands and Ghana and found that participants residing in the Netherlands (developed region) had greater grip strength compared to those residing in Ghana.

We also observed little variation in cutoff values among developed countries, especially those carried out in Europe, corroborating the results analyzed by Bohannon et al., (93) who combined normative values for handgrip of countries in developed regions and found no evidence of heterogeneity.

In an analysis made by a previous study (20) it was seen that when using the values recommended by the EWGSOP2 in the British population, approximately 25% of them had lower grip strength, however, if these values were applied in populations residing in developing countries the prevalence of muscle weakness could reach 60%. In view of this, it is suggested that the values established by the EWGSOP2 may not be specific enough for other regions, strengthening the need to develop normative values according to the population studied.

Only the study by Bachettini et al. (27) used values slightly higher (<29.7kg for man and <16.2 for women) than those available in the consensuses (EWGSOP2, FNIH and AWGS) for both genders. However, this is probably due to the population in which this cutoff point was developed, as unlike other studies included in this review, the Bachettini et al. (27) cutoff values were established using a young reference population (30 years old), based on in the study of Bielemann et al., (94)

When comparing the cutoff values between the gender, it is possible to notice that women have lower values when compared to men, both in the consensuses already established in the literature and in studies that developed cut-off values specific to their population. This can be explained by the fact that women naturally have a lower handgrip strength due to several factors, such as hormonal changes, since during the menopausal transition phase, the concentration of steroids reduces drastically. This influenced the muscle strength of this population group (31, 95, 96).

Regarding the prevalence, it was possible to observe a variation between 2.4 and 35.05%, which corroborates with previous reviews that report a prevalence rate ranging from 1.0% to 50% in community-dwelling older people around the world (24, 73). Regarding gender, we found 17 studies that reported a higher prevalence in men, while 9 indicated females had higher prevalence. This conflicting result corroborates with a systematic review carried out by Shafiee et al., (96) that shows that the association between sarcopenia and sex is still inconsistent, as some studies report a higher prevalence among men, while others show opposite results. In addition, the classification of sarcopenia and, consequently, its prevalence varies according to the methods used, the cutoff points chosen, and the population studied (89, 97).

Therefore, in this review, it became clear the variability of cut-off values for HGS used in screening for sarcopenia in community-dwelling older inserted in different contexts. What can be attributed to cultural, ethnic, social and lifestyle differences between individuals (98). In addition, the absence of a consensus for the assessment of HGS in epidemiological studies contributes to this divergence of values. Therefore, it is important to know which cutoff points are available in the literature and how they can influence the screening for sarcopenia depending on the region where this older person is located, so that it is possible to compare the results between different locations.

Strengths and limitations

This review has important limitations. First, to the absence of longitudinal studies that aimed to verify the sensitivity of the cutoff values specifically developed for each population, in the long term, and their capacity to detect adverse health outcomes. The second limitation is related to the fact that it was not possible to develop a subgroup analysis in this systematic review, so information on cutoff points in older people with specific impairments was not compared. Finally, we can also highlight the absence, in this review, of information on the diagnostic accuracy metrics of the handgrip tests, since this information was not present in most of the selected studies, therefore, it was not possible to analyze and compare which points cutoff (standard and population-specific values) perform worse or better in screening for sarcopenia. As for the strengths, this is the first review that aims to carry out a survey on the cutoff points of HGS used to identify sarcopenia in community-dwelling older people. Furthermore, it was also possible to discuss important topics related to the development of the limits of values and which factors can influence its variability. This systematic review shows the differences in the classification of sarcopenia when using the cutoff points from the consensuses and using those developed for each population, in addition to allowing the comparison of cutoff values between genders and regions of the world, allowing us to observe the differences between developed regions and in development.

Conclusion

There is variability of normative values for HGS in the screening of sarcopenia used in epidemiological studies around the world. Additionally, it became clear the absence of protocols and consensus regarding the measurement of this component, since details regarding the position adopted during the test, which dynamometer used, which hand chosen, among other aspects, are absent in most studies, and these different protocols can lead to changes in HGS measurements, thus influencing the screening of sarcopenia.

Therefore, this review shows the need to use specific values for different regions, highlighting the importance of carrying out studies that aim to develop cut-off values that take into account the particularities of the older population around the world, since the use of cut-off points based on different populations can underestimate or overestimate the prevalence values found.
