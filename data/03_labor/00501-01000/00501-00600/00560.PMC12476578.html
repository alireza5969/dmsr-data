Abstract

Background:

Unintentional injuries constitute the leading causes of death and long-term disabilities among children aged 5–15 years old.

Objective:

We aimed to systematically review published literature on interventions designed to prevent unintentional injuries among school-aged children (5–15 years old).

Data Sources:

We searched Medline, PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Cumulative Index to Nursing & Allied Health Literature, and PsycINFO and screened the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews.

Study Selection:

We included randomized controlled trials, controlled before and after studies and interrupted time series. We included studies focusing on primary prevention measures.

Data Extraction:

Two reviewers collected data on type of study design, setting, population, intervention, types of injuries, outcomes assessed, and statistical results.

Results:

Out of 30,179 identified studies, we included 117 articles. Most of the studies were conducted in high-income countries and assessed interventions addressing traffic-related injuries. Evidence from included studies suggests that multi-component educational interventions may be effective in improving safety knowledge, attitudes and behaviors in school-age children mainly when coupled with other approaches. Laws/legislations were shown to be effective in increasing cycle helmet use and reducing traffic-related injury rates. Findings highlight the relevance of infrastructure modification in reducing falls and improving pedestrian safety among children.

Conclusion:

Further studies are needed to evaluate the impact of unintentional injury prevention interventions on injury, hospitalizations and mortality rates. This review warrants future studies to comprehensively evaluate the impact of laws/legislations and infrastructure modification on preventing unintentional injuries among school-aged children.

Background

Unintentional injuries are among the leading causes of death and long-term disabilities for children aged 5–14.1 Unintentional injuries refer to injuries with no predetermined intent and include road traffic injuries, burns, drowning, poisoning, falls, suffocation and sports related injuries.2 These injuries are responsible for countless premature mortalities and disabilities among young children ranging in intensity from short-term to long-term disabilities and manifested in many years of life lost (YLL).3 The latest estimates from the Global Burden of Disease report (2017) showed that unintentional injuries accounted for almost 7.9% of total Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) for children aged 5–14 with road traffic injuries claiming a high share of 4%.4

Injury accounts for more than 875,000 children deaths every year, with nearly 95% occurring in low and middle-income countries (LMICs).5 A plausible explanation for this disparity in the global burden of injury rests in the understanding of the injury’s contributing risk factors such as socioeconomic status, education level, occupation, and income level.6 A steep social gradient exists in child’s exposure to injuries. Families’ socioeconomic status has a reverse association with children’s elevated risk of sustaining injuries as well as suffering more severe and fatal injuries7. Nearly 10% of all deaths occurring in LMICs are due to unintentional injuries.8 Further to its human tragedy, the economic burden of unintentional injuries is high. Road traffic injuries alone is responsible for a Gross Domestic Product annual decrease by 1 to 3 percent9, costing between 0.3% and 5% of Gross National Product. The substantial cost of injuries in LMICs adversely affects the population’s health and productivity, resulting in increased strains on countries’ economies and limited resourced health care systems.9

Child unintentional injury remains a real threat to child’s health and a substantial and persistent global health problem. To the authors’ knowledge, previous attempts to systematically review published literature for unintentional injuries prevention lack focus on school-aged children 5–15 years old3,10–15 and comprehensiveness in addressing multiple types of injuries, interventions and settings. Previous reviews either focused on studies related to a single injury such as burns16, poisoning17, drowning18,19, or pedestrian injuries20,21, or on specific settings such as homes22,23, or outdoors24 or targeted a particular setting such as community interventions25, school-based interventions26 or interventions in clinical settings.27 This systematic review aims to fill the knowledge gap by examining a range of interventions addressing multiple types of injuries and in a variety of settings among school-aged children (5–15 years old),. Focusing on this age group in specific can provide valuable evidence for those designing targeted interventions given the difference in children’s risk-taking behaviors and health beliefs and practices by age28. Knowledge gained from this systematic review of literature will also inform injury prevention policies and decisions on interventions that mitigate the burden of unintentional injuries among school-aged children.

Methods

Literature Search Strategy

We searched electronic databases including Medline, PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Cumulative Index to Nursing & Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and PsycINFO up to December 2020 without restrictions for language or date. Supplement 1 presents the search strategies for the electronic databases searched. Additionally, we screened the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews.

Eligibility criteria

We included studies involving school-age children 5–15 years old, parents and caregivers of school-age children. Studies with overlapping age groups (e.g. 0–8 or 10–19 years) and lacking data on school-age children were excluded. We focused on interventions or programs aimed at preventing all types of injuries among school-age children including drowning, burns, falls, poisonings, and road injuries (i.e. vehicle, pedestrian and bicycle). Single or multi-component interventions involving any of the eligible injury interventions were included. We included studies that addressed primary measures that prevent injuries while excluded secondary and tertiary prevention measures that aim to reduce the injury impact29. We included studies conducted in home, school and community settings as well as studies conducted in indoor and outdoor settings. We excluded interventions aiming at preventing injuries in hospital settings (e.g. falls in hospitals settings) and studies related to sports injuries. We included randomized controlled studies, controlled before and after studies and interrupted time series and excluded abstracts, meetings proceedings, editorials and commentaries. We included outcomes such as behavior change, observed safety practices and skills, and safety knowledge in addition to injury and mortality rates. Comparison/control groups considered are no intervention, other interventions or the same interventions delivered at a different level of intensity. We used standardized and pilot tested forms and conducted calibration exercises to ensure the validity of the selection and data abstraction processes.

Selection process

Teams of two reviewers (LBK, MJ, AH, RF, and NH) screened titles and abstracts of identified citations in duplicate and independently for potential eligibility. We retrieved the full text for citations judged as potentially eligible by at least one of the two reviewers.

Full-text screening was conducted in duplicate and independently by teams of two reviewers (LBK, MJ, AH, RF, and NH). The teams resolved disagreement by discussion or with the help of a third reviewer. We used standardized and pilot tested screening forms.

Data abstraction process

Teams of two reviewers (LBK, MJ, AH, RF, and NH) abstracted data from eligible studies into a abstraction form that was developed for this purpose. We used standardized and pilot tested data abstraction form. We collected data including study author(s), title, year, type of study design, country and its income level according to the World Bank list of July 2019, population (e.g. age range, sample size), characteristics of the intervention (e.g. setting, type of intervention), type of injury(ies), outcomes assessed, key findings, and statistical results. We conducted calibration exercises to ensure the validity of the data abstraction process.

Risk of bias assessment

One reviewer assessed the risk of bias in each study and one reviewer validated results. We used the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool to assess the risk of bias in randomized trials and the Cochrane Effective Practice and Organization of Care (EPOC) risk of bias criteria for controlled before and after studies and interrupted time series. We conducted calibration exercises to ensure the validity of the risk of bias assessment.

Data synthesis

We calculated the agreement between reviewers for the assessment of study eligibility at the full text screening stage using Fleiss’ Kappa statistics. We used the following values to judge the degree of agreement: 0.21–0.40 for fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 for moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 for substantial agreement and 0.81–1.00 for almost perfect agreement30.

For the quantitative analysis, we planned to conduct a meta-analysis. However, we were not able to pool the data into a meta-analysis because of the differences and heterogeneity in the types of interventions, the types of outcomes and measurement methods across studies. Therefore, we reported the results narratively. We also tabulated study characteristics, interventions, outcome measures, key results for all primary studies to aid in narrative synthesis.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 summarizes the selection process. Out of 30,179 citations identified from electronic databases and 201 studies retrieved from references screening of relevant systematic reviews, we included 117 articles31–147. At the full text screening, we excluded 543 articles for the following reasons: not population of interest (n=362), not study design of interest (n=160) and not intervention of interest (n=19) and missing full text (n=2). Supplement 2 provides the list of excluded studies with reasons for exclusion. The level of agreement between the two reviewers at the full text screening phase was substantial (Kappa= 0.777; 95% CI: 0.717 – 0.837).

Characteristics of Included Studies

Of the included 117 studies, the majority were Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) (53.8%) and Controlled Before and After (CBA) studies (42.7%) while 4 studies employed Interrupted Time Series (ITS) (3.4%). The majority of the studies were conducted in high-income countries (86.3%), specifically the United States (40.1%), Canada (15.3%) and United Kingdom (11.1%). The types of injuries mostly examined were road injuries (75.2%) followed by burns (18.8%) and falls (18.8%), with the least types being poisoning (8.5%) and drowning (5.9%). The majority of the studies evaluated the effectiveness of educational interventions (91.4%) while few studies assessed policies, laws and legislations and infrastructure and engineering interventions. Knowledge, attitudes and behavior were the main outcomes assessed (91.4%) while 12.1% of the included studies assessed the effectiveness of interventions on injury rates. (Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of included studies).

Assessment of Quality of Evidence

Figures 2, 3 and 4 provide a summary of the risk of bias assessment of RCTs, CBA studies and ITS respectively. Supplement 3 provides detailed risk of bias assessment for each included study. Overall, the quality of the evidence from the included RCTs was considered moderate, given that most of the studies scored “low risk” in the domains related to attrition (incomplete outcome data), detection (blinding of outcome assessment) and reporting bias. Half the studies scored “unclear risk” in the domains related to selection bias and performance bias. Overall the quality of the evidence from the included CBA was considered moderate. The majority of the studies scored “low risk” for baseline outcome measurements and baseline characteristics while half of the studies scored “unclear risk” for selective outcome reporting, incomplete outcome data and protection against contamination. For the included interrupted time series studies, the overall quality of the evidence was judged to be high. All studies scored “low risk” for the shape of intervention effect and the likelihood of the intervention to affect data collection and most studies scored “low risk” for independency of intervention of other changes and blinding of outcome assessment. Half of the studies scored “low risk” for incomplete outcome data and half scored “high risk” on selective outcome reporting.

Findings

We have organized the study findings according to the type of injury and its associated interventions adopted. Tables 2–10 present the details of interventions, outcomes and key results of included studies.

Road Injury (n=88):

Road safety claimed the largest share of the injury intervention programs and incorporated multiple approaches aiming at improving vehicle, bicycle and pedestrian safety, including (Supplement 4):

  1. Laws and legislations (n=4): All four studies proved effectiveness in reducing traffic-related injury rates40,108 and increasing bicycle helmet use53,65 among school-aged children with more compliance in younger children (i.e. elementary)53. Enforcement of an ordinance mandating bicycle helmet use coupled with helmet giveaway and education, was found to be highly effective in increasing helmet use among children aged 5–1265.

  2. Infrastructure (n=2): The combination of infrastructure and instructions showed positive results in improving pedestrian safety among children31,45. Children in combined interventions displayed improved safety behavior compared to those in education-only group45. Pedestrian safety intervention programs at a school consisting of education and infrastructure changes including crossing guards and physical barriers was effective in reducing hazardous events31.

  3. Cycling Training Courses (n=3): Cycling training programs and courses at school were found effective in teaching children simple behavioral strategies, such as signaling, visual search behavior and slowing down while approaching an intersection136 and in improving children’s cycling skills57. One study assessing Cycling safety skills course (Kids CAN-BIKE) found no impact on improving safe cycling behaviors, knowledge, or attitudes among grade 4 children92.

  4. Helmet use educational interventions (n=24): Twenty-four studies evaluated the effectiveness of interventions aiming at promoting bicycle helmet use. Eleven studies included educational interventions coupled with the distribution of discounted or free helmets. The educational interventions included single- or multiple- components such as classroom-based teaching, counselling, posters, messages from former Olympic cyclist, exhibition, skits, stories, parent’s information nights. Seven studies showed a positive impact on helmet ownership82 and use37,55,82,83,89,100,138, with one exhibiting no difference in helmet use when cost-sharing was required83. Three studies yielded mixed results with two demonstrating a positive impact on helmet ownership and no impact on helmet use110,133, and another showing positive impact in high-income schools and no impact in low-income schools on helmet use111. One study showed no impact on either113. Six studies evaluated the effectiveness of interventions developed based on the PRECEDE framework, examining factors surrounding an intervention including predisposing (i.e. high helmet cost), enabling (i.e. education and free/discounted helmets) and re-enforcing (i.e. parental education and awards for helmet use)74. These studies all yielded favorable outcomes indicating increased bicycle helmet use59,72,73 and decreased bicycle-related head injuries regardless of income60, gender, or age61 in addition to an improvement in knowledge74. Multi-component educational interventions exhibited favorable outcomes in knowledge and safety behaviors79,116 and proper helmet use38, a decline in bicycle injury rates79, and an increased helmet ownership and use68,86,99,116. Finally, a single study evaluating a low-cost eHealth software program, teaching bicycle safety knowledge and helmet wearing skills showed positive impact on safety knowledge and behaviors95.

  5. Other educational interventions adopted various approaches to promote child pedestrian safety (n=31), including PRECEDE Model51,127, table-top and roadside training32,129,130, virtual reality road safety training35,94,101,120–123,131,132, interactive computer-based video games33,56, educational videos112,126, active learning intervention programs76,105,143,144, school-based educational interventions36,91,135,145 and training52, and Think First Head and Spinal Cord Injury Prevention Program58,67,139,141. These approaches showed improvement in child’s road safety knowledge and behavior when crossing the road.

  6. Additional educational interventions (n=24) targeted various aspects of road safety34,39,43,46–49,63,64,66,81,87,88,90,97,98,107,109,114,117,137,146 including programs focusing on the etiology of spinal cord injury117, bike safety114, alcohol use39 helmet and seat belt use48,62,98,117, and booster seat use interactive sessions42. The overwhelming majority of these educational interventions demonstrated improvement in children and parents’ safety knowledge and practices. It is worth noting that the integration of an injury prevention specialist in an interactive setting substantially enhanced the effectiveness of the interventions compared to computerized tools66.

Falls (N=22):

Twenty-two studies reported the effectiveness of fall prevention interventions in reducing fall-related injuries and enhancing knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors (Supplement 5). Eighteen studies evaluated educational interventions46,66,67,81,88,90,102–104,106,109,115,117,118,137,139,141,146, three studies examined physical education and training50,54,70 and one study assessed infrastructural changes77. Educational interventions encompass Think First for Kids (TFFK) program, Risk Watch program, and interactive educational sessions using cartoons, puzzles and multi-component approaches such as lectures, lessons, presentations, videos, and posters. These interventions showed positive results in terms of improving fall safety knowledge, attitude and risk-taking behaviors (e.g. increased use of protective gear). One study found that coupling education about hazards in schools with close follow up on engineering report and assistance in funding can be effective in reducing fall hazards118. Studies exploring the effectiveness of physical education and training showed favorable results in improving balance and motor control ability70, diminishing children’s harmful responses to falls54 and reducing fracture incident rates50 more specifically with the increased number of years of physical education50. One study investigated playgrounds infrastructure modification (i.e. change the surface from wood fiber to granite sand) showed positive correlation with decreased arm fractures among children77.

Burns (N=22):

Twenty-two studies evaluated the effectiveness of educational interventions and programs including videos, instructions, lectures and sessions in addition to programs embedded in the curriculum in improving knowledge, attitude, and behaviors towards burns injuries among school-aged children (Supplement 6). The majority of education interventions were delivered at school with one intervention complemented by in-home visits and installation of smoke detectors78, two interventions were delivered at home98,134 and one delivered at a research laboratory41. Seventeen studies showed favorable results in terms of improving burn safety knowledge, behaviors and skills41,44,46,63,71,75,78,80,81,88,90,96,124,134,140,142,146 while five studies found no impact34,66,69,98,119. Notably, two studies demonstrated the positive effect of a multi-component education intervention on decreasing the frequency of burn injuries in the intervention group88,146.

Poisoning (n=10):

Ten studies investigated the impact of educational interventions in improving safety knowledge, attitude and behaviors about poisoning among school-aged children46,63,66,81,84,85,88,90,109,146 (Supplement 7). The majority of the interventions were administered in a school setting with one study administered at a pediatric clinic66. Educational programs such as ‘Injury Minimization Program’ and the ‘Risk Watch’ showed little improvement in children’s poisoning prevention knowledge63,81. Nonetheless, multi-component educational interventions that included letters, handbooks, posters, lectures, educational videos, manuals, and parents-child discussions were found to significantly increase knowledge safety, improve injury risk behavior46,84,109,146 and decrease poisoning frequency in intervention groups88,90. A multi-sensory interactive teaching approach was adopted in one study to educate children about chemical hazards; it proved effective in improving long-term knowledge retention compared to traditional teacher-centered educational approach85. Receiving safe behavior instructions through a computerized kiosk in a pediatric emergency department showed limited improvement in parents safe practices compared to information by an injury prevention specialist66.

Drowning (n=7):

Among the seven studies examining educational drowning interventions for school-age children (Supplement 8), five evaluated the usefulness of adopting multi-component education programs (i.e. videos, presentations, lectures, brochures, booklet, leaflets, information cards, and promotional materials)46,63,88,93,128,147 while one study assessed a testimonial-based video intervention125. Five of these studies were administered at a school setting46,63,88,125,128 while one study was administered at the swimming pool93. All seven studies consistently showed positive results in terms of improving children behaviors and safety knowledge46,63,88,125,128 and parental knowledge147 and supervision of children at public swimming pools93. One study found positive results in terms of decreasing minor and serious unintentional drowning88. It is worth noting that the reported positive results significantly differ across various age groups with a major improvement in younger age groups compared to the older population (e.g. 6–10 vs. 11–14 and 5–7 vs. 8–12 years old)93,128.

Discussion

This systematic review identified 117 studies that evaluated injury interventions and their effectiveness in preventing unintentional injuries among school-aged children. Despite the high prevalence of children injuries in low- and middle-income countries, this review confirmed the limited number of intervention studies in these countries. This is corroborated by findings from other reviews that noted similar evidence13,26,148. One plausible explanation is the inadequate safety measures adopted and the neglected efforts taken by these countries to prevent injuries in school-aged children.

This review shows that the majority of the injury intervention in literature examined traffic-related injuries compared to other types of injuries such as poisoning and drowning despite the fact that they claim a large share of the school-aged children injury burden. Most reported studies focused on educational interventions and their effectiveness in preventing unintentional injuries while fewer studies assessed other types of interventions such as laws and legislations, engineering and infrastructure. Notably, existing studies mainly reported the impact of interventions on knowledge, attitudes and behaviors with limited evidence on effectiveness in reducing injury, hospitalizations or mortality rates. This can be explained by the educational nature of most interventions investigated. Another explanation of the dearth of studies assessing the aforementioned outcomes can be the challenges in conducting and designing such studies given in terms of intensive resources and large sample size required.

The review suggests that multi-component educational interventions including school-based curricula such as Injury Minimization Programme for Schools (IMPS), Risk Watch, Think First can be effective in improving safety knowledge, attitudes and behaviors among school-aged children. However, evidence from included studies suggested that complementing educational interventions with other approaches can substantially increase knowledge retention, foster behavioral changes and result in more effective interventions. For instance, distributing free helmets along with educational interventions increased bicycle helmet use among children, mainly as a result of overpassing the financial barrier associated with helmet use among low income families. Multiple factors should be considered when designing educational interventions. For instance, parents’ involvement in educational interventions is equally important and shown to improve safety knowledge and behaviors among their children. Similar evidence has been noted by another review confirming that interventions targeting parents were effective in reducing child injuries13. As opposed to passive teaching, active and engaged learning present additional contributing factors to the success of injury interventions. Interactive sessions and simulation exercises increased the effectiveness of education interventions targeting school-aged children. Simulation exercises alone through trainings on tabletops or in virtual environments showed promising results in improving road crossing behavior in school-aged children. Although this review found breadth of evidence on the effectiveness of educational intervention on safety knowledge and behaviors, it highlighted the limited evidence on the impact of these educational interventions on injury rates26.

Laws and legislations were found to be major contributors to effective road safety interventions, particularly in increasing cycle helmet use and reducing traffic-related injury rates among school-aged children. These findings are consistent with Cochrane reviews demonstrating that mandatory bicycle helmet legislations are effective in increasing cycle helmet use and in reducing head injuries149150. This review highlights the scarcity of studies evaluating policies, laws or legislations aiming at preventing other types of unintentional injuries. Notably, this review underscores the age variation in safety regulation compliance, as younger children were more likely to abide by regulations. This suggests the need to couple laws and legislations with enforcement when dealing with older children.

A limited number of included studies evaluated the effectiveness of infrastructural interventions coupled with instructions and education in reducing injury risks and improving safe behaviors among children. These findings highlight the relevance of infrastructure modification and built-in safety and their impacts on reducing falls and in improving pedestrian safety among school-age children.

Implications for research, practice and policy

This review has identified a major knowledge gap in the impact of unintentional injury prevention interventions on child injury morbidity and mortality rates. It also highlights a gap in evaluating the impact of policies, laws and legislations on preventing unintentional injuries among school-aged children using rigorous methods. This is also applicable when it comes to interventions assessing infrastructure and engineering changes. We encourage global researcher to focus their future research on addressing these identified knowledge gaps mainly the effectiveness of different interventions in averting injury morbidity and mortality rates.

This review provides a powerful tool to inform policymakers and other stakeholders, interested in designing and delivering interventions to reduce risks of unintentional injuries in their communities. Funders are called to invest in research on the effectiveness of unintentional injury prevention interventions in low- and middle-income countries given the high burden of unintentional injuries in these countries and the limited contextualized evidence.

Strengths and Limitations

This systematic review has three main strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to examine a wide range of interventions to prevent unintentional injuries in school-aged children. We used standard, explicit, and rigorous methods in conducting this systematic review151 and we followed standard methods for reporting systematic reviews152. Third, we conducted a comprehensive search on multiple databases, and we have searched the references of all relevant systematic reviews. We have also included RCTs, CBAs studies and ITS that can provide high quality evidence. One of the limitations of this review is the lack of feasibility to perform meta-analysis and data pooling due to the variety of interventions, outcomes assessed and measurement methods. Moreover, the majority of included studies are conducted in high-income countries, which makes it challenging to generalize the review findings to LMICs.
