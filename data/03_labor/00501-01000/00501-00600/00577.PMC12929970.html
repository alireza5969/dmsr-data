Plant-Based Dietary Patterns, Body Composition, Muscle Strength and Function in Middle and Older Age: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Background

Sarcopenia is prevalent in middle-aged and older adults. A plant-based diet may be linked to changes in body composition and musculoskeletal health.

Objective

This systematic review aimed to investigate the effects of plant-based dietary patterns on the body composition, muscle strength and function in middle-aged and older adults. PRISMA guidelines were followed.

Method

A systematic search was completed on databases including MEDLINE, Embase, Global Health, PREMEDLINE, Cochrane library, CINAHL and Scopus on articles published until 1st March 2019. Search terms included ‘middle-aged’, ‘older adult’, ‘plant-based diet’ and ‘muscle strength’. Title and abstract screening were conducted on 1,868 publications after deduplication. Sample size, subject characteristics, recruitment, inclusion and exclusion criteria, dietary measures, health outcome measures, statistical results and confounders were sorted by author, study type, year and country. The quality of evidence of extracted data was assessed using the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) tools.

Results/Conclusions

17 papers were included from 31 articles selected for full review. The majority of the studies were cross-sectional studies (n = 9) and RCTs (n = 6). The remaining studies were cohort studies. Positive associations were found between plant-based dietary patterns; negative association with body composition; and inconclusive association with muscle function. However, further studies are needed to improve the understanding.

Introduction

The worldwide population is rapidly ageing. A natural and gradual loss of muscle mass and strength occurs as we age (1) with a 30% reduction in muscle mass expected to occur between the ages of 20 and 80 years (2). This phenomenon has been named sarcopenia and has been linked to higher risk of functional decline, falls and mortality (3). However, muscle function can be maintained with adequate physical activity and nutrition (4). A recent literature review pointed to the benefits of adequate nutrient intake on muscle strength and function, particularly protein, calcium and vitamin D (5).

Plant-based diets are characterized by a reduction or elimination of meat and meat product consumption. They generally consist of grains, vegetables, fruits, legumes and nuts. In 2017, an Australian study found that a plant-based diets were associated with better lower limb muscle strength in middle-aged women (6). At the same time, the number of vegans in United Kingdom quadrupled between 2014 and 2018 (7). This change in dietary habits may be linked to the emerging evidence relating to the benefits of a plant-based diet (8). Vegetarian diets have been shown to be protective against the incidence of ischemic heart disease (9). In addition, a metaanalysis found that plant-based diets were associated with weight reduction (10). Some other benefits of plant-based diets are related to animal welfare and the environment (11).

Approximately 60% of dietary protein consumed in the world is derived from plant sources (12). However, the unavailability of a complete essential amino acid profile in plant-based protein source compromises the anabolic ability of muscle. The essential amino acids are building blocks for muscle protein synthesis and signalling molecules that activate the process directly. Studies have demonstrated that older adults have a reduced rate of muscle anabolism but animal-derived protein sources could stimulate muscle protein synthesis to a great extent in that age group (12, 13). Less is known about the association between plant-based diets and musculoskeletal health, especially in high sarcopenic risk population groups. With the increasing ageing population and potentially significant influence of plant-based diet in modern society and public health, it is important to address this issue.

To the best of our knowledge, no reviews have collated studies investigating the effect of plant-based dietary protein sources on musculoskeletal health outcomes. The aim of this systematic review was to investigate the effects of plant-based dietary patterns on the body composition, muscle strength and function in middle-aged and older adults using Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.

Methods

A systematic literature review of English-language articles published in peer-reviewed journals indexed up to March 2019 has been conducted. The electronic databases MEDLINE (Ovid) (1946 to Week 2 February 2019), Embase Classic+Embase (Ovid) (1973 to 11 March 2019), Global Health (Ovid) (1910 to Week 9 2019) and PREMEDLINE (Ovid) were assessed. Additional searches were done on the Cochrane library, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) and Scopus. Key terms for population of interest included ‘middle-aged’, ‘older adult’ and ‘elderly’; for intervention were ‘vegetarian’, ‘plant-based diet’ and for outcomes were ‘gait speed’, ‘grip strength’, ‘muscle mass’, ‘body composition’ and ‘muscle strength’.

Included studies were cross-sectional, cohort, randomized control trials (RCTs) and previous systematic literature reviews. Literature reviews, case studies and non-study-based sources were not included. Measures of dietary intake had to be conducted by trained professionals and with valid dietary assessment methodology such as food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), 24-hour recall and weighed food record. Measures of body composition, muscle strength and function also had to be assessed by trained professionals with valid assessments. Eligible studies had to include nutrition status and body composition, physical strength or function as outcome variables. This review has been registered at PROSPERO (ID: CRD42019126867).

Two review authors independently assessed the titles and abstracts retrieved from the searches to determine relevance and eligibility. Articles were kept if they met inclusion criteria at title and abstract screening and if both review authors consented. Inclusion criteria were (i) population: individuals aged 45 years and older, (ii) intervention: measure of diet quality, (iii) comparator: no comparator, descriptive systematic review, (iv) outcome: body composition and function and (v) setting: community-based population (with a small proportion of institutionalised subjects).

Full texts of the potentially relevant studies were retrieved and reviewed by two review authors independently. These articles were coded based on the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as stage 1 screening. References of the selected articles were hand-searched for additional papers and these papers were screened. A third review author were called upon to resolve any conflicts if a consensus could not be reached between the two review authors.

Quality Assessment

The quality of evidence of extracted data was assessed using the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) tools by two review authors independently. The SIGN tool assessed potential biases such as selection bias, performance bias, attrition bias and detection bias. All included studies were scored as “High Quality (++)”, “Acceptable (+)”, “Unacceptable (0)” and “Low Quality (-)”. Differences were resolved by discussion of two reviewers or by appeal to a third review author if a consensus could not be reached. The results were summarized in Table 1.

The GRADE system was adopted to assess the overall quality of the extracted data. A final rating of the quality was assigned based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.

Results

Study Selection

A total of 2,670 records were obtained from the electronic database search. In all, 806 duplicated publications were removed, leaving 1,864 publications to be screened against inclusion and exclusion criteria in the first stage. From the reference list of eligible papers, four additional papers were identified from abstract screening. In the second stage, full texts of thirty-one articles were screened, and seventeen studies were identified as eligible for inclusion in this review.

Characteristics of participants and recruitment strategies

The characteristics of selected studies are described in Table 1. The majority of identified studies were cross-sectional (n = 9)(14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22) or RCTs (n = 6)(23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28) published between 1990 and 2018. The study populations were mostly from developed countries such as the United States (n = 9)(14, 15, 19, 22, 23, 24, 25, 27, 28). Although sample sizes ranged from 19 to 419,207 participants, eight studies (15, 16, 17, 19, 20, 21, 22, 29) had >100 participants. The mean age of most studies (n = 10) was <65 years (15, 17, 19, 20, 21, 25, 26, 27, 28). Two studies did not provide details about recruitment (24, 26), six recruited women only (14, 17, 18, 20, 21, 27), three men only (24, 25, 28), and the remaining studies did not restrict to gender (15, 16, 19, 22, 23, 26, 29).

Dietary Pattern

Dietary pattern was the exposure variable in this review, and it was assessed using various methods such as a priori approach (e.g. dietary indices) and posteriori approach (e.g. factor analysis). Two studies used factor analysis to identify groups with different dietary habits (17, 29). Eight studies recruited participants with known dietary patterns or assigned participants into different diet groups (14, 15, 21, 22, 24, 25, 26, 27), and since this review also considers a diet consisting of protein from solely plant sources as a plant-based diet, two studies measuring plant protein intake as an outcome variable were also included (18, 19). Nutrient analysis was the most common method to assess dietary patterns.

Body Composition

In all, thirteen studies reported data on body composition which included sarcopenic status (16, 29), percentage lean mass or fat-free mass (FFM) (14, 15, 18, 19, 20, 21, 24, 27, 28), FFM index (20), plasma and urinary total creatine content (24, 25, 28), muscle creatine and phosphocreatine conten t(24), muscle fibre area (25), and triceps skinfold thickness and upper arm muscle area (26). Percentage body fat was considered as it was a proxy of lean mass. The objective measurements were conducted using one of the following instruments or assessments: dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, bioelectrical impedance analysis, hydrostatic weighing, colorimetry, muscle, plasma and urine sampling, computed tomography and callipers.

Table 2 contains a summary of results from studies included in this systematic literature review. Five studies showed a gain in muscle mass or a reduced risk of sarcopenia when consuming a plant-based dietary pattern. A prospective cohort study (29) found that increased vegetables and fruits consumption was significantly associated with lower likelihood of sarcopenia in men at baseline, however, the association was not significant in women at baseline or in both groups at 4-year follow-up. Similarly, a cross-sectional study found an association between vegetable intake and reduced risk of sarcopenia in men, but not in women (29). Additionally, a cross-sectional study (14) found that vegetarians had lower percentage body fat than non-vegetarian i.e. higher percentage lean mass. Another cross-sectional study (20) suggested that higher intakes of vegetables and fruits were significantly associated with increased FFM, percentage FFM and FFM index. In an RCT (26), a vegetarian diet was associated with higher triceps skinfold thickness. Conversely, eight studies showed a negative association between plant-based dietary pattern and body composition. One cross-sectional study (15) found that vegetarians and vegans had significantly lower lean mass than regular meat eaters, however, the association was not significant amongst the Indian population. One RCT (27) also found a significant association between increased vegetable intake and reduced lean mass. In contrast, an American RCT (28) comparing individuals on vegetarian diet with those on omnivorous diet for two weeks found no difference in lower lean mass. However, those on the vegetarian diet had a significantly lower plasma creatine content. Five studies found non-significant association between a plant-based dietary pattern and lean mass (18, 19, 20, 21, 24), muscle creatine, total creatine and phosphocreatine contents (24) and upper arm muscle area 26).

Together these results indicate a possible relationship between plant-based diets and better body composition outcomes in older adults, however, the effects may be different for men and women. As for the negative effect of plant-based diets in body composition, most of the results were not significant. The quality of studies was fairly good, given that more than half of the studies were ranked as “Acceptable (+)” and “High Quality (++)”. Overall, the current evidence regarding the association between plant-based dietary pattern and body composition is inconsistent.

Muscle Strength

A total of six studies reported measurements of muscle strength of which four showed a positive association with plant-based dietary pattern. Hand grip strength was the most commonly used strength assessment. A borderline significant association between lower limb muscle strength and plant-based diet was observed in a cross-sectional study (17), and in another cross-sectional study (19), the association between plant protein and grip strength was significant. Individuals consuming animal protein showed greater grip strength, but the result was not statistically significant. In the same study, when analysis was limited to a group of participants aged 65 years and over, the longitudinal association between plant protein and grip strength was not significant (19). Another cross-sectional study (15) involving white and Indian population found that when compared to meat eaters, vegetarians had a significant lower grip strength, however, this association was not significant amongst vegans. Interestingly, in the same study, when grip strength was adjusted for lean mass, white vegans had comparable results to regular meat eaters, whereas Indian vegans had significantly lower results. In an RCT (22), increased fruit and vegetable intakes were associated with higher grip strength. However, two RCTs (24, 25) found no association between increased muscle strength as measured by leg extension and flexion, bilateral leg press, chest press and arm pull. However, a gain in muscular strength was observed after 12-week resistance training in one of the RCTs (25).

In summary four studies have found a positive association between plant-based dietary pattern and muscle strength, though, two other studies have demonstrated a significant negative association. Overall, most studies were ranked as “High Quality (++)” and “Acceptable (+)”, hence, the evidence for the positive association between plant-based dietary pattern and muscle strength is strong.

Muscle Function

Of the three studies investigating muscle function or physical performance, two showed a positive association with plant-based dietary pattern. A cross-sectional study (22) found that a significant association between vegetable and fruit intakes and 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) physical function subscale score. Another cross-sectional study (17) found a tendency towards better physical performance in tests such as step test, functional reach test and lateral reach test when consuming a plant-based diet. A negative but non-significant association between plant-based dietary pattern and time up and go performance was also reported. The association between fruit and vegetables intakes with muscle function — walk speed measurement, chair stand test and calculation of short physical performance battery score — were not statistically significant in a RCT (23).

In summary, there is a small body of evidence regarding the relationship between plant-based dietary pattern and muscle function. Half of the studies pointed to a positive association but the results were non-significant. Studies were ranked as “High Quality (++)” or “Acceptable (+)”, thus the current evidence of the association between plant-based dietary pattern and muscle function is strong, however, the association between plant-based dietary pattern and muscle function is inconclusive.

Quality Assessment

The selected studies consisted of a variety of study designs including cross-sectional, cohort and RCT. This review used SIGN tool to assess the quality of the selected studies, however, not all components of the SIGN tool were applicable to the identified studies namely study selection, data extraction, subject selection, blinding of study, participant drop-out rate, and comparisons between participants and those lost to follow-up. Ten of the selected studies had a cross-sectional or cohort design that only assessed a single population without follow-up or blinding, therefore, some questions related to subject selection and assessment sections were not applicable. In the ten cross-sectional and cohort studies, three (14, 15, 19) had no evidence to demonstrate that the methodology to assess outcome were valid and reliable. A total of nine studies did not measure exposure more than once(14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 29), participants groups were not comparable in three studies(15, 16, 17) and no confidence intervals were provided in five studies(14, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22). One study did not indicate the number of people asked to participate(18) and two did not consider the potential confounders(14, 18). Furthermore, in the six selected RCTs, four did not blind subjects or investigators(24, 25, 26, 27), one did not randomise subjects (25) and four did not mention concealment method used(24, 25, 26, 28). The treatment and control groups were not similar at baseline in two studies(23, 26). Drop-out rates were >20% for two of the seventeen studies(26, 27) and the rest had <20% drop-out rate during study or were not applicable to the question.

Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation System

Risk of bias

The SIGN tool showed that three studies had “High Quality (++)”,(17, 20, 29) ten were ranked as “Acceptable Quality (+)”,(14, 16, 19, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26, 27, 28) two had “Unacceptable (0),(15, 18) and one had “Low Quality (-)(25). Half of the studies were either cross-sectional or cohort in design, and most of RCTs had no blinding, randomisation of participants or concealment method revealed. Therefore, there is a higher level of risk of bias due to study type and design.

Inconsistency

The seventeen studies could not be directly compared due to inconsistencies between the measurement of dietary pattern and health outcomes. Six studies had fruits and vegetables intake as outcome variable(16, 22, 23, 26, 27, 29), seven studies used source of protein(14, 18, 19, 21, 24, 25, 28), and three studies converted the measurements into diet groups or patterns(15, 17, 26). The dietary measures used included questionnaire(16, 17, 19, 20, 21, 22, 27, 29), food records(14, 18, 23, 24, 25), 24-hour recalls(15, 26) and weighed food supply(28).

The outcome variables for body composition, muscle strength and function varied amongst the studies as each examined a different component and therefore different measures were used. The components included lean mass or fat mass as proxy(14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 24, 25, 27, 28, 29), grip strength(15, 19, 23, 29), gait speed(23, 29), biochemical components(24, 25, 28), arm muscle measurements(26) and various physical test performance(16, 17, 22). Therefore, there is a high heterogeneity across the selected studies.

Indirectness

All selected studies included populations of community-dwelling adults aged 45 or over and did not have diseases affecting oral intake. The selection criteria were comparable amongst the studies. Some studies re-recruited participants from previous similar health programs and they might be more health-conscious, although the overall quality of evidence is not affected(14, 15, 16, 17, 19, 20, 22, 27, 29). The population was well represented. However, the variations between study design and outcome measures made it hard to compare all studies.

Imprecision

In total, there are 426,861 participants across the seventeen studies, and it is an optimal information size addressing the research question.

Publication Bias

A comprehensive search on several electronic databases and hand-searching of reference lists for grey literature ensure all available literature was captured to minimize the risk of publication bias. Unpublished journals, articles not in major databases or non-English studies might have been missed, therefore, publication bias cannot be ruled out.

The quality of evidence of each outcome of this review was assessed with GRADE system in terms of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias and overall quality. Evidence for body composition, muscle strength and muscle function were rated moderate for risk of bias as a result of higher quality of evidence. In another category, the high variations in measures of body composition and muscle function caused higher risks of inconsistency whereas the evidence for muscle strength had a low risk. All three areas of interest had low risks of indirectness due to direct population and outcomes and low risks of imprecision due to large sample sizes. Since publication bias could not be ruled out so all areas were rated with moderate risk of publication bias. Overall, the confidence in the evidence on body composition, muscle strength and muscle function was good.

Discussion

After screening according to specific inclusion and exclusion criteria on population, intervention and outcome variables, seventeen studies comprising of cohort, cross-sectional, and RCTs, were included in this systematic literature review. This review assessed the evidence regarding the association of plant-based dietary pattern and body composition, muscle strength and function in middle-aged and older adults aged 45 years or above. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic literature review on this body of evidence. The studies included in this review varied in a great extent in study settings, dietary pattern and health outcome measurements and confounding factors adjusted for in the statistical models used. Therefore, there was a high heterogeneity in the results. Overall, there is a higher number of studies of high-quality pointing to a negative association between plant-based dietary pattern and body composition in middle-aged and older adults, however, most results were not statistically significant. There is a moderate confidence in this conclusion after using the GRADE system. Secondly, our confidence on the positive association of this dietary pattern and muscle strength is moderate due to a body of fairly good quality of evidence. Lastly, we also have moderate confidence on the inconclusive association between plant-based dietary pattern and muscle function. The limited evidence was mostly not statistically significant but was at a relatively high quality.

The difference in amino acid composition(17, 19) as well as availability(18), in various diets might contribute to the different effects on protein metabolism that partly determines muscle measures. It has been shown that protein from animal sources has a complete amino acid profile, thus, older individuals who consume a plant-based diet may have a higher risk of muscle atrophy (19). In addition, they tended to have a lower total protein intake (25), reflected by lower total creatine concentration (24). The lower muscle mass observed might be explained by the deficient in body protein accumulation. In contrast, some included studies suggested that antioxidants such as vitamin C and carotenoids in plant-based diet might have a protective action against oxidative stress on muscles, which in term might assist in muscle strength maintenance (16, 17, 23). A plant-based diet may also be part of a healthy lifestyle that is likely to relate to better physical function (22).

A recent systematic review (30) investigated the association between diet quality (as measured by nutrients and food groups intakes) and sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults, and found a strong association between “healthier diets” (greater fruits and vegetables, whole-meal cereals and oily fish consumptions) and smaller declines in physical performance. While the findings of this review were not based on a plant-based dietary pattern, it provided evidence of benefits of fruits and vegetables consumption on muscle health.

The current systematic review has a number of strengths and limitations. Firstly, well-recognized quality assessment tools were adopted i.e. SIGN tool and GRADE system. Secondly, the comprehensive search strategy employed on multiple major electronic databases and hand-searching captured all relevant journals. Furthermore, reporting of our results followed the PRISMA guidance, to provide a high quality and organized review. The included studies were mostly conducted in developed countries such as the United States and the United Kingdom, hence one of our limitations is that this review may not be generalizable to places with lower income such as those from developing countries. Another limitation of the current review relates to the diversity in methodology to assess both dietary pattern and health outcomes as it makes it difficult to assess overall quality of studies or conduct a meta-analysis. Another limitation of this review is that most studies captured the dietary pattern at one time point; effects of changes in diet on health outcomes were not assessed. The difference in confounding should be noted, six studies adjusted for physical activity in the analyses, four studies adjusted for smoking, and four adjusted for alcohol consumption.

Conclusion

This systematic review revealed some evidence demonstrating that plant-based dietary pattern is positively associated with muscle strength and negatively associated with body composition. The association between plant-based dietary pattern and muscle function was inconclusive. The large heterogeneity across the seventeen included studies and our moderate confidence on the effects, mean the associations remain unclear. Given the current evidence, further interventions could include populations from lower income countries and conducted over longer periods to improve our understanding of the effect of a plant-based diet in middle-aged and older adults.

## Introduction

The worldwide population is rapidly ageing. A natural and gradual loss of muscle mass and strength occurs as we age (1) with a 30% reduction in muscle mass expected to occur between the ages of 20 and 80 years (2). This phenomenon has been named sarcopenia and has been linked to higher risk of functional decline, falls and mortality (3). However, muscle function can be maintained with adequate physical activity and nutrition (4). A recent literature review pointed to the benefits of adequate nutrient intake on muscle strength and function, particularly protein, calcium and vitamin D (5).

Plant-based diets are characterized by a reduction or elimination of meat and meat product consumption. They generally consist of grains, vegetables, fruits, legumes and nuts. In 2017, an Australian study found that a plant-based diets were associated with better lower limb muscle strength in middle-aged women (6). At the same time, the number of vegans in United Kingdom quadrupled between 2014 and 2018 (7). This change in dietary habits may be linked to the emerging evidence relating to the benefits of a plant-based diet (8). Vegetarian diets have been shown to be protective against the incidence of ischemic heart disease (9). In addition, a metaanalysis found that plant-based diets were associated with weight reduction (10). Some other benefits of plant-based diets are related to animal welfare and the environment (11).

Approximately 60% of dietary protein consumed in the world is derived from plant sources (12). However, the unavailability of a complete essential amino acid profile in plant-based protein source compromises the anabolic ability of muscle. The essential amino acids are building blocks for muscle protein synthesis and signalling molecules that activate the process directly. Studies have demonstrated that older adults have a reduced rate of muscle anabolism but animal-derived protein sources could stimulate muscle protein synthesis to a great extent in that age group (12, 13). Less is known about the association between plant-based diets and musculoskeletal health, especially in high sarcopenic risk population groups. With the increasing ageing population and potentially significant influence of plant-based diet in modern society and public health, it is important to address this issue.

To the best of our knowledge, no reviews have collated studies investigating the effect of plant-based dietary protein sources on musculoskeletal health outcomes. The aim of this systematic review was to investigate the effects of plant-based dietary patterns on the body composition, muscle strength and function in middle-aged and older adults using Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.
## Methods

A systematic literature review of English-language articles published in peer-reviewed journals indexed up to March 2019 has been conducted. The electronic databases MEDLINE (Ovid) (1946 to Week 2 February 2019), Embase Classic+Embase (Ovid) (1973 to 11 March 2019), Global Health (Ovid) (1910 to Week 9 2019) and PREMEDLINE (Ovid) were assessed. Additional searches were done on the Cochrane library, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) and Scopus. Key terms for population of interest included ‘middle-aged’, ‘older adult’ and ‘elderly’; for intervention were ‘vegetarian’, ‘plant-based diet’ and for outcomes were ‘gait speed’, ‘grip strength’, ‘muscle mass’, ‘body composition’ and ‘muscle strength’.

Included studies were cross-sectional, cohort, randomized control trials (RCTs) and previous systematic literature reviews. Literature reviews, case studies and non-study-based sources were not included. Measures of dietary intake had to be conducted by trained professionals and with valid dietary assessment methodology such as food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), 24-hour recall and weighed food record. Measures of body composition, muscle strength and function also had to be assessed by trained professionals with valid assessments. Eligible studies had to include nutrition status and body composition, physical strength or function as outcome variables. This review has been registered at PROSPERO (ID: CRD42019126867).

Two review authors independently assessed the titles and abstracts retrieved from the searches to determine relevance and eligibility. Articles were kept if they met inclusion criteria at title and abstract screening and if both review authors consented. Inclusion criteria were (i) population: individuals aged 45 years and older, (ii) intervention: measure of diet quality, (iii) comparator: no comparator, descriptive systematic review, (iv) outcome: body composition and function and (v) setting: community-based population (with a small proportion of institutionalised subjects).

Full texts of the potentially relevant studies were retrieved and reviewed by two review authors independently. These articles were coded based on the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as stage 1 screening. References of the selected articles were hand-searched for additional papers and these papers were screened. A third review author were called upon to resolve any conflicts if a consensus could not be reached between the two review authors.

### Quality Assessment

The quality of evidence of extracted data was assessed using the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) tools by two review authors independently. The SIGN tool assessed potential biases such as selection bias, performance bias, attrition bias and detection bias. All included studies were scored as “High Quality (++)”, “Acceptable (+)”, “Unacceptable (0)” and “Low Quality (-)”. Differences were resolved by discussion of two reviewers or by appeal to a third review author if a consensus could not be reached. The results were summarized in Table 1.

The GRADE system was adopted to assess the overall quality of the extracted data. A final rating of the quality was assigned based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.
