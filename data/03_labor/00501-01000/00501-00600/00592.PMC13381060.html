Abstract

BACKGROUND:

Peer support has been utilized and associated with clinical outcomes (e.g., improved mood) in patients with solid malignancies. However, to date, there is minimal literature examining peer support among patients with hematologic malignancies and/or patients who have undergone hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).

METHODS:

In accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines, we completed a systematic review using five databases to assess the relationship between peer support and clinical outcomes (e.g., distress, physical symptoms) among patients with hematologic malignancies or HSCT recipients.

RESULTS:

The eight included studies examined peer support in a total of 574 patients. Four intervention studies highlighted the potential benefits of peer support, such as improved physical symptoms. Two studies, one interventional and one cross-sectional, highlighted the need for more empirically based peer support interventions in the HSCT population.

CONCLUSION:

Among patients with hematologic malignancies and/or HSCT recipients, there is a dearth of literature examining the association between peer support and outcomes, although few studies have described a positive association between peer support and better health outcomes. More randomized controlled studies are needed to better understand the role of peer support and peer support interventions on outcomes in these vulnerable populations.

INTRODUCTION

Peer support is a form of social support that entails the connection between individuals with similar illness or treatment experiences who share about their lived experiences with their illness or treatment, offer advice, and encourage each other [1]. Peer support can be delivered one-on-one or in group settings [2, 3]. In patients with solid malignancies, peer support has been associated with improvements in psychological well-being, coping skills, and self-efficacy, and increases in knowledge about their illness and recovery [4–6]. The benefits of peer support can be bidirectional and extend to both the givers and receivers of peer support [7]. For example, Mollica et al. assessed the feasibility and acceptability of a 2-month peer navigation program for African American breast cancer survivors [7]. They found that the intervention was feasible and acceptable to breast cancer survivors, while those providing peer support reported a sense of connectedness, strength, and purpose after participating in the program [7]. Although the majority of existing evidence on the association between peer support and clinical outcomes has been for patients with a variety of solid malignancies, including breast, prostate, or colorectal cancer [4, 5, 8], there are limited data on the utility and need for peer support interventions among patients with hematologic malignancies and/or those who have undergone HSCT.

Hematologic malignancies, a diverse group of disorders with notable variations in disease course, treatment options, and potential for curability are common, account for more than 6% of all cancers, and ~10% of all cancer deaths [9, 10]. Patients with hematologic malignancies must often cope with significant physical and psychological symptoms due to their illness and frequently exhaustive treatments accompanied by toxicities, adverse effects, and post-treatment complications [11]. Additionally, some patients with hematologic malignancies grapple with high rates of healthcare utilization, including hospitalizations and intensive care unit admissions, which undermine their quality of life (QOL). Among patients with indolent hematologic malignancies, intermittent treatment can precipitate long-term physical and psychological morbidities (e.g., fatigue, distress) [12]. Considering the established benefits of peer support on psychological well-being and QOL among patients with solid malignancies [4–6], peer support could help mitigate some of the unmet well-being needs in patients with hematologic malignancies.

Many patients with hematologic malignancies face higher prognostic uncertainty than individuals with solid tumors and must often undergo intensive therapies such as high-dose intensive chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) [13]. HSCT is a potentially life-saving treatment for patients with malignant and benign hematologic disorders [14]. However, the treatment is aggressive, accompanied by prolonged hospitalizations, as well as physical (e.g., organ damage, infections, and graft-versus-host disease [15]), psychological (e.g., depression, anxiety [16]), and social (e.g., role adjustments [17], spousal relationship dissatisfaction [18]) challenges. Further, during the period when their immune system is rebuilding, HSCT recipients must abide by strict isolation and quarantine requirements, necessitating the assistance of a reliable caregiver who can assist them with everyday tasks such as picking up medications from a pharmacy. Consequently, robust social support and psychological well-being are essential to all aspects of the HSCT process, from treatment to successful recovery following transplantation. Additionally, social support has been associated with important clinical and patient-reported outcomes, including better QOL and less psychological distress [19].

Given the unique challenges that patients with hematologic malignancies or those who have undergone HSCT have to cope with throughout their illness and care, a nuanced understanding of the role of peer support is needed as part of efforts to optimize social support and social well-being in these vulnerable populations [20]. Hence, we completed a systematic review aimed to describe and examine the relationship between peer support and outcomes in patients with hematologic malignancies or those who have undergone HSCT to clarify the role of peer support in the lived experiences of these populations.

METHODS

Overview

The review was conducted in accordance with the guidelines and criteria of the Preferred Reporting Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [21] to ensure rigor and accurate reporting of the data.

Search Strategy

Existing literature on peer support for patients with any cancer diagnosis guided our choice of search terms (Table 1). A comprehensive literature search of five electronic databases (i.e., PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and CINAHL) was conducted in May 2021 using our defined search terms. In each case, articles from database inception to the time of the search (May 2021) were included. Further, reference lists of all full-text manuscripts reviewed were screened to identify possible articles meeting the selection criteria. The search was limited to English-language publications due to a lack of translation services.

Selection Criteria

Our inclusion criteria entailed: 1) empirical articles examining peer support in patients with hematologic malignancies or those who have undergone HSCT; 2) study participants diagnosed with the following hematologic malignancy diagnoses: leukemia (acute or chronic, myeloid or lymphocytic, plasma cell), Hodgkin lymphoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, multiple myeloma, myelodysplastic syndrome or myeloproliferative neoplasms; 3) study samples consisting of adult and/or pediatric populations; 4) studies examining mixed samples of cancer diagnoses; 5) studies published in English and not limited by country. Additionally, we did not exclude articles based on study designs—all qualitative, cohort, descriptive, intervention development, and randomized controlled trials were included.

Selection Procedure and Data Analysis

Two study team members conducted systematic searches of the electronic databases (L.H. and R.L.) to identify potentially relevant articles. Searches were imported into the Covidence systematic review software [22], and duplicates were removed and prepared for review. L.H. and R.L. independently screened non-duplicate articles by titles and abstracts using the Covidence software to exclude studies that did not meet our inclusion criteria. Full-text articles were then screened independently for eligibility criteria by L.H. and R.L. Reference lists of included full-text articles were also screened for additional articles. Resolution of conflicts at each stage of the review process was completed by H.A. Once all eligible studies were identified, H.A., L.H., and R.L. reviewed all relevant articles prior to data extraction.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Data relating to authors’ names, year of publication, title of publication, setting, sample characteristics (i.e., gender, race), intervention characteristics (i.e., design, training for peer supporters, length of peer support contact, activities/support, impact on peer supporters), outcomes, and measurement were extracted by two study team members, L.H. and S.S., and reviewed by H.A.

Two study team members (L.H. and S.S.) independently assessed the articles’ quality using the National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute (NHLBI) quality assessment tools for observational cohort, cross-sectional, and controlled intervention studies [23, 24]. The checklists are composed of 14 questions relating to the articles’ methodological rigor and validity. Articles were rated on each item as follows: yes, no, and other (cannot determine, not applicable, not reported). Articles were awarded a score of 1 for each criterion met and 0 for those not met, with a maximum possible score of 14. The articles were categorized as good quality for scores of 11–14, fair quality for scores of 5–10, and poor quality for scores of 0–4. Although scores were not used to exclude articles from inclusion, they were discussed in consensus conferences between S.S. and L.H. Table 2 shows the detailed results of the quality assessment of our eight synthesized articles.

Data Synthesis

It was determined that a meta-analysis was not achievable due to the heterogeneity of study designs and the limited number of included quantitative studies.

RESULTS

Study Selection

Figure 1 shows a PRISMA flow diagram of the search and selection strategy. A total of 547 articles were identified from the initial search of electronic databases. Of these, 81 duplicate articles were removed. Abstract screening of the remaining 466 articles yielded eight articles that met eligibility criteria and transitioned to full-text screening. The most common reason for article exclusion was no discussion of peer support (n = 458). Screening of the eight full-text article reference lists did not yield any additional publications. Hence eight studies (1.7%) were included in the analysis. Table 2 provides the full details of each study and its findings. Data from the studies were extracted in accordance with the Population, Intervention, Comparator/comparison group, Outcomes, Timing, and Setting (PICOS) framework [25]. All studies were written in English. Five studies (62.5%) were completed in the United States and three (37.5%) in Denmark.

Participant Characteristics

The eight included studies examined peer support in a total of 574 patients and 37 peer supporters, with individual study sizes ranging from 8 to 315 patients. Of the studies that provided sociodemographic (i.e., n = 6 for gender; n = 5 for age; and n = 3 for race) information about patients, mean (standard deviation: SD) age was 51.37 (14.48; SD only provided for 2/5 studies that provided age data) years, 40% (n = 274) were female, and 79.5% (n = 353) were white. Of the studies (n = 2) that provided information about peer supporters, 56.8% (n = 21) were female; the mean age of peer supporters was 46 years. Patients and peer supporters reported a variety of hematologic malignancy diagnoses (e.g., acute myeloid leukemia, acute lymphatic leukemia, multiple myeloma, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma).

Study Outcome Measures

Of the five studies which reported on qualitative data, four were intervention studies [17, 26–28]. Three intervention studies reported on feasibility ratings of interventions or psychological and/or medical outcomes [26, 27, 29]. Two of the six total intervention studies used quantitative measures to assess: [27, 30] psychological outcomes (e.g., depression, anxiety) using the Brief Symptom Inventory [31] and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [32]; coping appraisal and self-efficacy using the General Self-Efficacy Scale [33]; symptom burden using the Cohen and Hoberman Inventory of Physical Symptoms [34] and the MD Anderson Symptom Inventory [35]; and Quality of life (QOL) using the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Bone Marrow Transplant (FACT-BMT) [36], Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy– Leukemia (FACT-Leu) [37], and European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-C30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) [38]. Additionally, Glover et al. assessed social support using the non-validated Patient Support Index [39], an inventory tool comprised of informational items extracted from validated social support instruments to collect data pertinent to their observational study [40].

Benefits of Peer Support

Four intervention studies highlighted the potential benefits of peer support for patients with hematologic malignancies or patients who have undergone HSCT, such as improved psychological well-being and physical symptoms [17, 27, 30, 41]. Only two of these studies used quantitative assessments to highlight the association between peer support and psychological outcomes (e.g., anxiety) [27, 30]. A four-arm randomized controlled trial with 315 participants by Rini et al. investigated whether a peer support intervention that involved writing to future HSCT recipients was beneficial to HSCT survivors [30]. This study showed that a four-week peer support expressive writing intervention reduced general distress (p < 0.05) and improved physical symptoms (p < 0.002) and QOL (p = 0.02) among HSCT survivors with high survivorship problems when compared to control interventions (i.e., a peer helping intervention, general expressive writing, or neutral writing) [30]. The second study was a single-arm feasibility study by Nørskov et al., which showed that a 12-week peer support intervention for patients newly diagnosed with leukemia was acceptable (i.e., 96.3% of patients and 80.6% of peer supporters reported satisfaction with the intervention) [27]. A pre-post assessment of the intervention’s impact on outcomes showed its significant association with improved anxiety (ΔM = −2.1, 95% CI = −3.6 to 0.6, p = 0.007), global health (ΔM = 11.5, 95% CI = 0.1 to 22.8, p = 0.047), role functioning (ΔM = 20.8, 95% CI = 4.4 to 37.2, p = 0.014), cognitive functioning (ΔM = 9.0, 95% CI = 0.3 to 17.8, p = 0.044), functional well-being (ΔM = 3.5, 95% CI = 0.8 to 6.3, p = 0.014), and patient activation level (ΔM = 0.6, 95% CI = 0.1 to 1.1, p = 0.021) from baseline to the 12-week follow-up period [27]. A pre-post assessment of the intervention’s impact on outcomes among peer supporters also showed a significant association with improved emotional well-being (ΔM = −1.0, 95% CI = −1.6 to 0.3, p = 0.004) over the same 12-week period [27].

The two intervention studies which used qualitative methods to elaborate on the potential benefits of peer support are Sherman et al. and Nørskov et al. [41, 42], The first study reported that peer support helped patients cope with the various challenges (e.g., physical complications, fatigue, strain in relationships) that could accompany hematologic malignancy diagnoses and/or treatment [42]. The second study reported that a peer support intervention fostered hope among patients as they connected with others who had completed their treatment [41].

Needs Assessment for Peer Support Resources

Two qualitative studies described social support resources utilized by HSCT recipients and highlighted the need for more empirically based peer support interventions in the HSCT population [40, 43]. First, one intervention study evaluated social support resources for 119 patients with relapsed lymphoma and those considering HSCT and demonstrated that family was the primary source of social support in 83% (n = 99) of their sample, followed by nurses as the primary source of clinical support by 79% (n = 94) of patients [40]. Although formal peer support was associated with reduced treatment delay (p = 0.045), only 1.7% (n = 2) and 6.7% (n = 8) reported engagement with one-on-one peer support or group peer support, respectively. Second, an observational study by Matheson et al. used qualitative interviews to explore peer support experiences among young adults with Hodgkin lymphoma in their first year following initial treatment [43]. This study reported a need for more age-specific peer support regarding body image, fertility, sexual relationships, and work since their informal experiences with peer support promoted acceptance and normalization of the threats that accompanied their diagnosis and treatment [43].

Potentially Negative Consequences of Peer Support

Three studies reported on potentially negative consequences of peer support that could make some patients hesitant about engaging with peer support programs [26–28]. Symes et al. highlighted that peer advice that encouraged patients to “rely on others” could be perceived as unhelpful by patients who did not have effective support networks [28]. Further, Nørskov et al. reported that significant differences between patients and their peer supporters could result in poor mentorship [26, 27]. First, differences in life phases, diagnoses, and other patient factors (e.g., availability of reliable caregivers, gender) could hinder the effective establishment of a peer support relationship and cause the alienation of some patients [26, 27]. Second, differences in diagnoses and survivorship phases could lead to an inappropriate exchange of knowledge and experiences [26, 27].

Training and Supervision of Peer Supporters

Three studies indicated some training of peer supporters before they participated in the peer support programs [26, 27, 29]. Two studies by Nørskov et al. described the training provided to their peer supporters, including an obligatory 6-h course led by study team clinicians and researchers [26, 27]. The course covered various program-related content, communication skills, disease and treatment information, and skills to manage psychological issues that may arise in peer mentoring [26, 27]. Further, the peer supporters interviewed in the study by Myrhøj et al. received 6 h of peer support training and were certified peer support providers prior to beginning their program [29].

Additionally, Nørskov et al. reported that the study team lead and study psychologist offered group supervision to peer supporters every 6 weeks with an option for individual sessions with the study psychologists. Supervision allowed for support to manage challenges such as establishing rapport with the patient or helping patients cope with treatment failure [26, 27].

Risk of Bias and Study Quality

All eight studies exhibited key methodological strengths such as detailed descriptions of study objectives, study population, and recruitment process. However, adherence to each NHLBI criterion was inconsistent across studies (Table 2). Two studies had an overall rating of “good” and showed a low risk of bias (quality assessment scores = 9–11) [27, 30], two studies were rated “fair” and demonstrated a moderate risk of bias (quality assessment scores = 5–8) [26, 40], and four studies were rated “poor” with a high risk of bias (quality assessment scores = 0–4) [28, 29, 42, 43]. Increased risk of bias was primarily due to lack of participant/provider blinding (when applicable) and lack of sample size justification, power description, or variance and effect estimates. Six studies that lacked a representative study sample raised concerns about sampling bias [27–29, 40, 41, 43]. Four studies also used self-report measurements, raising some concerns of potential social desirability bias [27, 29, 41, 44] and recall bias [29]. Finally, some articles did not report data on some of the criteria evaluated in the quality assessment tools.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review aimed to synthesize findings from all studies that described peer support and its associations with outcomes in patients with hematologic malignancies or those who have undergone HSCT and found that only eight studies have examined peer support in these vulnerable populations. Most studies (6/8) were intervention studies. While five of our identified studies (i.e., four intervention, one observational) used qualitative methods to explore peer support [17, 26–28, 43], two intervention studies examined and reported the association between peer support and various clinical outcomes (e.g., symptom burden, psychological distress, QOL) using validated instruments and quantitative assessments pre-post intervention [27, 30]. Only one study was a randomized controlled trial that compared a peer-based intervention with treatment as usual or other non-peer-based interventions [30]. Hence, while all studies suggest potential benefits of peer support for patients with hematologic malignancies and/or HSCT recipients, more research is needed to establish the true impact of peer support and/or peer support interventions in these populations.

Four studies, three intervention and one observational, suggest that peer support positively impacts various important outcomes, including QOL, psychological well-being, and social well-being [27, 30, 40, 43]. These findings are consistent with the potential benefits of peer support in other medical populations where peer support has been associated with increased health behaviors (i.e., physical activity) [45], improved functional social support [2], and enhanced self-efficacy [46]. Further, our findings are also consistent with prior work in patients with a variety of solid malignancies where peer support has been associated with a variety of important clinical outcomes such as improved mood and QOL [47–50]. Although few of our synthesized studies utilized validated instruments to assess outcomes, patients’ qualitative reports of the benefits of peer support are compelling and should be explored further with longitudinal studies. Patients’ attestations that peer support could have a beneficial role in their psychological well-being highlight the potential for peer support interventions to positively contribute to the unmet mental healthcare needs in this population, especially when access and utilization of specialty mental health care services are limited.

Two observational studies specifically highlighted the need for more peer support resources in patients with hematologic malignancies or those undergoing HSCT [40, 43]. Our extensive and rigorous systematic search, which yielded only eight studies that described or reported any data on peer support in the populations at hand, also supports the dearth of research and work aiming to understand peer support in the lived experiences of these vulnerable populations. Since patients in qualitative studies clearly articulated potential psychological, social, functioning, and QOL needs that peer support resources could bolster, there is a critical need for longitudinal studies that use rigorous qualitative and quantitative approaches to further examine peer support in these populations and to inform the development of effective supportive interventions that incorporate peer support.

Three studies highlighted the potential negative consequences of peer support programs, especially pertaining to differences in patient factors (e.g., age, gender, diagnoses, social networks) that could result in an inappropriate exchange of knowledge and patient alienation [26–28]. Prior work has also suggested that the psychological burden that peer supporters have to manage in helping patients could contribute to worse psychological health among peer supporters [8, 51]. Although few studies provided details about training and supervision of peer supporters, these concerns about peer support underscore the importance of rigorous continuous training of peer supporters on various topics especially pertaining to guiding patients to manage challenging emotions during treatment and in recovery. Hence, effective training of peer supporters should also be studied alongside the development of peer support programs in this population.

This systematic review has several notable limitations. First, despite a rigorous systematic search and review with broad inclusion criteria, we only yielded eight articles for analysis, limiting our ability to comprehensively describe peer support and its association with outcomes. Relatedly, some articles were based on the same dataset, so this review is based on eight articles but only five original datasets. Second, most of the studies employed qualitative methods with relatively small sample sizes, which are not representative and raise concerns about the generalizability of study findings. Third, the few studies that used validated instruments to assess outcomes were cross-sectional, limiting our ability to examine the causal relationships between peer support and outcomes. Fourth, for studies that reported the sociodemographic data of their sample, the majority of study participants were White with minimal representation from minority populations—prior evidence suggests that the social support needs of patients from ethnic minority or underserved populations are unique [52]. Finally, the small sample size and the limited use of quantitative assessments precluded us from conducting a meta-analysis.

In conclusion, peer support is potentially beneficial for various clinical outcomes, including psychological and QOL outcomes among patients with hematologic malignancies or those who have undergone HSCT. However, observational studies that elucidate the role of peer support and its association with diverse clinical outcomes in these populations are critical but limited. Hence, more prospective studies and randomized controlled trials with robust sample sizes are essentially needed to comprehensively describe the association between peer support and outcomes in patients with hematologic malignancies or those who have undergone HSCT, which can then inform the development of supportive oncology interventions.
