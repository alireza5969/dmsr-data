Addressing the Third Epidemic of Retinopathy of Prematurity Through Telemedicine and Technology: A Systematic Review

Abstract

The rising prevalence of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) in low- and middle-income countries has increased the need for screening at-risk infants. The purpose of this article was to review the impact of tele-medicine and technology on ROP screening programs. Adhering to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, a systematic review was performed using PubMed, ProQuest, and Google Scholar bibliographic search engine. Terms searched included retinopathy of prematurity, telemedicine, and tele-ophthalmology. Data regarding internet access and gross domestic product per capita were obtained from the World Bank. Information was also obtained about internet access, speeds, and costs in low-income countries. There has been increasing integration of telemedicine and technology for ROP screening and management. Low-income countries are using available internet options and information and communications technology for ROP screening, which can aid in addressing the unique challenges faced by low-income countries. This provides a promising solution to the third epidemic of ROP by expanding and improving screening and management. Although telemedicine systems may serve as a cost-effective approach to facilitate delivery of health care, programs (especially in low- and middle-income countries) require national support to maintain its infrastructure.

INTRODUCTION

The internet has revolutionized modern life on a scale that is nearly unparalleled in history. Telecommunications in health care dates back to 1906 when Wilhelm Einthoven, the inventor of the electrocardiograph, conducted remote consultations via the telephone network.1 Another early application of telemedicine was with the National Aeronautics and Space Administration and the 1995 Spacebridge to Russia, which connected medical institutions in Russia and the United States and transmitted medical information and image data.2

Despite the success of telemedicine in industrialized nations, few large-scale systems are currently in place. The benefits of telemedicine include the ability to extend access to health care to remote regions, reduce travel time for health care professionals, streamline provision of care, and create a searchable database of medical records for research purposes or medical litigation.3–5 Telemedicine is suitable for image-based fields, including radiology, dermatology, and pathology. In ophthalmology, telemedicine has widespread applications, especially in retinal diseases including retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), and the potential to further expand in low- and middle-income countries. The objectives of this article were to review: (1) information and communications technology (ICT) available for telemedicine; (2) internet access and economic status of countries at an increased risk for higher prevalence of ROP; (3) framework of established ROP telemedicine programs; and (4) cost-effectiveness of telemedicine.

REVIEW OF ROP

Methods

Adhering to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Figure A, available in the online version of this article), a systematic review was performed using PubMed, ProQuest, and Google Scholar bibliographic search engine.6 Terms searched included retinopathy of prematurity, telemedicine, and tele-ophthalmology (Table A, available in the online version of this article). Data regarding internet access and gross domestic product per capita were obtained from the World Bank. Information was also obtained about internet access, speeds, and costs in low-income countries.

ROP: First, Second, and Third Epidemics

ROP is a vasoproliferative retinal disease in premature infants characterized by abnormal vessel growth at the boundary of the vascular and avascular retina. In the 1940s, an epidemic of severe ROP emerged in developed countries, likely secondary to uncontrolled use of supplemental oxygen in neonates. The second epidemic of ROP occurred in the late 1970s and 1980s due to increased survival of low birth weight infants.7 Advances in ROP screening programs have been made in high-income countries with low infant mortality rates (< 8 per 1,000 live births), where the prevalence of blindness caused by ROP is comparably low and largely restricted to infants with extremely low birth weights. In low-income countries with high infant mortality rates (> 60 per 1,000 live births), ROP is historically a rare cause of blindness because neonatal care is not readily accessible and preterm infants generally do not survive to develop ROP.8,9

However, in middle-income countries where neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) are being developed, ROP is emerging as a leading cause of childhood blindness. The increasing incidence of ROP in these countries, predominantly in Latin America and former Soviet Union states, has been termed the “third epidemic.” Studies evaluating ROP requiring treatment indicate that the population at risk varies widely with respect to birth weight and gestational age.10 Factors contributing to the third epidemic include increased survival of premature infants, higher birth rates, lack of oxygen monitoring and regulating resources in NICUs, lack of screening and treatment programs, absence of awareness of the risk of ROP, shortage of professionals trained to manage ROP, and remote location of NICUs. The complexity of the third epidemic is multi-faceted and partly due to the lack of consensus in ROP diagnosis and treatment.

Screening and Management of ROP

Since the second epidemic of ROP, high-income countries have delineated screening guidelines to detect at-risk infants. These guidelines vary between countries, and the criteria used in high-income countries are poorly suited to detect every case of ROP in low-income countries.11–14 There is a need to establish screening programs with criteria specific to each country.14–16

The development of the International Classification for ROP (ICROP) in 1984 facilitated multicenter trials that laid the foundation for establishing treatment standards.17 The Cryotherapy for ROP and Early Treatment for ROP trials demonstrated the importance of timely evaluation and treatment of at-risk infants to prevent significant visual morbidity.18,19

INTERNET ACCESS AND TELEMEDICINE

Challenges of ROP Care

Despite the advances in understanding the etiology of ROP and establishing treatment standards, hurdles remain with regard to the adverse legal climate, high cost of liability insurance, complex multi-disciplinary approach, and variability in classification of ROP, especially plus disease.20–23 Furthermore, there is a paucity of ROP-trained ophthalmologists in remote areas and inadequate ROP training during residency and fellowship.24 Alternative approaches are needed to address these challenges.

Internet in Low-Income Countries

Over the past two decades, internet access has grown exponentially worldwide. Between 1999 and 2012, the number of internet users globally increased nearly 1,700% from 150 million to 2.5 billion users.25,26 By the end of 2012, there were 640 million fixed broadband subscriptions in the world—a 9% global penetration rate. Although more than half of these subscriptions were in high-income countries, this growth has recently spread to middle-income countries in Latin America, Central and Eastern Europe, and East Asia and the Pacific.26

Table 1 displays reported rates of ROP relative to gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and internet accessibility in middle-income countries.27–31 The data in Table 1 reflect each country’s GDP per capita and information about internet infrastructure in 2017. Of note, the average internet speeds account for higher-speed connections in major cities. Lower connection speeds in rural regions are not considered separately, although it is in these locations where ROP screening may go amiss. However, there are successful telemedicine programs that have used currently available internet options. For example, in India where the average internet speed was reported to be 6.5 mbps, ROP telemedicine programs that rely on image upload can run within that bandwidth and are even capable of running at lower speeds.31,32 Similarly, in South Africa where the average internet speed was reported to be 6.7 mbps, a tele-ophthalmology program that uses teleconferencing was conducted at 384 kbps.31,33 These examples are described in further detail in subsequent sections of this review.

Wired communication such as Integrated Services Digital Network (ISDN) involves the transmission of information through cables of various materials (eg, fiber optics, copper, etc).34 However, this requires intense labor to establish the networks. In low-income countries, internet services rarely spread beyond the capital and a few large metropolitan centers.25 Internet service providers contribute to the disparity between urban and rural regions by charging higher prices for services in rural areas and distance away from the capital.25 Internet penetration in low-income countries may increase in rural areas if new technologies such as wireless internet technology are deployed.

Alternative Technology to Enhance Internet Connectivity

Low-cost, low-power, long-range, and high-bandwidth wireless technology is required to deploy telemedicine systems for digital image-based diagnosis in low-income countries.35 Research into cost-effective technologies in sparsely populated regions has led to two potential solutions. First, WiFi (IIEE 802.11), which can be massively produced at a cost of only US$5, delivers bandwidth of up to 54 mbps and spans up to 88.7 km.36 It has been shown to be successfully implemented in several countries.37–41 Second, CDMA450 is a cellular technology that has reduced costs by lowering the band frequency (450 MHz) and can achieve a larger coverage base per station (49 km radius).36

Rather than relying on networks that are more difficult to build and support in rural areas for real-time, synchronous communication, telemedicine systems can operate on delay tolerant networks (DTNs). A DTN is a system that allows information to be communicated in pulses rather than in a constant fashion.42 Such technology is capable of the store-and-forward modality where data are collected at one time point and analyzed at a separate time point, in contrast to real-time telemedicine technologies. The store-and-forward method is helpful for ROP telemedicine and can compensate for the power outages that form a basic hurdle for ICT in low-income countries.42–44

Telemedicine Programs Using Available Internet Options in Middle-income Countries

Reports of successful telemedicine programs in low-income countries are few compared to the body of literature discussing the potential uses for telemedicine. Evidence of telemedicine networks providing clinical services in low-income countries shows that they are used primarily for disaster relief and for obtaining second opinions.45 One such tele-medicine program is the RAFT (Réseau en Afrique Francophone pour la Télémédecine) network, created in 2001 and coordinated by the Geneva University Hospitals, which connects physicians in “medical deserts.” The system uses a tool called Dudal, which was developed with JAVA and is supported by a low-bandwidth connection (20 to 30 kbps) via a satellite connection.46 The network also used iPath, a web-based platform used for medical communications to manage and transfer complex cases to a hospital.

The use of an ISDN as a means to transmit voice, video, and other data has been shown to be successful in various underserved regions. In rural areas of South Africa, the National Telemedicine System provided health and educational services to 28 pilot sites in 6 provinces. The nation’s Department of Health recruited the company Siemens to provide the telemedicine equipment and Telekom SA, a telecommunications provider, to install ISDN connections, consisting of two basic-rate lines of 256 kbps set up at every location.47

One of the sites included the Edendale Hospital in KwaZulu-Natal, which established a tele-ophthalmology program, consulting with Moorfields Eye Hospital in London, United Kingdom.33 Evaluation of cases was facilitated through the use of a Sony PCS5100 videoconferencing unit at 384 kbps via an ISDN. Even when the connection ran at a lower speed at 256 kbps or landlines were disabled, the image quality was not affected, and the digital network seemed to work effectively.33 The Moorfields Eye Hospital tele-ophthalmology program provided services to various other locations in the African continent and South East Asia. The tele-ophthalmology program was run via a secure web-based application (SSL encryption).48

Similar findings were noted in China when The Chinese University of Hong Kong Faculty of Medicine funded the establishment of telemedicine programs. Videoconferencing from those sites required a 384 kbps connection or multiple lines of 64 kbps bidirectional flow of signals.49 Although effective, adopting an ISDN incurs a cost in rural areas. In addition to the installation fee, the cost of ISDN use at Edendale Hospital, for example, was 41 pence or US$0.53 per minute per line, excluding the cost of operation on the receiving end at Moorfields Eye Hospital.50

WiLDNet is a system that provides network coverage to rural areas by using point-to-point high bandwidth links that cost less than US$1,000 and provide hundreds of kilometers of coverage. It was deployed at Aravind Eye Hospital (AEH) and its telemedicine network in 2007. This system consisted of a total of 11 wireless routers and 9 point-to-point links. It was shown to be financially stable and self-sustaining and expanded to several other vision centers and hospitals.51,52

FRAMEWORK

Telemedicine Programs for ROP

Telemedicine systems for ROP screening in infants are feasible solutions to address the third epidemic of ROP. Studies in high-income countries, including the Stanford University Network for Diagnosis of Retinopathy of Prematurity (SUNDROP), have demonstrated the efficacy of retinal imaging by nonphysicians as an alternative to binocular indirect ophthalmoscopy for diagnosis of ROP, including disease requiring treatment.53–55 Weaver and Murdock56 implemented the SUNDROP protocol to conduct a digital image–based ROP screening program in rural Montana under the surveillance of pediatric ophthalmologists. The authors reported the successful referral and management of cases requiring treatment.56 The telemedicine approaches to evaluating the acute-phase ROP (e-ROP) group found that a team of nonphysician imagers and nonphysician image interpreters captured adequate quality images 91% of the time and provided a diagnosis with a 90% sensitivity and 87% specificity when performing bilateral assessment for referral-warranted ROP.57 The development of portable imaging technology enables the capture of diagnostic quality digital retinal images and transmission of those images via the internet. This technology can be coupled with the expansion of the internet into regions where the third epidemic has taken hold. Table B (available in the online version of this article) describes countries with established telemedicine screening programs for ROP.32,55,58–66 These examples suggest that modern internet connections and speeds in middle-income countries are sufficient to run telemedicine programs for ROP.

The Karnataka Internet-assisted Diagnosis of Retinopathy of Prematurity (KIDROP) program was started in 2008 by ophthalmologists in Bangalore, India, and covers 81 NICUs.62 A validated training team captures images with the RetCam Shuttle (Clarity Systems, Inc).63 Images are sent from a laptop with Tele-Care (i2i Telesolutions) software to ROP specialists at the Narayana Netralaya Postgraduate Institute of Ophthalmology via a secure tele-ROP platform.62 With internet speeds ranging between 128 kbps and 3.1 mbps, time to upload a set of images ranges between 1.67 and 15.5 minutes.32 Specialists view the images on a smartphone and provide a report in approximately 3.67 minutes.32 Infants with type 1 ROP are prioritized. The system allows mothers to receive a diagnosis for their infant before returning to their rural homes.

A study in Lima, Peru, demonstrated the feasibility of an internet-based telemedicine system where neonatal nurses were trained to obtain digital fundus images using a 30° NIDEK NM200-D camera (Nidek Co, Ltd).64 Five remote specialists evaluated the image sets using a secure internet database. The portable imaging equipment costs US$16,000 compared to the US$60,000 RetCam Shuttle. Images were saved as JPEG files and exported from the NIDEK platform.64

The AEH is a network of five self-sustaining hospitals in Tamil Nadu, India, that has focused on sustainably eradicating preventable blindness since its inception using existing technology. Physicians at the hospital had the ability to videoconference and connect with “eye camps” in rural areas using a small-aperture terminal (VSAT) satellite connection at 384 kbps.58 The Retinopathy of Prematurity Eradication-Save Our Sight (ROPE-SOS) currently has screening programs established in Tamil Nadu and Kerala.59 Through various software platforms, ROP images are quickly transmitted to a ROP specialist at the base hospital. After grading the report, the diagnosis and plan is submitted back to the physician at the screening site within 15 minutes.60

Limitations in directly comparing telemedicine networks and software functionality include variability in the telecommunication system, infrastructure, and specific uses. Future studies could use the “Tele-medicine Benchmark” to measure logistical parameters and performance of the various programs.67 The Model for Assessment of Telemedicine (MAST) is a tool that has been used to evaluate telemedicine programs in European countries. A similar tool can be created for low-income countries to better assess the quality and effectiveness of telemedicine programs.68

Artificial Intelligence in ROP

The development of artificial intelligence in the form of computer-based image analysis systems has the potential to address both the shortage of workers and the subjectivity of ROP diagnosis. There have been numerous studies on computer-aided quantification of vascular features in ROP, such as the use of the ROPtool and i-ROP system that produces precise, numerical measurements.69–71 Finally, digital imaging for the remote diagnosis of ROP has allowed the creation of a repository of images that can be used for tele-education programs to train ophthalmologists on how to diagnose and manage ROP.72 This increases exposure to and may standardize training in ROP.

COST ANALYSIS OF TELEMEDICINE FOR ROP

Cost and Cost-effectiveness of Telemedicine for ROP

The set-up and maintenance expenses of a tele-medicine network include equipment, training personnel, ICT, clinical examinations, diagnostic tests, transportation, treatment, and admission fees.50 Cost analyses are limited in the literature, especially for telemedicine in ophthalmology, and there is a need for formal health economic analyses.73 There are business models for telemedicine programs around the world that rely on a combination of private and public sector support.

Previous literature has supported the cost-effectiveness of ROP telemedicine programs.74,75 In a study from Columbia University, the cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) for standard ophthalmoscopy was more than US$2,000 greater than cost per QALY for telemedicine.76 In low-income countries without existing telemedicine networks, it is important to consider the additional cost of creating the infrastructure. This includes accounting for the cost of transportation of infants requiring urgent treatment. In the study by Weaver and Murdock,56 it was estimated that the cost of a single patient transportation via plane ranged between US$8,100 and $9,270. One way to reduce the initial cost of a telemedicine system is by using more affordable imaging technology. A pilot study for a telemedicine screening system for ROP in Lima, Peru, demonstrated high sensitivity, specificity, and reliability despite using a less expensive narrow-field camera (NIDEK NM200-D).64 The NIDEK camera is highly portable, which enables easy transport into remote areas for screening, and is roughly one-fourth the cost of the RetCam Shuttle. The telemedicine system at the AEH is unique in that the costs of the most marginalized populations are predominantly covered by revenue from paying patients and a large market base.58

Supporting Infrastructure for Telemedicine for ROP in Low-income Countries

Although telemedicine has a decades-long history in high-income countries, and large-scale tele-medicine operations are common in some medical fields (eg, dermatology or radiology) in high-income countries, ICT infrastructure for telemedicine is dependent on economic status and is fledgling in low-income countries.77,78 In the United States, funding from the federal government fueled research for telemedicine and hastened its diffusion in the health care sector. Indeed, the government played an integral role not only in funding telemedicine research, but also in ensuring the country’s networked readiness for the deployment of telecommunications in the medical realm. Historically, this model has worked in high-income countries where the telecommunications sector had resided in the public domain. However, Beardsley et al34 identified several trends that have altered the status quo that are pushing governments away from direct control of the telecommunications sector toward market-based mechanisms. These include accelerating technological change, which often exceeds the pace at which state-owned companies adapt, growing political consensus regarding benefits of open market, stakeholders pushing for change, and increasing fiscal pressure on governments. Beardsley et al noted that in low-income countries, the funding needed to bring the telecommunications network to scale can realistically only be attained through market-based reform. Reform has begun in developed and emerging countries in the form of liberalization and privatization. Compared to countries where reform has not occurred, countries that have begun to adopt measures of reform have seen a greater increase in internet penetration as of 2000.34

This research has important implications for the use of telemedicine in low-income countries because it defines what economic changes are needed to implement internet-based programs. A recent analysis of the use of ICT for health in low-income countries based on the Center for Health Market Innovations database found that funding for telehealth programs comes largely from the private sector, and the government is the main source of funding for only 22% of telehealth programs.79 This poses an issue in that these programs become reliant on donor funding, inhibiting self-sustainability and growth.79

Sustainability is a key aspect of successful tele-medicine programs. A needs assessment of the local habits and practices is essential in the success of the program, and initial and ongoing training of local users in ICT is vital to the continued viability of the program.80,81

CONCLUSION

This review identifies the potential for telemedicine ROP programs in low- and middle-income countries based on internet options. The key points include: (1) incorporation of telecommunication technologies to expand access of care to rural areas; (2) current telemedicine programs using available internet options; (3) impact of internet speed and its potential in telemedicine for ROP; and (4) cost analysis of telemedicine for ROP. The rise in ROP prevalence among middle-income countries has demanded the attention of local eye hospitals, national governments, and academic institutions in addressing the third epidemic. Globally, premature infants are at risk for blindness due to the lack of international consensus in establishing diagnostic criteria, the absence of national screening guidelines, poor management of infants in the NICUs, and a scarcity of ophthalmologists competent in the field of ROP. The creation of the internet as a network connecting near and far, and its incorporation within societies, has been key in the operation of ROP telemedicine programs especially in low-income countries.
