Prevalence of multidimensional frailty and pre-frailty in older people in different settings: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Frailty is a common condition in older people. The epidemiological data available, however, are mainly based on the physical frailty phenotype. An extensive literature has suggested that frailty should be identified using a multidimensional approach. Based on these recommendations, we estimated the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty in the older population, using the multidimensional prognostic index (MPI), a common tool for defining multidimensional frailty. We searched several databases until 10th May 2021 for studies reporting the prevalence of frailty according to MPI values. MPI was categorized, where possible, in < 0.33 (robustness), 0.33–0.66 (pre-frailty) and > 0.66 (frailty) or using a RECursive Partition and AMalgamation approach. A meta-analysis of the prevalence, with the correspondent 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of pre-frailty and frailty was performed stratified by setting (population-based, ambulatory, nursing home, and hospital). Among 177 papers initially screened, we included 57 studies for a total of 56,407 older people. The mean age was 78.6 years, with a slight prevalence of women (58%). The overall prevalence of multidimensional frailty (MPI-3) was 26.8% (95% CI: 22.1–31.5), being higher in nursing home setting (51.5%) and lower in population-based studies (13.3%). The prevalence of pre-frailty (MPI-2) was 36.4% (95%CI: 33.1–39.7), being higher in hospital setting (39.3%) and lower in nursing home (20%). In conclusion, frailty and pre-frailty, according to a multidimensional definition, are common in older people affecting, respectively, one person over four and one over three. Our work further strengths the importance of screening frailty in older people using a multidimensional approach.

Introduction

1.

Frailty is typically associated with a decline in functioning in multiple physiological systems with a resultant elevated vulnerability to stressors.(Hoogendijk et al., 2019) Frailty exposes to a greater risk of multiple adverse health outcomes such as loss of mobility, falls, hospitalization, institutionalization and increased risk of mortality.(Clegg et al., 2013) Several studies explored the prevalence of frailty across different settings such as in community dwellers (Collard et al., 2012) or in hospital (Cunha et al., 2019), but were mainly based on phenotypic model of frailty condition.(Fried et al., 2001) Despite consolidating evidence sustaining the importance of multidimensional approach to frailty rooted on comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) (Pilotto et al., 2020a; Rodríguez-Mañas et al., 2013; Turner and Clegg, 2014), there is still a lack of estimates of frailty across different clinical settings, based on a multidimensional approach.

The validated CGA-based diagnostic and prognostic tool called the Multidimensional Prognostic Index (MPI) is a common tool for clinical decision making able to capture different aspects of frailty condition according to multidimensional model.(Dent et al., 2019; Mattace-Raso and Pilotto, 2021) The MPI was originally developed and validated as prognostic index able to predict mortality in older hospitalized patients. (Pilotto et al., 2008) Furthermore, the MPI has been widely applied in hospital and/or ambulatory clinical settings to predict short- and long-term mortality and other negative health outcomes in subjects with different acute and chronic diseases such as heart failure (Pilotto et al., 2010), acute myocardial infarction and other cardiovascular (Cammalleri et al., 2021; Carriere et al., 2018; Van Mourik et al., 2019), gastrointestinal (Lenti et al., 2021; Pilotto et al., 2009a), chronic kidney (Aucella et al., 2012; Lai et al., 2020; Pilotto et al., 2012) diseases, as well as pneumonia (Pilotto et al., 2009b), acute respiratory failure (Custodero et al., 2021), COVID-19 (Pilotto et al., 2021; Verholt et al., 2021), dementia (D’Onofrio et al., 2016; Gallucci et al., 2014; Pilotto et al., 2009c), and cancer.(Giantin et al., 2018; Pata et al., 2021) (Brunello et al., 2016) A further MPI version, the MPI-SVaMA (Scheda Valutazione Multidimensionale Anziano) was developed and validated in community-dwelling older people who required a public health support including being admitted to a nursing home and/or homecare services.(Pilotto et al., 2013) A self-administered version of the MPI (SELFY-MPI) was developed and validated in the context of the EU co-funded EFFICHRONIC project as frailty screening tool for community-dwelling people (Pilotto et al., 2019b) underwent an evidence-based Chronic Disease Self-Management Programme (CDSMP) (Boone et al., 2019) earlier and its shorter version (SELFY-MPI-SF) in community-dwelling older people later.(Cella et al., 2020) Recently, also a telephone-administered version of the Multidimensional Prognostic Index (TELE-MPI) was introduced for remote monitoring of community-dwelling older adults in the framework of a telemedicine program.(Custodero et al., 2021) Robust evidence achieved from different multicenter studies showed that MPI is an excellent diagnostic tool in terms of reliability, validity and feasibility in clinical practice of older people. (Dent et al., 2016; Pilotto et al., 2020a; Warnier et al., 2016) Due to the importance to assess the presence of frailty in older adults, the MPI and its versions were used in several different settings that makes this tool ideal for mapping the prevalence of frailty in different settings.

Given this background, the aim of the present study is to explore the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty using the same tool, i.e., the MPI, and its versions in different settings in order to report a map of multidimensional frailty in older adults.

Methods

2.

This systematic review adhered to the PRISMA statement (Liberati et al., 2009) and followed a pre-planned, but unpublished protocol that can be requested by contacting the corresponding author.

The multidimensional prognostic index (MPI)

2.1.

The MPI could be calculated by the inclusion of information from eight different domains of the CGA (Pilotto et al., 2008) as follows:

  1. Functional status with the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) index (Katz et al., 1970);

  2. Independence in the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) (Lawton and Brody, 1969);

  3. Cognitive status through the Short Portable Mental Status Questionnaire (SPMSQ) (Pfeiffer, 1975);

  4. Co-morbidity was examined using the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale (CIRS) (Linn et al., 1968) The CIRS uses a 5-point ordinal scale (score 1–5) to estimate the severity of pathology in each of 13 systems. Based on the ratings, the Comorbidity Index (CIRS-CI) score, reflecting the number of concomitant diseases, were derived from the total number of categories in which moderate or severe levels (grade from 3 to 5) of disease were identified (range from 0 to 13).

  5. Nutritional status was investigated with the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) short form (SF) (Guigoz and Vellas, 1999), which includes information on several nutritional aspects;

  6. Risk of developing pressure sores was evaluated through the Exton Smith Scale (ESS) (Bliss et al., 1966);

  7. The number of drugs used by patients at admission was also recorded;

  8. Co-habitation categorized in living alone, in family (or with other support) and in institution.

For each domain, a tripartite hierarchy was used, i.e., 0 = no problems, 0.5 = minor problems, and 1 = major problems, based on conventional cut-off points derived from the literature for the singular items. The sum of the calculated scores from the eight domains was divided by 8 to obtain a final MPI risk score ranging from 0 = no risk to 1 = higher risk of mortality. The MPI is usually expressed as three categories of risk: MPI-1 low risk (robust participants, MPI value < 0.33), MPI-2 moderate risk (pre-frailty, MPI value between 0.34 and 0.66) and MPI-3 severe risk (frailty, MPI value > 0.66). (Pilotto et al., 2008) MPI requires between 15 and 25 min for its complete execution. (Pilotto et al., 2019a).

Since MPI is a plastic tool, other versions are available (Supplementary Table 1). For example, the MPI-SVaMA was initially developed and validated in 12,202 older community-dwelling individuals (Pilotto et al., 2013) and includes the following nine domains: (1) age, (2) sex, (3) main diagnosis, (4) nursing care needs (VIP), (5) cognitive status (VCOG), evaluated by the SPMSQ, (6) pressure sores risk (VPIA), evaluated by the Exton-Smith Scale, (7) activities of daily living (VADL) and (8) mobility (VMOB) evaluated by the Barthel Index(Mahoney and Barthel, 1965), and (9) social support (VSOC). (Pilotto et al., 2013) The SVaMA-MPI is therefore calculated using a weighted sum of each individual domain. (Pilotto et al., 2013) Similarly, the MPI was calculated in other contexts, such as in the InChianti study.(Pilotto et al., 2020b), the SNAC-K study (Swedish National Aging and Care in Kungsholmen, Sweden) (Angleman et al., 2015), the TILDA study (Irish Longitudinal Study on Ageing) (Veronese et al., 2020a) and the American Osteo-Arthritis Initiative. (Veronese et al., 2020b).

Data sources and Searches

2.2.

Two investigators (NV and CC) independently conducted a literature search using PubMed and SCOPUS without language restriction, until 10th May 2021. In PubMed, the following search strategy was used: (“multidimensional prognostic index” OR “MPI” OR “multidimensional frailty”). Reference lists of included articles were hand searched to identify any potential additional relevant articles. Any inconsistencies were resolved by consensus, with a senior author (AP), expert of the field.

Study selection

2.3.

Inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis were studies: (i) including adults (age > 18 years); (ii) reporting data on the prevalence of frailty (MPI-3) or pre-frailty (MPI-2). Studies were excluded if: (i) did not include humans; (ii) were conference abstracts; (iii) were written in languages other than English.

Data extraction

2.4.

Two independent investigators (NV and CC) extracted key data from the included articles in a standardized Excel spread sheet and a third independent investigator (AP) checked these data. For each article, we extracted data about authors, year of publication, country, setting, mean age, percentage of females, MPI values and, if possible, the values of the single domains of the MPI (for co-habitation, the percentage of people of living alone was extracted). When some information was missing, first and/or corresponding authors of the original article were contacted (one time in one month) to obtain unpublished data.

Outcomes

2.5.

The primary outcome of our investigation was the prevalence of frailty (MPI-3) and pre-frailty (MPI-2) according to the setting. For the MPI standard evaluation, frailty was defined as a value > 0.66, pre-frailty as values between 0.33 and 0.66. A similar approach was used for the versions of MPI for which exist a validation study, such as the SELFY-MPI (Zora et al., 2019) or the TELE-MPI. (Custodero et al., 2021).

For the versions of the MPI for which not univocal cut-offs exist for defining frailty and pre-frailty (e.g., SVaMA-MPI), we used the RECursive Partition and AMalgamation (RECPAM) algorithm (Ciampi, 1993), to identify the two best cut-off for creating categories of patients at different risks of overall mortality.

Data synthesis and analysis

2.6.

All analyses were performed using STATA version 14.0 (StataCorp). The primary analysis reported the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty (in %) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), applying a random-effect model. (DerSimonian and Laird, 1986) The data are reported by setting categorized in ambulatory, hospital, nursing home, and population-based studies. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed by the I2 metric and χ2 statistics, taking an I2 ⩾50% (p < 0.05) as a significant heterogeneity. For the hospital setting, we tried to explain the heterogeneity using a stratification by the medical condition of admission and the mean age and the percentage of females in a multivariable meta-regression analysis, reporting the data as betas with their standard error (SE).

Publication bias was assessed by visually inspecting funnel plots and using the Egger bias test.(Egger et al., 1997) Then, in case of publication bias, the trim-and-fill method was used, based on the assumption that the effect sizes of all the studies are normally distributed around the center of a funnel plot; in the event of asymmetries, it adjusts for the potential effect of unpublished (trimmed) studies.(Duval and Tweedie, 2000).

Finally, for descriptive purposes, we reported the mean values of MPI and its domains, by setting, weighted for the sample size.

For all analyses, a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

3.

Search results

3.1.

Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the PRISMA flow-chart. Overall, among 177 papers initially screened, we revised the full texts of 89 articles. Of them, 57 were eligible for the aims of our systematic review and meta-analysis (references list in Supplementary Table 2).

Study and patient characteristics

3.2.

Table 1 reports the main descriptive findings weighted for the sample sizes and stratified according to the setting of the studies included. Overall, 57 independent studies for a total of 56,407 participants were included, with a slight prevalence of females (=58.04%). Overall, 48/57 studies used the standard MPI, three the SELFY-MPI, two the SVaMA-MPI, one the TELE-MPI and the other three studies versions derived using the data already present in the datasets. The participants aged a mean of 78.59 ± 7.11 years: the population-based studies included the youngest people (mean age: 65.63 years) and the nursing home setting the oldest (mean age: 86.11 years).

In all the studies included, the mean MPI value was 0.39 ± 0.22, in a range between 0 and 1: in mean the lowest value was observed in the population-based studies (0.19), the highest in nursing home (0.75). Table 1 also reports data regarding the single domains of the MPI. In the ambulatory setting, for example, the data showed that people are, on average, slightly disabled in ADL/IADL and at risk of malnutrition, they used 4.57 medications while the prevalence of cognitive impairment assessed by SPMSQ was low. Moreover, about a quarter lived alone (24.55%). On the contrary, in nursing home setting, people were highly disabled, at high risk of malnutrition and pressure sores, used about 7 medications each day, and a large percentage had substantial cognitive problems. Finally, in hospital, we observed, in mean, values for ADL/IADL/MNA similar to those observed in ambulatory setting, as well as a similar number of medications. On the contrary the percentage of people living alone was practically doubled compared to the ambulatory setting (Table 1).

Prevalence of multidimensional frailty and pre-frailty in older people

3.3.

Fig. 1 reports the prevalence of frailty, as defined of people in the MPI-3 group, according to settings. Overall, the highest prevalence of frailty was observed in the nursing home setting (n = 2 studies; 4107 participants; prevalence=51.5%; 95%CI: 50.0–53.1%), whilst the lowest was observed in population-based studies (n = 6, 11,317 participants; prevalence=13.3%; 95%CI: 7.1–19.6%) (Fig. 1) (p for interaction <0.0001). The prevalence of frailty in hospital setting (n = 35 studies; 22,506 participants) was 29.8% (95%CI: 24.2–35.4%). After the meta-analysis, the overall prevalence of frailty in older people (MPI-3) was 26.9% (95%CI: 22.2–31.5%). The publication bias analysis suggested the presence of a possible bias (Egger’s test: = 11.4 ± 3.3; p = 0.001). However, the trim and fill analysis did not change our results.

A similar analysis was run for the prevalence of pre-frailty (MPI-2). For this outcome, the prevalence across the different settings was similar (32.8% in ambulatory, 39.3% in hospital, 33.7% in the population-based studies), whilst in nursing home we observed the lowest prevalence (20.0%) (Fig. 2) (p for interaction <0.0001). Using these data, the overall prevalence of pre-frailty in older people was 36.4% (95%CI: 33.1–39.7%). This outcome did not suffer on any publication bias (Egger’s test p-value=0.09).

Taken together the data of these meta-analyses, as reported in Fig. 3, the prevalence of frailty (MPI-3), independently from the setting was particularly high, as well as the prevalence of pre-frailty (MPI-2), even if they were significantly different according to the setting considered.

Multidimensional frailty and pre-frailty in hospital

3.4.

Since hospital included most of the studies (n = 35 studies) and participants (22,506) and since the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty was highly heterogeneous (I2 =99%), we did run some sensitivity and meta-regression analyses for explaining this issue. The highest prevalence of frailty was observed in respiratory conditions (e.g., acute respiratory failure and community acquired pneumonia) (=59.6%), the lowest among people suffering on cardiovascular conditions (12.4%), even if this latter condition we observed the highest prevalence of pre-frailty (MPI-2) (Supplementary Table 3). In a multivariable meta-regression analysis, adjusting for sex did not moderate the heterogeneity of these findings (beta=0.002 ± 0.003; p = 0.44), whilst each increase in one mean year was associated with an increase prevalence of frailty of 4% (beta=0.04 ± 0.001; p = 0.001).

Discussion

4.

In this work we summarized the current state of the art on the use of the MPI in older people. Overall, in about 57,000 older people, we found that pooled estimates of prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty, according to the multidimensional model of frailty, were 26.8% and 36.4%, respectively and varied widely across different clinical settings.

We believe that our findings could be important for several reasons. First, the epidemiological evidence regarding the prevalence of frailty is still limited to few systematic investigations that report an estimation of frailty only in one specific setting (e.g., community-dwelling) or using only one specific definition for frailty, mainly based on Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS) criteria identifying physical frailty. (Fried et al., 2001) Therefore even if of importance these works do not consider other definitions of frailty (Collard et al., 2012; Kojima, 2015), such as that based on multidimensional impairment. Second, we described for the first time, the prevalence of frailty using the MPI, a tool that indicates the presence of frailty, as assessed by a multidimensional approach, a novel conceptual model of frailty based on a hierarchical organization of different levels of complexity. (Ferrucci et al., 2017; Pilotto et al., 2020a) In particular, the multidimensional model of frailty includes the clinical and functional manifestations of frailty, e.g., reduced mobility, cognitive impairment, loss of independence in the basal and instrumental activities of daily living, malnutrition, co-habitation, multiple chronic diseases, polypharmacy, and geriatric syndromes.(Pilotto et al., 2020a) In this model, described by the MPI, frailty is conceptualized as the loss of harmonic interaction between domains posed at different levels (genetic, biological, functional, cognitive, psychological and socio-economic domain) that can be captured by the traditional CGA.

In particular, epidemiological data based on such frailty model suggest that the highest prevalence of frailty, as defined by MPI-3 group, can be observed in nursing home in which frailty affects about one older person over two (51.5%). This finding is consistent with those from previous systematic revisions, based on heterogenous frailty definitions, which estimated a prevalence ranging between 52.3% and 55.8% among institutionalized older adults.(Kojima, 2015) Limited evidence are available on frequency of frailty condition in hospital setting, with current estimates, derived from specific geographic area or single observational studies, ranging between 39% and 49%.(Cunha et al., 2019) Here, among over than 22,500 hospitalized older adults, we found a frailty prevalence, based on MPI, of 29.8%. Lower frailty prevalence was estimated among older patients observed in ambulatory activities (18.5%) or those community-dwelling (13.3%).(O’Caoimh et al., 2021) In the latter setting, recent meta-analysis showed a frailty prevalence around 12% according physical frailty model and around 24% for the deficit accumulation model. (O’Caoimh et al., 2021).

Regarding pre-frailty, as defined by MPI-2 group, we found the highest prevalence in hospital setting (39.3%) and the lowest in nursing home (20%), with a similar trend to what observed in a recent systematic revision carried out in South-American population. (O’Caoimh et al., 2021)At the same time in the hospital setting, we observed that pre-frailty is particularly high in people affected by cardiovascular conditions. We believe that this finding is probably due to a selection bias in our studies, since people included in this group should undergo TAVI (Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation), therefore should be not frail at the same time of the intervention. At the same time, our meta-analysis showed that pre-frailty is particularly high in this population and we have already shown that pre-frailty itself is a strong predictor of future cardiovascular conditions in older people.(Sergi et al., 2017).

Overall, our findings confirmed the idea that multidimensional frailty is a common condition in older people, but at the same time has different prevalence according to the setting that introduces the concept that CGA has different meanings (e.g., foundation for preventive or therapeutic strategies) in different settings. Moreover, CGA and its derived MPI tool, could help identify areas of health spectrum which deserve more aggressive approach based on impaired domains. For example, in community-dwellers, the role of the CGA could be the prevention of the transition from robustness, identified in our studies by the MPI-1 group, to pre-frailty and frailty. In this sense, the geriatrician should recommend some preventive interventions, such as vaccinations, increasing physical activity, nutritional advices and other non-pharmacological suggestions. In other settings in which frailty is, unfortunately, more common such as nursing home and hospital, the role of geriatricians is different. In particular, in this scenario, the geriatrician should prevent the transition from frailty to disability and finally to death. For reaching these aims other interventions are needed, such as decreasing unnecessary medications, improving nutritional status, resolving social issues and to optimize functional status suggesting, for example, physiotherapy.

This study has also some limitations. Firstly, our results should be taken with caution because, as reflected by the I2 values, there is a wide heterogeneity across the studies. Searching for potential reasons, for example among hospitalized older adults, we conducted sensitivity and meta-regression analysis that showed a significant contribution of age differences and main health problems (e.g., subjects with respiratory disease or cancer had almost two-time higher prevalence of frailty condition compared to overall estimate in this setting) on heterogeneity. Secondly, estimates on nursing home setting were based on only two papers, thus further studies should be encouraged to strengthen these findings. Thirdly, since the majority of the included subjects derived from hospital setting the overall prevalence of frailty might be slightly overestimated. Finally, present findings are mainly generalizable to Western countries. More data are needed about other geographical areas and low/middle-income countries.

In conclusion, with this work, we described the prevalence of frailty and prefrailty conditions using the MPI a common CGA-based tool assessing frailty through a multidimensional approach. Our findings suggest that geriatricians and people interested in geriatrics face different prevalence of frailty and consequently different challenges and aims according to the prevalence of frailty, across different settings. A multidimensional approach to frailty could help tailoring interventions targeting in earlier phase domain necessitating more intensive support.
