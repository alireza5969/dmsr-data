Inappropriate use of Thyroid Ultrasound: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

Abstract

Purpose:

Nearly half the cases of thyroid cancer, a malignancy rapidly rising in incidence within the United States, are attributable to small and asymptomatic papillary thyroid cancers that will not increase mortality. A primary driver of thyroid cancer overdiagnosis is ultrasound use. It is therefore valuable to understand how inappropriate use of thyroid ultrasound is defined, analyze the current evidence for its frequency, and identify interventions to lessen misuse.

Methods:

Ovid MEDLINE(R), Ovid EMBASE, and Scopus databases were searched from inception to June 2020 for studies assessing inappropriate use of thyroid ultrasound. Reviewers, working independently and in duplicate, evaluated studies for inclusion, extracted data, and graded risk of bias. We used a random-effects model with a generalized linear mixed approach to calculate the mean overall proportion estimates of inappropriate use.

Results:

Seven studies (total n= 1,573) met the inclusion criteria with moderate to high risk of bias. Inappropriate thyroid ultrasound use was described variably, using published practice guidelines, third-party expert reviewers, or author interpretations of the literature. The overall frequency of inappropriate thyroid ultrasound use was 46% (95%CI 15–82%; n=388) and 34% (95%CI 16–57%; n= 190) among studies using guideline based definitions; 17% (95%CI 7–37%; n= 191) was attributed to thyroid dysfunction and 11% (95%CI 5–22%; n= 124) to non-specific symptoms without palpable mass. No study examined interventions to address inappropriate use.

Conclusions:

Low quality evidence suggests that inappropriate use of thyroid ultrasound is common. Interventional studies aiming to decrease the inappropriate use of thyroid ultrasound are urgently needed.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer is one of the most rapidly increasing malignancies in the United States, with an incidence that has tripled over the last three decades [1]. Approximately 50% of the increased incidence is attributable to the identification of small and asymptomatic papillary thyroid cancers (PTCs) that will not increase mortality [2]. These small PTCs have been found in 11% of thyroid glands removed for benign thyroid conditions or examined at autopsy in patients who died of unrelated causes [3]. An estimated 17 million Americans are believed to unknowingly have small thyroid cancers of 1.5 cm or smaller. In fact, despite increased diagnosis and treatment of thyroid cancer, the mortality rate continues to be extremely low, given the overall indolent nature of most lesions [4]. The high incidence and low mortality have raised concerns about thyroid cancer overdiagnosis [5], or the detection of subclinical cancers never destined to cause harm. Thyroid cancer overdiagnosis exposes people to potential medical and financial side effects of treatment without an equal expectation of benefit, as the cancer is unlikely to advance [6]; patients with thyroid cancer are at higher risk of bankruptcy than patients with other types of cancer or those without cancer [6,7]. The detrimental personal and societal impact of thyroid cancer overdiagnosis demands a better understanding of its causes and interventions.

One of the drivers of thyroid cancer overdiagnosis is the use of thyroid ultrasound [2]. Thyroid ultrasounds have the potential to reveal incidentally small thyroid cancers in asymptomatic patients [8]. A cross sectional study [9], using the Georgia and Los Angeles Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registries, found that thyroid micro-carcinomas are more likely to be detected by ultrasound compared with other imaging modalities. Similarly, another study demonstrated that people living in regions with high use of thyroid ultrasound are more likely to be diagnosed with thyroid cancer [10]. Despite the association between thyroid ultrasound use and the detection of low risk thyroid cancer, the use of thyroid ultrasound continues to increase at a rate of 20% per year [11].

Given the increase in use of thyroid ultrasound and the rising prevalence of thyroid cancer, it is imperative to summarize what constitutes inappropriate versus appropriate use of thyroid ultrasound, analyze the current evidence regarding the frequency of inappropriate thyroid ultrasound use, and identify interventions to reduce its use.

Methods

This manuscript is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [12].

Eligibility criteria and study selection

We included original studies with patients older than 18 years that detailed the use of thyroid ultrasound and described at least two of three outcomes related to the inappropriate use of thyroid ultrasound (hereafter, abbreviated as iUS): 1) definition of iUS, 2) frequency of iUS, and 3) any intervention to decrease iUS. We did not exclude studies based on language or study design.

Data sources and searches

We applied a search strategy developed in collaboration with an experienced librarian (add) to find potentially eligible studies in Ovid MEDLINE(R) Epub Ahead of Print, In-Process & Other Non-Indexed Citations, Ovid MEDLINE(R) Daily, Ovid EMBASE, and Scopus from each database’s inception until June, 3rd 2020. Conference abstracts were included. Reference lists of selected studies were searched to identify additional publications. Our full search strategy is included as Supplementary Figure 1.

Study selection

Search results were uploaded into a systematic review software program (DistillerSR, Ottawa, ON, Canada) [13]. Reviewers, working independently and in duplicate, screened abstracts and titles for eligibility using standardized instructions and forms. For abstract screening, articles included by at least one reviewer were considered for full-text screening. At full text screening, only articles included by both reviewers were deemed eligible for this systematic review and cases of disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer (J.P.B). Abstract screening agreement using Cohen’s kappa was substantial (k=0.76).

Data collection and management

Two reviewers (M.K.E. and N.I.A) independently extracted data from all eligible studies using a spreadsheet form and compared results for accuracy and completeness, resolving discrepancies by consensus or by a third reviewer (J.P.B). Extracted variables included: 1) characteristics of the studies: author, date of publication, country and study design; 2) characteristics of the participants: age, sex; 3) definition and frequency of iUS; 4) factors influencing the use of iUS. For studies that included information about interventions to decrease iUS, we extracted the study design; type of intervention addressing iUS (e.g., clinician education, clinical decision support, provider feedback); the impact of the intervention on number of ultrasounds ordered and number of thyroid nodules or thyroid cancers avoided.

Study quality was assessed independently by two reviewers (E.L.N. and J.P.B) using the nine-star Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies [14,15], a tool comprised of three domains: selection of participants (population representativeness), comparability (adjustment for confounders), and ascertainment of outcomes of interests. Studies that scored nine to eight were judged to be at low risk of bias, studies that received a score of seven to five were considered at moderate risk, and those that scored four or less were considered at high risk of bias [14].

Data analysis

A random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. We used the number of people with iUS indications and the total number of people with thyroid ultrasounds in each study to determine the mean overall proportion estimates and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The mean overall proportion was estimated with generalized linear mixed models, as these models perform better with proportions close to 0 and 1 [2,16]. The confidence interval was calculated by using the exact method for binomial proportions [17]. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed with the I2 statistic and visually [18]. We considered that I2 < 25% reflected low inconsistency and I2 > 75% reflected high inconsistency. In terms of subgroups analyses, we performed predefined comparisons based on the reason for thyroid ultrasound and the definition used for iUS. Statistical analyses were performed with the statistical program R Studio [19].

Data repository

To improve the transparency of our research and facilitate evaluation, all documents used in the screening phases, tables, figures, and analysis codes are freely available on Figshare: https://figshare.com/s/94b8b01d083e87b0b30f.

Results

Study characteristics and risk of bias assessment

We included 7 studies [20–26] (Table 1) with a total of 1,573 participants, mean age of 52 years, and predominance of women (82%). All the studies were retrospective case series conducted in single centers, mostly in the United States, and including patients referred to endocrinology.

Furthermore, all of the included studies were deemed to be at moderate to high risk of bias due to selection bias, lack of blinding assessment, and ascertainment of outcomes (Supplementary Table 1).

iUS definition

All studies, except for one [25], provided a definition of inappropriate thyroid ultrasound. Two studies [20,21] defined inappropriate use by referencing practice guidelines (ultrasound ordered for functional thyroid disease [hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism] or neck pain without presence of palpable mass/nodule). Two additional studies used third-party reviewers to identify iUS (a committee of radiologists and family medicine physicians referencing the Canadian Association of Radiologist guidelines and their personal expertise [22], and a reviewing endocrinologist deeming the ultrasound to be unnecessary for diagnosis [24]). Two other studies used definitions derived from the authors’ interpretations of the literature (ultrasounds ordered in patients without histories of thyroid nodules or irradiation [23] and ultrasounds ordered due to hypothyroidism alone [26]).

Frequency of iUS and interventions to decrease overuse

Overall, the frequency of iUS was 46% (95% CI 15–82%; n=388, I2 99%) (Figure 1). For those studies using a definition based on practice guidelines [20,21], the pooled frequency was 34% (95% CI 16–57%; n= 190, I2 96%). The pooled frequency of iUS due to thyroid dysfunction (either hypothyroidism or thyrotoxicosis) was 17% (95% CI 7–37%; n= 191, I2 97%), due to hypothyroidism alone was 11% (95% CI 5–22%; n= 124, I2 94%), and due to thyrotoxicosis alone was 9% (95% CI 3–21%; n=67, I2 90%) (Figure 2). Moreover, the pooled frequency of iUS due to non-specific symptoms without a palpable mass was 11% (95% CI 8%−16%; n= 70, I2 60%) and due to other, unspecified reasons was 7% (95% CI 0–71%; n=33, I2 96%) (Figure 1).

No studies evaluating/developing interventions to decrease iUS were found.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of studies assessing the frequency of iUS, we found that ~ 46% of ultrasounds ordered to evaluate the thyroid were inappropriate. Of the few studies that reported the reason for iUS, the most common condition was thyroid dysfunction (17%), followed by non-specific symptoms without a palpable mass (11%). In addition, we found significant variation in the definitions used to assess inappropriateness. Only two studies [20,21] used a definition based on clinical guidelines and professional societies. One of these definitions was suggested by the Endocrine Society and the Choosing Wisely Campaign [27], which recommend against routine thyroid ultrasound in patients with abnormal thyroid function tests but no palpable abnormality of the thyroid gland. This guideline definition provides a good starting point to guide clinicians about the appropriateness of thyroid ultrasound, yet, it does not address many other clinical determinants of thyroid ultrasound use such as presence of compression symptoms. Although defining iUS might be challenging, even with the most conservative criteria based on guidelines, at least 1 in 3 patients undergo thyroid ultrasound inappropriately (34%).

This paucity of evidence about the frequency, drivers, and interventions to decrease iUS, in the context of the rising use of thyroid ultrasound and thyroid cancer overdiagnosis, represents a research gap with the potential to profoundly impact society. As iUS exposes a large reservoir of small and likely clinically irrelevant cancers, even a slight decrease in the number of iUS will significantly reduce the number of patients with thyroid cancer diagnoses. Haymart et al. [11] estimated that if the number of ultrasounds performed remained the same in 2002 compared to 2013, about 1,800 fewer thyroid cancers would be detected. In addition to the epidemiologic reduction in the number of thyroid cancer cases, avoiding the overdiagnosis of small thyroid cancers would prevent significant harm to patients. Patients with thyroid cancer diagnoses have a higher rate of bankruptcy than patients with other malignancies [6] and experience significantly higher rates of anxiety and depression [28,29]. Moreover, the majority of patients with thyroid cancer, regardless of size and method of diagnosis, are treated with thyroid surgery which can cause clinically significant morbidity associated with alterations in voice due to damage to recurrent and superior laryngeal nerves, hypoparathyroidism, and, rarely, airway obstruction associated with bilateral vocal cord dysfunction, hematoma formation, or infection [5,30,31]. This concern is particularly relevant when the surgery is performed by a low volume surgeon, which describes the majority of surgeons currently conducting these procedures [32].

Our study’s estimation of the frequency of iUS, although imprecise, is similar to another obtained in a recent cross sectional survey conducted on 610 physicians involved in thyroid cancer care [33]. In this survey, one in four clinicians self-reported the inappropriate use of ultrasound for investigating abnormal thyroid function tests. This highlights the need for interventions aimed at educating clinicians on best practices with neck/thyroid ultrasound and potential downstream harms of its misuse. In addition to this potential area of intervention, there is a need to understand other factors driving iUS, including the role of patient preferences (e.g., beliefs that more testing is better), organizational factors (e.g., payment structures), institutional culture, and guideline clarity.

The strengths of this review relate to its comprehensive literature search and the use of two independent reviewers for study selection. However, our study has several limitations. Due to the paucity of data, we could not conduct a more comprehensive meta-analysis or subgroup analyses; the analysis that was conducted led to imprecise results due to small sample sizes and the heterogeneity of included studies.

Conclusion

Low quality evidence suggests that approximately 46% of thyroid ultrasounds are ordered inappropriately and at least 34% are outside of the Endocrine Society and Choosing Wisely Campaign recommendations. Despite the potential impact of reducing iUS to decrease the cases of indolent thyroid cancer, our search did not find any intervention to address iUS. This is a research gap that demands additional studies.
