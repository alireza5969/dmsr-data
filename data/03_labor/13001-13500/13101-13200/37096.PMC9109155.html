Abstract

Background:

To date, the economic burden and patient-reported outcomes associated with carcinoid syndrome (CS) in neuroendocrine tumor (NET) patients remain largely unknown. The objective of this study is to perform a systematic review of economic and quality of life (QOL) studies related to the treatment of CS.

Methods:

Articles included in the review were extracted from PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane library. Studies had to be in English and published between 1/1/2000 and 7/2/2020. Other study eligibility criteria included: NET patients with CS receiving treatment for CS; study outcomes of cost or QOL; and clinical trials or population-based studies using claims or other secondary databases. The interventions included somatostatin analogues, telotristat ethyl, or other treatment for CS. To evaluate the quality and bias of the included studies, the 24-item CHEERS and 10-item Gill and Feinstein checklists were used. We report a narrative synthesis of the findings from the selected studies.

Results:

A total of 12 economic and 12 QOL studies met the inclusion criteria and were included for review. Patients with uncontrolled CS symptoms had 23%-92% higher costs than those with controlled CS; mostly, ambulatory/outpatient services were the primary drivers of the cost. The use of telotristat ethyl may be cost-effective if the societal willingness to pay is as high as $150,000 per quality-adjusted life-year in the US. Of the 12 QOL papers, only 3 case-control studies assessed QOL at baseline and more than 2 follow-up time points. Seven studies evaluated QOL at two or more time points but lacked a control group, obscuring direct intervention effects on patients’ wellbeing.

Conclusions:

We observed heterogeneity among the reviewed studies evaluating the economic burden and patient-reported outcomes, in terms of cost and quality of life, of patients with CS. While QOL is consistently impaired and costs are consistently increased by CS, the number of both cost and QOL studies among this patient population remains sparse, and many of the existing studies indicated an important need for quality improvement.

INTRODUCTION

The number of patients diagnosed with carcinoid syndrome (CS) and neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) has increased steadily since 2000 [1]. CS is a constellation of symptoms present among 19-20% of patients with NETs, which results from the release of serotonin and other bioactive amines from NETs into the bloodstream. Common symptoms of CS include wheezing, flushing, and diarrhea; advanced cases may present with right-sided fibrotic valvular heart failure requiring multi-valve replacement. It has been reported that over half of patients with CS develop heart disease [2]. Studies have found NET patients with CS have lower quality of life (QOL) [3] and worse survival than NET patients without the syndrome, even after controlling for common prognostic factors such as grade, stage, and primary tumor site [1]. Thus, the presence of CS is associated with multiple adverse clinical features and worse overall survival for these patients.

Resection is the primary treatment for localized NETs. Somatostatin analogues (SSAs), such as octreotide or lanreotide, are the recommended first-line treatment for advanced-stage NET patients with CS [4]. Other treatments for CS include telotristat ethyl (TE), which was approved in 2017 as a therapy added to SSAs for patients with CS who have an insufficient response to SSA therapy [2]. Peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), surgery, and liver-directed therapy are employed in practice to reduce hormone burden even for patients with advanced disease, yet existing studies have a low-level of evidence.

To date, limited information is available on the economic burden and patient-reported outcomes associated with CS. This study provides a comprehensive review of the extant literature to evaluate the impact of treatment options on several important outcomes of NET patients with CS. The objective of the study is to perform a systematic review of economic and quality of life studies related to the treatment of CS in order to uncover areas for future research.

METHODS

Eligibility criteria

We conducted a literature search in PubMed/Medline, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library. The following search strategies were applied:

For Medline (Ovid) + PubMed:

Economic studies: “(((“health care costs”[MeSH] OR costs[Title/Abstract/Keyword] OR cost effective*[Title/Abstract/Keyword]) OR (cost*[Title/Abstract/Keyword] OR “costs and cost analysis”[MeSH:noexp] OR cost benefit analysis*[Title/Abstract] OR cost benefit analysis[MeSH] OR health care costs[MeSH:noexp]))).” We then cross-referenced the above search filter with search terms related to carcinoid syndrome “((carcinoid syndrome) OR ((carcinoid syndrome) & (neuroendocrine)))”.

Quality of life studies: “quality of life” [MeSH] OR “quality of life” [Title/Abstract] or “well being”: ti,ab OR “QoL” (all fields). We then cross-referenced the above search filter with search terms related to carcinoid syndrome “((carcinoid syndrome) OR ((carcinoid syndrome) & (neuroendocrine)))”.

For EMBASE and Cochrane Library:

Economic studies: “[(“cost.ab or cost.ti or cost.kw) AND ((carcinoid syndrome).ab or (carcinoid syndrome).ti or (carcinoid syndrome).kw)] for EMBASE and “carcinoid syndrome” AND “cost” for Cochrane Library.

Quality of life studies: [((quality of life).ab or (quality of life).ti or (quality of life).kw) OR ((well being).ab or (well-being).ti or (well being).kw) OR (Qol.ab or Qol.ti or Qol.kw)] AND [(carcinoid syndrome).ab or (carcinoid syndrome).ti or (carcinoid syndrome).kw] for EMBASE and [“carcinoid syndrome” AND (“quality of life” or “Qol” or “well being”)] for Cochrane Library.

Finally, we included studies meeting the following criteria: 1) patients received treatment for CS; 2) interventions included SSAs or telotristat ethyl [5] or other treatment for CS symptom control; 3) the outcomes were cost, qualify-adjusted life years, health-related quality of life, incremental cost, incremental effectiveness, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratio; 4) articles published between January 1, 2000, and July 2, 2020; 5) studies reported in English; and 6) the setting of the studies were clinical trials or population-based studies using claims or other secondary databases. We excluded case reports or single institution studies. Research focusing on tumor control using systemic therapies in patients with advanced NET or carcinoid tumors without examining symptom control for CS were also excluded.

Study records:

We designed a “review template” in Excel to store and record data. Two independent reviewers (Pan, Kim) went through each phase of the review process (identification, screening, eligibility, and included) and extracted data independently. If the two reviewers' opinions were discordant, a third reviewer (Shih) joined the discussion to reach a mutual agreement.

We synthesized information collected from the included literature. For economic studies, we included authors, year of publication, country, sponsorship, target population, interventions, study method, results, and conclusions. Our review of study methods covered the following: types of economic evaluation (e.g., cost, cost-effectiveness, or budget impact), data sources, discounting, cost type, the reference year for cost, timeframe, analytical approach, and outcomes. For quality of life studies, information collected from each study included authors, year of publication, country, sponsorship, target population (age, groups, and sample size), interventions, study method, results, and conclusions. Study methods included QOL instrument used, domain(s) measured, analytical approach, and outcomes. We evaluated the quality and bias for the economic and QOL studies using a 24-item checklist in the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEER)[6] and a 10-item checklist by Gill and Feinstein [7, 8], respectively.

Synthesis:

We report a narrative synthesis of the findings from the selected studies. The information included and quality assessment criteria have been mentioned above. For quantitative data analysis, mean costs, mean effectiveness, and mean quality of life scores were reported. Cost data were converted to the same currency and normalized to 2019 US dollars using the medical care component of the consumer price index [9]. We converted the costs in other currencies to the $US using the purchasing power parity index (PPP) [10].

RESULTS

The PRISMA flowcharts describe the identification, screening, eligibility and inclusion steps used to uncover relevant cost and QOL studies of CS for this review (Figures 1-1 and 1-2) [11]. We identified 94 economic studies according to the search strategies. After removing 31 duplicates, 63 studies were screened for further inclusion criteria. After excluding studies published before 2000, conference papers, studies without CS treatment cost information, not original studies, and manuscripts not relevant to CS treatment costs, 14 studies were eligible for full-text review. Upon further assessment of the full-text articles, 12 studies were included in the final synthesis review. For QOL studies, we identified 245 studies using the search strategies described in the Methods section. One hundred and ninety studies were screened. After excluding papers published before 2000, single institution studies or case reports, studies without CS treatment outcomes of QOL, clinical trials without results reported, not original studies, and studies not relevant to CS outcomes, 14 studies were eligible for full-text article assessment. Finally, 12 QOL studies were included in the synthesis review.

Review of Cost of Carcinoid Syndrome

Among 12 studies that evaluated CS treatment costs, most studies employed the payer or health-sector perspective and estimated direct medical costs using payment from claims data of private or public insurance or drug acquisition cost. One study took the societal perspective and included productivity loss in its evaluation. Eight were retrospective cohort studies in the US, of which 7 used commercial claims for patients with mean age ranging between 50 and 66 years old. Among the study cohorts of these 7 studies, more than half were female and nearly 50% were from the South Census Region [12-18]. Only one cohort study focused on the elderly population in the US using the SEER-Medicare database [19]. The remaining four studies were economic evaluation studies that applied the Markov simulation model to compare SSA with TE from the payer perspective in Sweden (2), Canadian (1), and the US (1) [20-23]. All four studies were sponsored by the manufacturers of the evaluated drugs (Table 1).

Two studies found uncontrolled CS to be more costly than controlled CS among NET patients. Shen et al. estimated overall costs of CS among NET patients using Medicare reimbursement rates in inpatient and outpatient care settings, comparing patients who remained alive within the first year of care vs those who died. Mean monthly CS-related costs were significantly higher in patients who remained alive by $543 (95% CI: 348, 805) (p < .001) [19]. Another study in Sweden used a national payment database and found that the cost of uncontrolled CS was 40% higher than controlled CS [18]. In both studies, the higher cost was driven substantially by SSAs (Table 1).

Three studies estimated costs of CS for commercially insured patients; results suggested patients with diarrhea presented a significantly higher financial burden than those without diarrhea (p < 0.001) [12, 14, 15]. Broder et al. and Dasari et al. estimated annual mean direct medical and pharmacy costs for CS patients with diarrhea as $98,538 (in 2019 USD) and $105,153 (original cost estimate), and without diarrhea as $62,008 (in 2019 USD) and $54,701 (original cost estimate), respectively. Original cost estimates were reported if the study did not provide information on the reference year of costs, making it impossible to convert to 2019 USD. Median annual costs for productivity loss were $43,666 and $29,817 for CS patients with and without diarrhea, respectively (Table 1).

Two studies examined the impact of dose escalation for CS symptom improvement [13, 17]. According to Burton et al., who studied long-acting octreotide treatment, CS-related costs were significantly higher post-escalation (mean ± SD: $3,700 ± $4,369 vs $8,325 ± $9,579, p < 0.001), mostly driven by ambulatory care. Moreover, Huynh et al. studied above-standard dose octreotide-LAR treatment and found that mean annual costs in patients who experienced CS symptom improvement were significantly lower by approximately $15,000–$19,000 per patient than those without resolutions in flushing or diarrhea (Table 1).

Five studies compared costs between CS treatments. A study by Huynh et al. concluded that mean monthly all-cause and NET-related costs were significantly lower among patients who received long-acting octreotide relative to lanreotide-treated patients [16]. Four studies evaluated costs associated with CS symptom control using telotristat ethyl (TE) added to SSAs compared to SSAs only [20-23]. In a cost-utility analysis, the Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health reported annual mean drug costs were $28,541-$29,269 for standard-dose SSAs (octreotide acetate or lanreotide,13 injections, every four weeks), $29,269 for increased-dose SSAs (octreotide acetate, 26 injections, 2*30 mg, every four weeks), and additional $94,806 for TE (250 mg, three times a day) added to SSAs[23]. A cost-effectiveness study using US data conducted by Joish et al. estimated the incremental cost per QALY gained for SSA+TE versus SSA was $142,545. SSA+TE was cost-effective when the willingness-to-pay threshold was $150,000 [22]. The other two studies were budget impact analyses that estimated monthly or 4-week CS treatment costs in the US and Sweden. Joish and colleagues in the US used monthly treatment costs of $7,930 for somatostatin analog long-acting release (SSA LAR) and $11,749 for SSA LAR and TE to project a 3-year budget. The authors concluded that providing access to TE for patients whose CS symptoms were not adequately controlled on SSA LAR therapy would have a minimal budgetary impact to a US health plan [21]. Fust et al. in Sweden applied $1,464 (2019 USD, €982.27) for TE costs over a 4-week period, and estimated per patient per month drug costs to be $2,305 (€1,546) for SSA LAR and $2,877 (€1,602) for SSA LAR and TE, respectively [20]. The 5-year cumulative net budget impact of adding TE to current standard of care was $256,942 (€172,346), 44 patients were expected to initiate TE treatment (Table 1). Again, these data support a low budgetary impact of adding TE to standard of care for treatment of uncontrolled CS.

Assessment of study quality based on CHEERS for the economic studies of carcinoid syndrome

We conducted qualitative assessment of the 12 studies using 24 items in the CHEERS checklist and summarized our findings in Table 2. Overall quality of these studies was deemed acceptable by CHEERS criteria. However, most cost studies did not meet the following criteria: study perspective, discount rate, study parameters, and characterizing uncertainty [12-19]. Four studies did not report study perspective; seven studies did not report the choice of discount rate. Seven studies did not characterize the uncertainty undertaken with patient-level data in the choice of parameters or perspectives. Lastly, five studies did not report sources of study parameters and did not justify the values used to estimate costs. Two economic evaluation studies did not indicate the reference year of the estimated costs.

Review of Quality of Life of Patients with Carcinoid Syndrome

Tools and domain measures of quality of life for carcinoid syndrome

Among the 12 QOL studies reviewed, the most commonly used QOL measurement tool was the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) QLQ-C30 (8 studies used this questionnaire) [24-31]. This QOL instrument contains the following symptom scales: fatigue (3 questions), nausea and vomiting (2), dyspnea (1), insomnia (1), appetite loss (1), constipation (1), and diarrhea (1). Other measured items include physical (5), social (2), pain (2), emotional (4), global health status (2), role (2), cognitive (2), and financial difficulties (1) (Table 3) [32, 33]. Six of the 8 studies used EORTC QLQ-C30 combined with the disease-specific EORTC GINET 21 [34] questionnaires to evaluate CS control and quality of life [24-26, 29-31]. Two of the remaining four studies used the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy – General (FACT-G) [35] questionnaire plus Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS©) [36, 37]. One study used FACT-G plus a symptom-specific subscale measuring Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQOL) specific to diarrhea [38]. The other study used Index de Sante Perceptuel de Nottingham (ISPN), the French version of the Nottingham Health Profile (Table 3) [39].

The purpose of treatment for patients with CS is to reduce CS symptoms and improve quality of life. All tools had symptom scales, which are the essential dimension of quality of life evaluation for NET patients with CS. Two of these tools (EORTC QLQ-C30 and FACT-G) had specific measures for vomiting and diarrhea. The EORTC QLQ –GI.NET21 is a validated supplemental tool of EORTC QLQ-C30 specifically for assessing quality of life in patients with gut, pancreas, and liver NETs [34]. The GI.NET 21 questionnaire contains items on hot flashes, sweats, abdominal discomfort, and side effects from treatment.

Types of interventions and QOL results

We categorized twelve studies assessing QOL among NET patients with CS into two categories by line of therapy: seven studies on first-line therapy [24-27, 36, 37, 40] and five on second-line therapy [28-31, 38]. The first-line therapy studies focused on the treatment effects of three SSAs (lanreotide, octreotide, and pasireotide). In the second-line therapy studies, for those who had received first-line SSAs but showed inadequate control of CS symptoms, two medications were evaluated: addition of TE [28-31] and pasireotide after initial therapy with a different SSA (Table 4) [38].

Two of the first-line therapy studies assessing patients’ quality of life after lanreotide applied different approaches to analyze patient-reported outcomes in the ELECT trial [24, 25]. The ELECT trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of lanreotide depot/autogel 120 mg for the control of CS symptoms in patients with NETs [25]. Vinik et al. [25] compared global health status (from the EORTC QLQ-C30), gastrointestinal symptoms, and endocrine symptoms (from the QLQ-GINET21) in patients receiving lanreotide versus placebo. All scores between baseline and week 12 were improved in the lanreotide group versus placebo. However, the 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the adjusted treatment differences between the two groups were wide and insignificant; (difference in global health status: 4.05, 95% CI (−2.09, 10.20); GI symptoms: −5.68, 95% CI (−9.63, 0.26); endocrine symptoms: −7.04, 95% CI (14.8, 0.73)). Blot et al. [24] conducted principal component analysis to identify independent variable clusters (principal components) from baseline data of the patients in the ELECT trial and found three domains (i.e., the C30 summary score, diarrhea burden, and flushing burden) to be highly correlated with these principal components. The authors concluded that lanreotide-treated patients had a higher probability of being a responder on at least one of the three domains than patients in the placebo group (Table 4).

Ruszniewski et al. examined QOL after 71 NET patients with CS were given lanreotide 6 months and found that the EORTC QLQ C-30 diarrhea subscale score decreased from 66.7 to 33.3; all other measures did not show significant changes from baseline to the 6-month follow-up [27]. A similar result was found in another international observational study; the median diarrhea subscale score was 33.3 at the time of visit for 273 NET patients who received lanreotide for more than three months. Patients who were satisfied with diarrhea control also reported good global QOL scores (Table 4) [26].

Halperin and colleagues conducted two studies through the Neuroendocrine Cancer Awareness Network to assess changes in QOL for patients who had CS [36, 37]. Their study cohort included 117 NET patients with CS (95% of them received SSA therapy in the prior month), mean age of 58 years [37]. The studies used PROMIS-29 and FACT-G with supplemental 29 CS-specific subscales to assess patients' QOL. The first study assessed QOL at time point one (T1: between July 21 and October 31, 2016); the mean FACT-G score was 67.6 +/− 20.0, and the mean score from the supplemental questions was 65.7 +/− 20.4. FACT-G total score was significantly higher for patients taking SSA for more than 8 years than those taking SSA < 2.7 years. The second study collected additional data from time point two (T2: between January and April 2017), which was six months after T1 [36]. Of the study cohort, 89 of 117 patients (mean age 59.2 years old) completed both surveys. Eighty-seven patients were treated with SSAs; of those 11, 37, and 39 received treatment for 0-2 years, 2-5 years, and more than five years, respectively. Only patients who received SSAs for 0-2 years had a positive change in FACT-G total score (3.7 between surveys at T1 and T2 and 6.0 in an additional set of CS-specific questions). All others did not show a clinically meaningful improvement in QOL scores when assessed by FACT-G and PROMIS-29 between T1 and T2 (Table 4).

One crossover study directly compared symptom control between patients with CS who used lanreotide versus octreotide. The study used ISPN to assess patients’ QOL. Mean age of the 33 patients who participated in the study was 63.5 years. No meaningful difference in change of QOL was observed between patients who were treated with octreotide for 30 days followed by lanreotide for 30 days compared to those treated with lanreotide for 30 days followed by octreotide for 30 days (Table 4) [29].

The TELESTAR trial evaluated clinical outcomes of TE for NET patients with CS inadequately controlled by SSAs. A 3 or 4 week screening period established baseline symptom data for the participants. A total of 135 patients were equally and randomly assigned to three treatments: placebo (n=45), 250 mg TE (n=45), and 500 mg TE (n=45). All patients were given oral doses three times per day during the 12 week double-blinded treatment (DBT) period. Patients were also offered treatment with TE 500 mg three times per day for 36-week open-label extension (OLE) after DBT. The average ages of the participants in the trial were 63.3 years (placebo), 62.4 (250 mg), and 64.9 (500 mg).

Three studies evaluated the QOL data from the TELESTAR trial [28, 29, 31]. The first study evaluated changes in the EORTC QLQ-C30 scores over the DBT period. The results showed differences in improvement among treatments in diarrhea subscales: placebo (8.5); 250 mg TE (19.2); 500 mg TE (21.6) on a scale up to 100 with higher scores indicating better symptom control [41]. Cella et al. investigated the relationship between CS symptoms and HR-QOL benefits in patients with CS [29]. The authors separated the patients into two groups, durable responders (DRs) and non-durable responders (NDRs). The DR group was defined as patients having a bowel movement (BM) frequency reduction of >= 30% from baseline for >= 50% of the evaluation period. The study analyzed the differences in changes of EORTC QLQ-C30 between the DRs and NDRs, regardless of inclusion in the randomized treatment groups. At the end of the DBT period, 48 of 135 cases were DRs (n=20, 250 mg; n=19, 500 mg; n= 9, placebo). A significant and greater improvement was seen in CS symptoms and QOL among DRs during the DBT period. DRs also showed more improvement in EORTC QLQ-C30 global health status, diarrhea subscale, and pain. Differences were also seen between DRs and NDRs in nausea, vomiting, and dyspnea as well as gastrointestinal symptoms (QLQ-GINET21 measurement). Hudgens et al. applied an anchor-based approach to derive a meaningful change threshold [31]. This approach used a clinical indicator, i.e., BM frequency, to map PRO outcomes (QOL) to determine the minimal important difference in patient-reported QOL outcomes [42]. In this study, the QLQ-C30 diarrhea subscale at week 12, changes in the QLQ-C30 diarrhea subscale between baseline and week 12, and changes in GINET21 GI symptoms domain score between baseline and week 12 yielded a correlation coefficient with average BM frequency >= 0.30 (the acceptability threshold) [31]. The study found that a meaningful change in BM frequency coincided with an improvement in patient-reported outcomes (QOL) for patients treated with TE (Table 4).

Gelhorn et al. conducted a qualitative study using a one-time phone interview with prior mail survey materials after the TE phase II trial [30]. Ten of 11 patients consented to participate in the study and completed and returned the survey. The average global health and diarrhea subscales were 56.7 and 70, respectively. Compared to the general population, NET patients with CS had worse QOL, with all GI.NET21 scores under 50. The EORTC QLQ-C30 provides equal weight for all 30 questions to calculate the overall scores without increasing weight for CS symptoms (i.e., diarrhea subscales). As such, this study suggested that the overall scores of EORTC QLQ-C30 might not be able to capture significant improvement of CS treatment. Kvols et al. evaluated the safety and efficacy of pasireotide as second-line therapy for patients whose CS was inadequately controlled by octreotide and assessed QOL for these patients [38]. They concluded that patients who responded to pasireotide improved their health-related QOL slightly (Table 4).

Assessment based on Gill and Feinstein checklist for quality of life of carcinoid syndrome

Seven of twelve studies met 80% of Gill and Feinstein's quality criteria (Table 5) [24, 29]. All studies distinguished overall quality of life and health-related quality of life by using the EORTC QLQ-C30 and FACT-G questionnaires, and also used supplemental items (e.g., QLQ GINET21) specifically for CS symptoms. The Gill and Feinstein’s criteria stated that “unless investigators have explicitly identified the underlying quality-of-life construct, readers will be unable to determine whether the choice of domains suitably represented the desired target”. However, we found that the majority of the reviewed studies did not explicitly define the “quality of life” construct in their study.

DISCUSSION

Carcinoid syndrome (CS) is broadly understood to substantially impact NET patient quality of life, and drive up the costs incurred by NET patients seeking treatment for symptom relief. Precise quantification of these impacts and the benefits of the interventions commonly used in clinical practice is essential to our understanding of whether these interventions provide clinically meaningful, cost-effective treatment to alleviate CS symptoms. Such data are particularly important as patients bear an increasing portion of the costs of care. Therefore, we sought a broad, quantitative understanding of the impact of CS on QOL and costs of care.

Due to limited studies focusing on the economic burden of patients with CS, we comprehensively included studies that analyzed (1) treatment costs of NET patients with and without CS; (2) treatment costs of CS patients with and without diarrhea or other CS symptoms; (3) the cost of productivity loss due to CS; and (4) medication costs of first-line or second-line therapies for CS patients. Among the eight studies reporting study perspectives, only one study employed the societal perspective. Seven studies took the perspectives of payers or the healthcare system, although the societal perspective is recommended for economic evaluation [6]. All studies were sponsored by the manufacturers of the studied medications; thus, the estimated cost from the payers’ perspectives may provide useful information for post-market surveillance. This, however, raised concerns whether results from these studies would be biased in favor of the sponsor’s medications [6]. Several studies found that patients with uncontrolled CS symptoms, such as diarrhea or flushing, had higher economic burden (23%-92% higher costs) than those with controlled CS [12, 14, 17, 18]; mostly, ambulatory/outpatient services were the main cost driver [14, 17].

Only 5 of 12 economic studies directly evaluated the intervention costs of CS treatments. Four of these studies evaluated the cost of second-line therapy (i.e., addition of TE to the current standard of care [treatment with SSAs] for patients with uncontrolled CS symptoms) [20-23] and showed that the incremental cost of TE per QALY was high. At least 95% price reduction for TE would be required to achieve an incremental cost utility ratio (ICUR) less than $100,000 per QALY in Canada [23]. TE addition appears to be cost-effective only if the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) threshold is above $150,000 per QALY [22].

The QOL studies utilized data from several trials for first and second-line therapies for patients with CS. Ideally, case-control studies with data from baseline and several follow-up time-points assessing multiple measures of QOL would be instrumental in deciphering more direct effects of the interventions on QOL changes. Of the 12 papers included in this systematic review, only 3 case-control studies assessed QOL at baseline and several time-points. Seven studies had at least two time-point evaluations of QOL but no control group to more accurately estimate the true effect of the interventions on CS patients. The final two studies reported a onetime assessment of quality of life. It is thus unclear whether changes in QOL in these studies were due to direct effects of the interventions on the outcome measures; this ambiguity hinders a comparison of results among the selected studies.

The generic tools and methods for evaluating QOL, such as SF-12, may not work well for NET patients with CS because their specific clinical characteristics may be diluted by inapplicable and unaffected domains. More recently, the combination of EORTC QLQ-C30 and GINET21 became a popular method to assess QOL measurements in CS patients. It is believed that the scales can better serve and evaluate changes in QOL after baseline for the interventions. The tools provide comprehensive modules to evaluate overall QOL, health-related QOL, and symptoms scales specifically to patients with CS. Also, several studies used the concept of “clinically meaningful changes” to interpret QOL outcomes. These studies intended to use CS symptom improvement as a proxy for higher QOL. In two studies, patients were regrouped and analyzed according to strict definitions of treatment “responders” and “non-responders” [29, 31]. The other study used principal component analysis to identify the association between diarrhea and flushing symptoms and the calculated principal components [24]. Weighing and quantifying symptoms, such as the number of bowel movements and flushing, and translating CS symptoms to QOL by validating the association between the symptoms and QOL are recommended.

This study provided a comprehensive review of the economic burden (costs) and patient-reported outcomes (QOL) in NET patients with CS. However, our study provided limited synthesis results of the reviewed studies for the following reasons. First, many of the economic studies had incomplete information on the economic perspective being taken. Second, some studies lacked information on the discount rate and/or reference year of the costs being estimated. Third, study methodology varied across the economic studies. Among the QOL studies, we found variations in the measurement tools, study populations, types of interventions, and the timing of QOL assessment. Lastly, we found variations in the analytical methods used and the interpretation of the results in subgroup analyses.

CONCLUSIONS

We observed heterogeneity among the reviewed studies for evaluating the economic burden and health outcomes in terms of cost and QOL for CS patients. Overall, the number of cost and QOL studies for patients with CS remains sparse, and there is a need to improve both the quantity and quality of CS studies. A consistent trend across all studies is that CS significantly affects QOL and costs of care, necessitating a deeper understanding of the impacts of disease and therapy alike.
