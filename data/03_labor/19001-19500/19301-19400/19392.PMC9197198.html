Abstract

Background:

The presence of esophageal varices is considered a relative contraindication to transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) by cardiology professional societies, so gastroenterologists are often consulted to perform upper endoscopy prior to TEE in patients with cirrhosis.

Aim:

Perform a systematic review to quantify the risk of bleeding complications in patients with cirrhosis following TEE

Methods:

Two reviewers searched MEDLINE and EMBASE databases from January 1992 to May 2021 for studies reporting bleeding complications from TEE in patients with cirrhosis. We calculated the pooled incidence rate of bleeding events using the metaprop command with a random-effects model.

Results:

We identified 21 studies comprising 4050 unique patients with cirrhosis; nine studies (n=3015) assessed risk of intraoperative TEE during liver transplant (LT) and twelve studies (n=1035) assessed bleeding risk in patients undergoing TEE for other indications. The pooled incidence of bleeding post-TEE was 0.37% (95%CI 0.04 – 0.94%) across all studies. Bleeding complications were low among patients undergoing TEE during LT as well as those undergoing TEE for other diagnostic reasons (0.97% vs 0.004%) and among studies with mean MELD >18 compared to those with mean MELD <18 (0.43% vs 0.08%). Few studies had a comparator arm, and data on patient-level factors impacting bleeding complications (including degree of liver dysfunction and coagulopathy) were limited across studies.

Conclusions:

The risk of bleeding complications following TEE is low in patients with cirrhosis, suggesting TEE is safe and risk stratification with upper endoscopy may not be necessary.

Graphical Abstract

INTRODUCTION

Variceal bleeding is a feared complication of portal hypertension and common cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with cirrhosis.1 Gastroesophageal varices can be found in approximately 50% of all patients with cirrhosis, including 30–40% of patients with compensated cirrhosis and 85% of those with decompensated cirrhosis.2 The risk of variceal bleeding depends on several factors including degree of liver dysfunction, size of varices, and presence of red wale signs.2, 3 Professional society guidelines recommend screening endoscopy to assess for gastroesophageal varices every 2–3 years in compensated cirrhosis and annually in those with decompensated cirrhosis.2

While transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is generally considered a low risk procedure with serious complications being rare4, 5, current cardiology professional society guidelines consider the presence of esophageal varices as a relative contraindication to TEE.6, 7 Therefore, gastroenterologists are also often consulted to perform an upper endoscopy to evaluate for the presence and severity of esophageal varices prior to TEE in patients with cirrhosis. However, it is unclear whether performing an upper endoscopy is beneficial—there is no clear gastroenterology or hepatology society guidance on the need for endoscopic assessment of varices prior to TEE. Although there is a theoretical risk of mechanical injury from the TEE probe in patients with cirrhosis and esophageal varices, the magnitude of this risk is unknown – particularly given the safety of doing other procedures such as side-viewing duodenoscopy or endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) without pre-procedure upper endoscopy.8, 9 Furthermore, TEE is used routinely during liver transplant surgery, as it facilitates hemodynamic monitoring and rapid diagnosis of life-threatening conditions that may be otherwise missed.10

Although several studies have evaluated the safety of TEE in patients with cirrhosis and esophageal varices, most are single-center studies and have been limited by small sample size, precluding precise estimates of risk. Therefore, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of available literature to quantify the risk of bleeding complications among patients with cirrhosis undergoing TEE.

METHODS

Literature Search

We searched Ovid MEDLINE, MEDLINE In-Process, and EMBASE databases from January 1992 to May 2021 using search terms, with MeSH terms, as detailed in Supplemental Methods. We restricted our search after 1992 to reflect current intra-procedural TEE processes and management of cirrhosis. We then performed a recursive literature search by reviewing reference lists of included articles for potentially relevant titles that may have been missed by the original search terms. Additionally, a manual review of 2017–2020 meeting abstracts from several professional organizations was conducted including the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) The Liver Meeting, American Gastroenterology Association Digestive Disease Week, and American Congress of Gastroenterology Annual Scientific Meeting. The study was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.11

Study Selection

After duplicates were removed, two authors (M.O. and A.S.) independently reviewed all articles identified by the literature search. Each article was screened in an iterative, multi-step process based on title and abstract for relevance to the study, and full texts were searched to assess for study inclusion (Figure 1). Any disagreements between authors were resolved by consensus and discussion with a third author (N.E.R.). Studies were included in the analysis if they met the following criteria: 1) included adults ≥18 years of age and 2) reported outcomes of patients with cirrhosis undergoing TEE. Studies were excluded if they lacked original data (e.g., reviews, editorials), were limited to case reports, were non-English or non-human studies, or were not on the topic of outcomes after TEE in patients with cirrhosis. In the case of studies reporting data from overlapping cohorts, we selected the most comprehensive cohort for inclusion in the study.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Three authors (M.O., A.S., N.E.R.) independently extracted data from included studies using standardized forms assessing: study design, study years, indication for TEE (intraoperatively during liver transplant vs. diagnostic indication), characteristics of the cohort (including sex, liver disease etiology, presence of cirrhosis, mean Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score, and mean platelet count), number of bleeding events, upper endoscopy findings (i.e., presence of any or large varices), and need for subsequent endoscopic procedures or any interventions. Varices were classified as large if grade II or grade III, consistent with Baveno and AASLD guidelines.12 We assessed the risk of bias for each included study using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies, which assesses selection of the study cohort, adequacy of exposure assessment, and ascertainment of the outcome of interest.13 We specifically addressed the following: 1) patient selection, 2) exposure measures and assessment; 3) methods for outcome ascertainment (i.e., bleeding events), and 4) length of follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

For each individual study, we recorded the absolute number of bleeding events post-TEE among the total number of patients with cirrhosis. We calculated point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the incidence of bleeding complications using the adjusted Wald method.14 We performed Chi-square test to examine heterogeneity between subgroups. Using the metaprop command as a random effects model15, we calculated pooled incidence rates for bleeding for 1) the overall population, 2) the subset of studies in which TEE was performed intraoperatively during liver transplantation, and 3) studies in which TEE was performed for other non-transplant indications.16 We evaluated heterogeneity both by visual examination of forest plots and statistically using the inconsistency index (I2 statistic). The I2 statistic was interpreted as follows: >75% indicates high heterogeneity, 50–75% moderate heterogeneity, and <50% mild heterogeneity. We assessed the potential for publication bias using visual inspection of funnel plots and the Egger’s test.17 All analyses were performed using Stata version 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Literature Search

Our search strategy resulted in 2702 potentially relevant citations; After removing 375 duplicates there were 2327 unique citations (Figure 1). After initial review, 282 titles potentially met inclusion criteria and abstracts reviewed, with 222 studies excluded based on the abstract. Finally, 60 full texts were carefully assessed for eligibility. Of these, 40 were excluded and 20 met inclusion criteria. Fifteen studies on the topic of TEE in patients with cirrhosis were excluded due to having insufficient data on the incidence of bleeding following the procedure, and 1 study was excluded due an overlapping cohort.18 Recursive literature searches and review of meeting abstracts yielded one additional citation for a total of 21 articles. Agreement between the two reviewers for study inclusion was >90%. Visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested asymmetry with small studies of large effect sizes potentially missing (Supplemental Figure 1); however, we found no evidence of publication bias by Egger’s test (p=0.09).

Study Characteristics

We identified 21 studies comprising 4050 patients evaluating outcomes of TEE in patients with cirrhosis19–40; TEE was performed intraoperatively prior to liver transplantation in nine studies (n=3015) and for other indications (e.g., cardiopulmonary evaluation or rule out infective endocarditis) in twelve studies (n=1035). Clinical characteristics of included studies are reported in Table 1. In brief, 19 of 21 studies were retrospective in design, 17 of 21 were performed in the United States and most were published after 2015. Upper endoscopy was performed at some time prior to TEE in 16 studies but was performed immediately prior to TEE with the intention of ruling out varices in only one of the studies.27

Incidence of Bleeding Events Following TEE

The pooled incidence rate of bleeding complications was 0.37% (95%CI 0.04% – 0.94%; I2 = 56.8%) across all studies. In subgroup analyses, we found the pooled incidence of bleeding events in which TEE was performed intraoperatively during liver transplantation was 0.97% (95% CI 0.23% – 2.05%; I2 = 75.3%), which was significantly higher (p<0.01) than incidence when TEE was performed for other indications (0.004%, 95%CI 0.0% – 0.34%; I2 = 8.2%) (Figure 2). Similarly, the pooled incidence of bleeding was 0.43% (95%CI 0.0% – 1.75%) among studies with mean MELD >18 compared to 0.08% (95%CI 0.0%– 1.54%) among those with a mean MELD <18, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.43). Among the subset of studies in which EGD was performed at some point prior to TEE (n=16), the pooled incidence of bleeding was 0.20% (95%CI 0.0% – 0.90%), which did not significantly differ from 1.11% (95%CI 0.20% – 2.44%) incidence among studies in which EGD was not performed prior to TEE (p=0.63).

The presence of large (i.e., grade II or III) varices was rare among studies with reported data (n=17); 62.7% had known esophageal varices,17.1% of which had large varices. Similarly, the risk of incidental bleeding and need for subsequent endoscopic procedures was rare. Among studies assessing intraoperative TEE during liver transplant, 35 bleeding events were reported (though 14 of these were ultimately in patients determined to have minor oropharyngeal bleeding) with only 7 requiring follow-up esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and 5 requiring therapeutic intervention (2 requiring Blakemore placement, 3 requiring banding). Among the studies assessing TEE for other indications, only 6 patients had bleeding events, with only 2 requiring follow-up EGD, neither of which required therapeutic intervention (Table 2).

Nine studies reported data on a comparator group (e.g., bleeding events among patients with cirrhosis who did not undergo TEE) or reported stratified results based on known risk factors for variceal bleeding (e.g., up to date with variceal screening vs. not). In a study of liver transplant recipients of whom 66% received TEE, Myo Bui et al found that alcohol-related cirrhosis was associated with increased risk of major bleeding complications, whereas receipt of TEE at time of transplant was not.28 Markin et al found no significant association between prior history of GI bleeding requiring intervention and new bleeding during intraoperative TEE among patients undergoing liver transplant.31 Pai et al demonstrated that only 1 of 232 patients who underwent intraoperative TEE during transplant had variceal bleeding, compared to 1 episode among the 1579 patients that did not have a TEE probe placed.24 In single-center studies, Liu et al and Chandra et al both found no significant difference in bleeding rates between patients who were up-to-date on variceal screening prior to TEE compared to those who were not.21 Finally, Burger-Klepp et al observed no difference in transfusion requirements between patients with and without history of varices who had intraoperative TEE during liver transplant.33

Quality Assessment

The quality assessment for included studies is outlined in Supplemental Table 1. Overall, ten studies were regarded as having high risk of bias and the remaining were at moderate risk of bias; no studies had low risk of bias. The cohorts were well defined and representative, including patients with various liver disease etiologies and degrees of liver dysfunction, and almost all studies had an adequate assessment of the study outcome, as post-procedure bleeding is typically readily apparent; however, many studies combined clinically significant bleeding with mild bleeding that did not require intervention. Most studies failed to adequately adjust for confounders that could affect risk of bleeding. While most studies provided descriptive data on cirrhosis severity (e.g., MELD score, Child Turcotte Pugh class) and parameters of coagulopathy (e.g., platelet count, INR, fibrinogen), only one study performed multivariable analyses of risk of bleeding adjusting for these factors.39 While some studies reported prior endoscopic findings, prior endoscopic intervention, and/or history of variceal bleeding, only three studies reported the proportion of patients taking non-selective beta-blockers.22, 39, 41 The use of medications that may increase the risk of bleeding was rarely reported; Sack et al was the only study to report the proportion of patients taking anticoagulants and/or antiplatelets prior to TEE. While several studies specified the indications for TEE (e.g., rule out infective endocarditis), no studies compared or reported the risk of bleeding by TEE indication. Most studies assessing bleeding risk of TEE performed for “other indications” reported endoscopic findings, whereas these data were missing from most studies assessing risk of bleeding after intraoperative TEE performed during liver transplantation. Chandra et al was the only study to assess whether patients were up-to-date on variceal screening per AASLD guidelines42, and only one study specifically excluded patients with history of varices that were banded within 7 days prior to TEE.27 All but one of the studies39 failed to include a justification for sample size or report variance in outcomes, and all but nine studies failed to report loss to follow-up.

DISCUSSION

We conducted a comprehensive systematic review of the literature assessing risk of bleeding in patients with cirrhosis undergoing TEE. We found the risk of bleeding complications following TEE in patients with cirrhosis to be low, at <1%. Bleeding events appeared to be minor with only a small proportion requiring subsequent therapeutic intervention for the bleeding. This risk is similar to the risk of major bleeding observed in other populations undergoing diagnostic TEE, estimated to be as high as 0.8%5, with higher bleeding rates observed in patients undergoing interventional cardiology procedures.43 Risk of bleeding was low across subgroups including those with more severe liver disease (e.g., higher MELD, patients undergoing liver transplant). These data suggest the risk of bleeding with TEE is sufficiently low in patients with cirrhosis that pre-operative EGD risk stratification may not be necessary, especially if appropriate esophageal variceal screening/surveillance has already been performed.

There continues to be debate surrounding whether the presence of portal hypertension and varices are a relative or absolute contraindication to TEE. According to current American Society of Echocardiography guidelines, esophageal varices are considered a relative contraindication for TEE,44 whereas American Society of Anesthesiology guidelines cite equivocal opinions about the absolute or relative contraindication of esophageal varices to TEE due to insufficient evidence.45 Although they suggest possible gastroenterology consult and EGD prior to TEE in patients that may be considered high risk, they do not explicitly clarify how patients should be risk stratified. Moreover, there are no specific gastroenterology or hepatology guidelines on whether endoscopic evaluation prior to TEE should be performed. Discrepancies in guidelines likely stem from a lack of large, prospective studies investigating bleeding complications associated with TEE. These inconsistencies subsequently contribute to provider hesitancy about performing TEE blindly in patients with cirrhosis.

This study provides more evidence suggesting the safety of TEE in patients with cirrhosis. These findings are perhaps not surprising given the typical mechanism of variceal bleeding in patients with cirrhosis is related to increased portal pressures, rather than mechanical trauma.46 This is supported by the observed safety of performing similar procedures such as ERCP for biliary drainage or stone extraction in patients with varices9 and passage of nasogastric tubes and endoscopic ultrasound probes without preceding EGD to risk stratify patients.47, 48 Furthermore, while there was initial hesitancy to perform TEE during liver transplant due to the risk of variceal rupture, more recent studies have demonstrated that risk of hemorrhage remains quite low, even in this high-risk population with severe liver disease and coagulopathy. Rather, TEE has been shown to be beneficial in the intraoperative hemodynamic monitoring and management of liver transplant patients.29, 49The rarity of bleeding complications with TEE has also been demonstrated in other high bleeding risk groups, such as cancer patients with thrombocytopenia with no major bleeding events reported after TEE. 50

The rarity of bleeding complications with TEE also raises the question of whether routine EGD prior to TEE is clinically beneficial. While an EGD for risk stratification prior to TEE may appear to be relatively benign, it carries its own risks (e.g., those related to anesthesia, aspiration) as well as increased costs.51 Furthermore, awaiting GI consultation prior to performing TEE may lead to delays in patient care and prolonged hospitalizations. While this may be acceptable in patients requiring elective TEE, this may have significant consequences for patients requiring urgent TEE. The risk of bleeding may be increased if banding is performed prior to TEE given risk of dislodging one of the bands. Our findings suggest that such guidelines could be reframed considering the knowledge that bleeding rates in patients with portal hypertension and known varices (including patients with the most severe liver disease such as those undergoing liver transplantation) are exceedingly rare. However, given so few studies investigated the question of whether EGD prior to TEE mitigates the risk of bleeding, we cannot make a firm recommendation on the benefit of EGD prior to TEE in patients with cirrhosis. In this study, incidence rates of bleeding remained low among patients with more advanced liver disease and irrespective of indication for TEE. However, other variables including high-risk medication exposures (e.g., anticoagulants) and duration of TEE procedure (e.g., for an interventional cardiology procedure) could have affected bleeding outcomes. An individualized approach should be taken that considers multiple patient-specific factors including other comorbidities, medication exposures, anticipated duration of TEE exposure, and whether appropriate endoscopic screening/surveillance has occurred in accordance with AASLD guidelines. Taking these factors into account, EGD prior to TEE may be beneficial in only a few instances, especially considering the low risk of bleeding after TEE in this population, and routine EGD prior to TEE is unlikely to be cost-effective.

Our study has a few limitations, including those inherent to meta-analyses, which are themselves limited by the quality of the included studies. First, we acknowledge the potential for selection bias as most studies were retrospective in nature, single center, comprised of small sample sizes, and lacked a control group. Additionally, the fact that some patients deemed “low risk” may have not undergone upper endoscopy prior to TEE, whereas some patients deemed “higher risk” may have been considered too sick to undergo TEE and were therefore excluded in the included studies may introduce additional selection bias. Further, patients that received EGD prior to TEE for risk stratification and were found to have large varices may have not received TEE and were therefore excluded. Second, data on patient-level factors that impact bleeding risk (e.g. MELD score, parameters of coagulopathy, medication exposures) were inconsistently reported and limited across all studies. Despite these limitations, the pooled risk of bleeding events remained quite low even among patients undergoing TEE during liver transplantation, which represents a population with severe liver disease that is presumably at high-risk for portal hypertensive bleeding events.

In conclusion, the incidence of bleeding complications in patients with cirrhosis undergoing TEE is low, at less than 1%, even in patients with severe liver disease undergoing liver transplantation. Therefore, available data suggests that performing a TEE is safe in patients with cirrhosis and risk stratification with EGD may be unnecessary.
