A systematic review of retractions in the field of cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia

Lisa Q. Rong[1]

Katia Audisio[2]

Mohamed Rahouma[2]

Giovanni J. Soletti[2]

Gianmarco Cancelli[2]

Mario Gaudino[2]

[1] Department of Anesthesiology, Weill Cornell Medicine, New York, New York, USA

[2] Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, Weill Cornell Medicine, New York, New York, USA

Abstract

Objectives:

No systematic studies on retractions in cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia exist. The aim of this analysis is to identify characteristics and trends of retractions in this field over the past three decades.

Design:

A search of the Retraction Watch database for retracted articles published between 1990 and 2020 in the field of cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia was performed.

Setting:

Bibliometric Study

Participants:

5,344 retractions with the term “medicine” in the subject code were selected. Retractions of full-length English articles reporting finding in cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia were included.

Interventions:

None

Measurements and Main Results:

A total of 63 articles published in 31 journals from January 1990 to August 2020 were retracted. The majority were original articles (n=60, 95.2%) and retracted for scientific misconduct (n=50, 79.4%). The percentage of retractions due to misconduct increased from 2010, with a spike in 2011 (n=26/50, 52.0%), and reached a plateau in 2014. The three most common reasons for retraction were misconduct by author (n=31, 49.2%), duplication (n=12, 19.0%), and errors within the manuscript (n=11, 17.5%). The median time from publication to retraction was 4.3 years (IQR:1.7–9.4) and decreased significantly over time (P<0.001). The median impact factor of the journals that published retracted articles was 3.5 (IQR 2.0–4.5) and decreased significantly over the study period (P<0.001).

Conclusions:

Scientific misconduct represents the most common reason for retraction in cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia. The median time to retraction and journal impact factor decreased significantly over time. While this is promising, future efforts should be made to screen for falsified data and standardize the processes after retraction to highlight problematic manuscripts.

Introduction

Original investigation through research is the cornerstone of evidence-based which drives standards and clinical practice. Articles that have undergone the peer-review process and are published in reputable journals are assumed to be reliable, which is why when that in not the case, retractions are important to preserve the integrity of the scientific literature. The Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) presented recommendations to editors on how to deal with unreliable study findings due to research misconduct, errors, ethical concerns, or plagiarism that are either intentional or non-intentional.(1) While the total number of retractions have been relatively low over the last 30 years,(2) several of the biggest retractions scandals in science have included anesthesiologists: Drs. Scott Reuben, Ueshima Hironobu, Joachim Boldt, and Yoshitaka Fujii are responsible for hundreds of articles published that were then retracted mainly for scientific misconduct.(3–5) To date, no systematic studies on retractions in cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia exist. The aim of this analysis is to examine reasons for retraction and rates of article retraction in the field of cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesiology over the last three decades.

Methods

For the purpose of this analysis, ethical approval was waived by the local Institutional Review Board (Weill Cornell Medicine, New York, USA). Data collection form and analytic plan were specified a priori, but not published.

Data sources and inclusion criteria

A search of the largest database of retracted articles, the Retraction Watch database (founded in August 2020),(6) was performed to quantify the number of retracted publications in cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia. More details, including how retraction records are collected and entered in the database, can be found at the following link: https://retractionwatch.com/retraction-watch-database-user-guide/.

After signing a data use agreement, the database was queried on August 12, 2020. We searched for all articles with the term “medicine” in the subject code. Retracted entries totaling 5,344 were then screened by two authors (K.A. and G.J.S.) for all articles relating to “anesthesiology” and “anesthesia” and “perioperative” in title, abstract, and journal. Discrepancies were resolved by a third author (M.G.). After manual review of the title, abstract, and manuscripts of these 450 articles by a cardiothoracic anesthesiologist (L.R.), only full-length articles written in English and reporting results in the field of cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia were included. This was defined by the patient population undergoing cardiac, thoracic, or vascular surgery where the intervention or observation took place in the intraoperative period. We excluded the following article types: individual or committee opinions, conference abstracts, and practice bulletins.

Data extraction

Two authors (K.A. and G.J.S.) independently extracted data. Any disagreement was resolved by a third author (M.G.). For each retracted manuscript, the following data were extracted: article type, number of authors, first and last author institutional affiliation, name and 2019 impact factor (IF) of the publishing journal, year of publication, year of retraction, country of origin, open access or paywall-protected retraction notice, reasons for retraction, and number of citations. The IF and citations number were extracted from Web of Science and Google Scholar.

According to previous studies,(2,7–9) scientific misconduct was classified as one of the following eight reasons : plagiarism, duplication, falsification or fabrication of data, images or results, ethical violation, conflict of interest, authorship issues, missing informed consent, and compromised peer review. A complete list of the reasons for retraction with their corresponding definitions are available in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

According to the Shapiro-Wilkinson normality test, continuous variables were not normally distributed. Thus they were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using Kruskal-Wallis or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using a binomial test. Visual assessment of plots summarizing the number of retractions by year demonstrated a non-linear relationship. As such, segmented piece-wise regression was employed to estimate whether a change-point existed. The dependent variable was plotted against publication year from 1990 to 2020 and a linear regression model was first fitted to assess changes in the yearly trend.

A bootstrap resampling algorithm was then used to empirically estimate whether a change-point existed in the fitted regression model by examining for a change in slope parameter via an iterative process. The algorithm first fixes the change-point based on a starting value and calculates the slope parameter of the line segment before the change point and the slope parameter of the line after the change point; the model is then fitted with these slopes. A coefficient that measures parameterization of the breakpoint as the difference between the gap of two fitted straight lines was calculated at each iteration of the model. The algorithm converges when the estimated difference between the two fitted straight lines is approximately null (or statistically 0), implying that the gap between the two straight lines is zero and the lines intersect.

The Spearman test was used to assess correlations that were presented as correlation plots with the corresponding correlation coefficient (r). All analyses were performed using R version 3.6.2 within R studio with “tableone” and “segmented” packages. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant without multiplicity adjustment. Sensitivity analyses were performed on retracted articles after excluding first and last author manuscripts by Dr. Boldt.

Results

General characteristics

The analysis included 63 articles published from January 1990 to January 2020 and retracted from August 1999 to June 2020 (Supplementary Figure 1). The year with the highest number of retractions was 2011 (n=30, 47.6%); this remained true after excluding retractions due to Dr. Boldt (n=17, 38.6%). Details on retractions by year are available in Table 4. The median time from publication to retraction was 4.3 years (IQR [Interquartile Range]: 1.7–9.4, range 0.0–30.0 years) and decreased significantly over time (P <0.001) (Figure 1). Details of the time from publication to retraction are given in Supplementary Figure 2A.

Original articles (n=60, 95.2%) represented the majority of retracted articles, followed by case reports (n=2, 3.2%), and meta-analysis or review articles (n=1, 1.6%) (Table 2).

Nine countries from 4 continents accounted for all the retractions. The continent with the highest number of retractions was Europe (n=39, 61.9%), followed by Asia (n=12, 19.0%), and North America (n=10, 15.9%) (Table 2). Germany was the country with the highest number of retracted articles (n=36, 57.1%) and the highest number of misconducts (n=33/36, 91.7%), followed by United States (n=9, 14.3%), and Japan (n=4, 6.3%) (Figure 2). Excluding Boldt retractions, Germany remained the country with the highest number of retractions with United States as the second highest, as well as when considering retractions due to scientific misconduct. As a denominator, the overall number of published studies during this time period from 1990–2020 was estimated to be 582,065 (Supplementary Table 1).

Reasons for retraction

The most common reason for retraction was scientific misconduct (n=50, 79.4%). Misconduct by author (n=31, 49.2%), duplication (n=12, 19.0%), and falsification or fabrication of manuscript content (n=10, 15.9%) represented the 3 most common causes of retractions misconduct-related (Table 3). After excluding Boldt, plagiarism (n=7, 15.9%) became the third most common reason for retraction, while the first two reasons remained the same. No misconduct was recorded prior to 2010. The percentage of retractions due to misconduct increased from 2010, spiked in 2011 (n=26/50, 52.0%), and reached a plateau in 2014 (Figure 3).

Among articles retracted for non-misconduct reasons, the most frequent reasons for retraction were errors within the manuscript (n=11, 17.

Retraction by author and journal

Among the 31 journals containing retracted articles, Journal of Cardiothoracic and Vascular Anesthesia (n=10, 15.9%), Anesthesia & Analgesia (n=9, 14.3%), and British Journal of Anaesthesia (n=5, 7.9%), were those with the highest number of retractions (Table 5). This remained true after excluding retractions by Boldt. While 30.1% of the journals had only one retraction, these 3 journals accounted for 38.1% of total retractions.

The median IF of journals publishing retracted articles was 3.5 (IQR: 2.0–4.5) and decreased significantly over the study period (P<0.001) (Figure 4). The journal with the highest IF (17.1) was Intensive Care Medicine with 3 (4.8%) retractions. Journals with an IF between 0 and 5 had the greatest number of retracted articles (n=49, 77.8%) while retractions from journals with an IF higher than 10 represented a minority (n=3, 4.8%) (Table 5).

A total of 2 (3.2%) retraction notices were restricted behind a paywall. The use of a paywall was not associated with time from publication to retraction (P=0.52).

The median number of citations of retracted articles was 15.0 (IQR: 5.0–38.0). The majority of retracted articles were cited between 0 to 20 times (n=36, 57.1%), 9 (14.3%) retractions received between 50 to 100 citations each, and 2 (3.2%) were cited more than 100 times each (Supplementary Figure 2B).

The median number of authors per retracted article was 5.00 (IQR: 5.00–6.00). One author from Germany, Dr. Joachim Boldt, was associated with a significant number of retracted articles (n=19, 30.1%). Of these, 13 (68.4%) were from 2011 and 17 (89.4%) were due to misconduct. The authors with the next highest number of retracted articles are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The median number of retractions per authors was 1.00 (interquartile range: IQR 1.00, 1.00) and the mean number was 1.66 (standard deviation: SD 2.34).

Discussion

In this comprehensive study of cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia literature over the last 30 years, we found that only 63 articles were retracted, the majority of which were due to misconduct (79.4%, 50 of 63). Our results are the first to evaluate retractions over time in this field up until 2020; we found that the number of retractions plateaued and the time from publication to retraction as well as the journal impact factor decreased over time. Dr. Joachim Boldt was associated with a significant number of retracted articles (30.1%, 19 of 63), and of these, 13 (68.4%) were from 2011 and 17 (89.4%) were due to misconduct. After performing a sensitivity analyses on the remaining retracted studies after excluding for Boldt, many of the characteristics remained the same. The one main difference was that plagiarism replaced falsification/fabrication of manuscript content as the third most common reason for retractions due to scientific misconduct, after author misconduct and duplication. Notably, the first retraction due to misconduct in anesthesiology was in 2010.

Two earlier studies of retractions from 1980s to early 2000s reported that they were mostly due to honest error/non-misconduct (40% vs 62%), and less to research misconduct (28% vs 27%).(10,11) However more recent analyses in clinical fields demonstrate a reversal in this trend. In a recent review of 187 retracted general surgical articles, the top three reasons cited for retraction were duplication (65, 35.3%), data falsification (27, 14.7%) and IRB violations (34, 18%), suggesting that over 60% of retractions were due to scientific misconduct, according to our definitions (Supplementary Table 1). A recent meta-analysis in the obstetrical literature found that retraction of scientific articles is increasing over time and were most often due to scientific misconduct, including the top two reasons of article duplication (26, 21.3%) and plagiarism (23, 18.9%), and found a significant number of retractions lacked a watermark or retraction notice (19,15.6%).(12) Recent analyses in other surgical fields of orthopedic surgery and neurosurgery demonstrate increased number of retractions with time, and found similarly that retractions are most commonly due to misconduct.(13,14) Finally, a recent systematic review in emergency medicine found that the most frequent reason for retraction was misconduct through plagiarism, but that the median time from publication to retraction was less than 1 year.(15)

Nair et al, in a comprehensive review of anesthesiology retractions overall, found a similar rate of scientific misconduct (76%, 266 of 350) and similar median journal impact factor (3.6 [IQR: 2.5–4.0] vs 3.5 [IQR: 2.0–4.5]), but a longer median time from publication to retraction (8 years [IQR: 3–14] vs 4.3 years [IQR: 1.7–9.4]).(16) This paper included both clinical and basic research retractions, while our is focused specifically in cardiovascular and thoracic papers that were clinically relevant.

Our analysis cannot shed any light on how good the peer-review process is at discovering erroneous or false data; one could argue that it may be excellent as there are few retractions over the total number of manuscripts published. However, the number of erroneous or flawed manuscripts that were rejected and never got to be published is unknown. Also, there could be an unknown number of manuscripts that meet criteria for retraction but have not been discovered yet.

Optimistically, the system could be very good, but even so, the road to timely retraction is often by chance. For the case of Dr. Joachim Boldt, several readers of Anesthesia & Analgesia brought their suspicions of Dr. Boldt’s data to then Editor-in-Chief Dr. Shafer shortly after publication. He attempted for several months to get a response from Boldt and finally brought the issue to Landesärztekammer Rheinland-Pfalz (LÄK-RLP), the State Medical Association of Rheinland-Pfalz, Germany. Their investigation found that Dr. Boldt’s studies lacked approval by the ethics committee, written informed consent, or prospective randomization.(17) This ultimately led to a joint statement of Editors-In-Chiefs from 18 major anesthesiology and surgery journals retracting 88 articles by Dr. Boldt, leading to the spike in retractions in 2011.(18) Dr. Boldt’s papers continue to be retracted to this day; in September 2020, the British Journal of Anaesthesia retracted more than 20 papers after continued investigation by Klinikum Ludwigshafen: the institution where Boldt worked in his early career did not find evidence that the research had been conducted as published.(19) It is important to note that without his involvement, the number of overall retractions would be lower; however, upon sensitivity analysis excluding retracted manuscripts where he was first or last author, many of the characteristics of the retractions remained the same.

The majority of retractions we studied were investigated by the authors company and institution (n=17, 38.6%) or the journal or publisher of the manuscript (n=9, 20.5%), while the minority were investigated by a third party (n=3, 6.8%). Undoubtedly, journal editors and the peer-review process have a major role in judging research rigor and authenticity. Over the last several years, journal editors have screened manuscripts for plagiarism before sending them for external peer review. They have been aided by technology and the development of specific plagiarism software that list the percentage similarity to other. The need for a parallel process could be made for scientific conduct, where technology and dedicated software could detect anomalous or suspicious data earlier, and require audit and careful screening before publication and spread of incorrect results. While dedicated websites such as Retraction Watch and Pub Peer assist in policing scientific misconduct and disseminating the information to the public, standardized processes at the Editorial and peer review levels may necessary. This also speaks to the importance of institutions with their internal regulatory boards for research in investigating fraud and scientific misconduct. However, it unknown beyond individual cases such as Dr. Bolts’ how retractions were first identified. Collecting this information in the future may help develop screening criteria for falsified manuscripts.

Similarly, there is no standard process today on how to disseminate a retraction. A recent article found that 15% (43 of 289) of retraction notices were not compliant with COPE guidelines and retraction watermarks were not present on 47 out of 278 (17%) retracted papers that were still available for viewing.(3) This may be the reason why some retracted articles continue to be cited. To maximize the impact of the retractions, journals and editors should consider strategies beyond watermarking such as broad dissemination among the internet, social media, and journal website to publicize and correct misinformation.

Our study has certain limitations. We used the Retraction Watch database, the most comprehensive database for retractions, and did not manually search retracted articles in the literature. Searching other databases may have captured additional articles not included in our study. We also only included those retracted in the subspecialty of cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia so this may not be broadly applicable to other fields and subspecialties. Another limitation is that we reported multiple reasons for a retraction which led to double-counting of a manuscript due to multiple retraction causes. We also reported results as percentages with the denominator being the number overall retractions, not the total articles published. In addition, we categorized retractions due to scientific misconduct according to previous studies, but these may not have been standardized across all studies. One author, Dr. Boldt was associated with a significant number of retractions, but a sensitivity analyses was performed. Finally, since we interrogated the database in August 2020, we did not include articles that were published during this time period but were not yet retracted due to the time lag to retraction.

In conclusion, we found that retractions in cardiothoracic and vascular anesthesia were due mainly to scientific misconduct. However, they have plateaued in recent years and the latency time from publication to retraction, as well as the journal impact factor, has decreased significantly over the last 30 years. While this is promising, future efforts should be made to screen for falsified data and standardize the processes after retraction to highlight problematic manuscripts.
