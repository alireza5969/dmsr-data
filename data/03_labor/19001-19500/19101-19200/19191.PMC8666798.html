Dropout from Face-to-Face, Multi-Session Psychological Treatments for Problem and Disordered Gambling: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Abstract

Objective:

The aims of this systematic review and meta-analysis were to examine the overall prevalence of dropout from psychological treatments for problem gambling and gambling disorder and to examine how study, client, and treatment variables influenced dropout rates.

Method:

A systematic search was conducted to identify studies of cognitive and/or behavioral therapies and motivational interventions for problem gambling and gambling disorder. Meta-analysis was used to calculate an overall weighted dropout rate. Random effect meta-regressions were used to examine covariates of dropout rates. Mixed effect subgroup analyses were used to examine moderators of dropout rates.

Results:

The systematic search identified 24 studies (31 dropout rates) comprising 2,791 participants. Using a random effects model, the overall weighted dropout rate was 39.1%, 95% CI [33.0%, 45.6%]. Increases in the percentage of married participants were significantly associated with lower dropout rates. Dropout rates were significantly higher when dropout was defined as attending all sessions of a treatment protocol compared to when defined as attending a prespecified number of sessions different from the total in the protocol and when defined as study therapists judging participants to be dropouts. Insufficient reporting of some gambling-related variables and other psychological symptom variables prevented a thorough examination of covariates and moderators.

Conclusions:

A large proportion of individuals drop out of treatment for problem gambling and gambling disorder. Future research should examine the reasons for dropout across marital statuses and should adopt dropout definitions that consider session-by-session symptom change.

Problem gambling and gambling disorder represent major public health problems across the world (Gainsbury et al., 2014; Petry & Blanco, 2013; Wardle et al., 2019). Across the world, between 0.1% to 0.7% experience clinically significant problems with gambling (Petry et al., 2018). These gambling-related difficulties involve persistent and recurrent betting that results in financial, psychological, relational, and vocational difficulties (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) and warrant the need for efficacious treatment (Petry & Blanco, 2013; Potenza et al., 2019).

To date, cognitive and/or behavioral therapies and motivational interventions have received the most empirical support for the treatment of problem gambling and gambling disorder (Cowlishaw et al., 2012; Petry et al., 2017; Yakovenko et al., 2015). Randomized controlled trials have determined those treatments yield significantly superior outcomes to no treatment controls at posttreatment (e.g., Grant et al., 2009; Petry et al., 2006; Petry et al., 2016). Greater doses (i.e., more sessions) of cognitive and/or behavioral therapies and motivational interventions have been associated with better outcomes (Pfund et al., 2020).

Arguably, the most significant problem with these face-to-face treatments for problem gambling and gambling disorder is that a sizable proportion of participants discontinue treatment prematurely (Melville et al., 2007), which likely limits therapeutic benefits (Pfund et al., 2020). Dropout, which is often defined as occurring when an individual discontinues treatment before its completion, mounts at rates as high as 50% (Melville et al., 2007; Pfund et al., 2018). This rate is particularly troubling because it is likely that individuals who drop out do not receive the full benefits of treatment that would have otherwise been achieved had they continued attending (Pfund et al., 2020; Swift et al., 2009).

Several factors may influence dropout risk, including client (e.g., demographics, gambling disorder symptom severity) and treatment (e.g., treatment satisfaction) characteristics. For example, in substance use disorder treatment, studies with higher proportions of individuals identifying as African American have increased dropout rates (Lappan et al., 2020). These findings may reflect the lack of culturally relevant treatment components (Resnicow et al., 2000). Other studies on substance use disorder treatment have indicated treatment dissatisfaction may influence dropout risk, which may be directly due to poor alliance with a therapist (Meier et al., 2006). However, the last review on dropout from psychological treatments for problem gambling and gambling disorder was unable to converge on a set of factors associated with dropout risk (Melville et al., 2007).

The last review may have been unable to identify a set of dropout risk factors for at least two reasons. First, the review was limited in its methodological approach because it was primarily narrative rather than quantitative. The review attempted to identify dropout risk factors by counting the number of studies reporting factors significantly associated with dropout. By today’s standards, this approach is considered suboptimal because advances in meta-analysis now allow weights to be assigned to studies and the use of moderator analyses (e.g., meta-regression). Weights are essential for estimating the occurrence of dropout and identifying its risk factors because they provide an estimate of each study’s precision (e.g., variance, standard error, confidence intervals). As a substitute for moderator analyses, the prior narrative review counted the number of studies reporting factors significantly associated with dropout. This approach is problematic because it assumes each study is equally precise, overrepresenting the effect of risk factors on dropout from studies with small sample sizes and underrepresenting the effect of risk factors on dropout from studies with large sample sizes. The use of modern meta-analytic techniques would, therefore, constitute a significant improvement upon the precision of calculating the overall weighted dropout rate and moderator analyses. Second, the review may have been confounded by variation in study authors’ dropout definitions. Dropout has been defined in a variety of ways across gambling treatment studies, including the failure to attend all prescribed sessions of a treatment protocol (i.e., “treatment completion,” Petry et al. 2016), the failure to attend a prespecified number of sessions (different from the prescribed sessions of a protocol) (i.e., “sessions,” Ladouceur et al., 2001; Ladouceur et al., 2003), the subjective judgment of dropouts as determined by therapists/researchers (i.e., “therapist judgment,” Smith et al., 2015), and the failure to achieve statistically significant reductions in symptoms prior to discontinuing treatment (i.e., “symptoms,” Pfund et al., 2018). Unfortunately, the authors of the last review only suggested the possibility that dropout rates significantly varied based on disparate dropout definitions and did not empirically test it.

A recent study aimed to empirically test whether disparate dropout definitions resulted in significantly different dropout rates. In a time-unlimited, in-person treatment, Pfund and colleagues (2018) examined potential differences in dropout rates between the sessions definition and the symptoms definition. They found that the sessions definition resulted in a significantly lower dropout rate (40%) than the symptoms definition (50%). This study was important because it empirically demonstrated, for the first time in treatments for problem gambling and gambling disorder, that dropout rates could vary based on the definition used. However, this study was limited in that it was a highly unique sample (i.e., mostly female treatment-seeking gamblers from an outpatient private practice in the United States) and that the symptoms definition has never been used in gambling treatment studies (the symptoms definition was a novel definition created by the authors to test when dropout occurred during treatment). Thus, it is not known whether there are significant differences in dropout rates among other samples, and the three most commonly used dropout definitions in gambling treatment studies: treatment completion, sessions, and therapist judgment. Such knowledge could help accurately gauge the prevalence of dropout in treatments for problem gambling and gambling disorder and determine whether certain definitions over- or under-estimate compared to other definitions.

The present study sought to address the limitations of two past studies on dropout from psychological treatment for problem gambling and gambling disorder. To address the limitations of the last review on dropout (Melville et al., 2007), we employed meta-analytic methods to adequately quantify the prevalence of dropout and to adequately examine moderators of dropout. To address the limitations of the study by Pfund and colleagues (2018), we conducted a systematic article search to better represent the population of individuals participating in gambling-focused treatments and examined potential differences in dropout rates among the three dropout definitions most commonly used in gambling treatment studies. Identifying the risk factors for dropout is essential to reduce its occurrence and improve treatment outcomes (Pfund et al., 2020).

Method

Search Strategy

Searches were performed in PsycINFO and PUBMED. The specific Boolean search in PsycINFO was: (“Intervention”[keyword] OR “Treatment”[keyword] OR “Therapy”[keyword]) AND (“gambl*”[keyword]). The specific Boolean search in PUBMED was: (“Intervention”[title/abstract] OR “Treatment”[title/abstract] OR “Therapy”[title/abstract]) AND (“gambl*”[title/abstract]). No stopping rule was used in the search strategy. There was no start date limitation on the search, and the last search date for inclusion in the review was July 2020 and used PRISMA guidelines (Moher et al., 2009). The reference lists from a recent meta-analysis (Pfund et al., 2020), a recent systematic review (Petry et al., 2017), and the last systematic review on dropout (Melville et al., 2007), were used to search for additional studies.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Published studies from all countries were considered to increase the representativeness and generalizability of dropout from problem gambling and gambling disorder treatment. Studies were included in this review if they (1) were published in English; (2) involved a psychological treatment that was conducted face-to-face with a therapist; (3) employed cognitive and/or behavioral therapy or a motivational intervention; (4) used a manualized treatment protocol with a prescribed amount of treatment sessions; (5) reported sufficient information to calculate a dropout rate; and (6) consisted of participants who met diagnosis for problem gambling or gambling disorder according to an empirically validated assessment strategy. Studies were excluded if they (1) involved pharmacotherapy because it may affect retention to treatment; (2) involved telephone, Internet/computer, and self-help/workbooks because these modes of treatment delivery were designed for individuals who experience difficulty accessing face-to-face treatments (Ginley et al., 2019); (3) involved therapeutic approaches aside from cognitive and/or behavioral therapies or motivational interventions (e.g., Gamblers’ Anonymous, mindfulness therapies) because their comparative efficacy is less understood; (4) involved one session interventions (e.g., personalized feedback) because they have insufficient treatment length for calculating dropout rates; or (5) were review articles or descriptions of planned study protocols. When multiple studies presented data from the same sample of participants, we chose the study with the largest sample size.

Literature Search Procedure

A flowchart of the studies included in the meta-analysis is displayed in Figure 1. The initial literature search yielded a total of 3,297 articles after the removal of duplicate articles. Those 3,297 articles then underwent initial screening at the abstract level, which was done independently by the first author. A total of 136 articles were identified for possible inclusion and were then reviewed at the full-text level by the first author and second author. Using a codebook, the two reviewers agreed upon 24 independent articles for inclusion in the meta-analysis. Inter-rater agreement between two independent reviewers was 98.5%. A list of articles along with the primary reason for their exclusion from the systematic review and meta-analysis are available in the Supplementary Materials.

Data Extraction

Data extraction included study, client, and treatment variables. The study variables included the study publication year, the country where the study was conducted, and the type of study design (i.e., randomized versus nonrandomized). Most of the client and treatment variables were selected based on the list compiled in a prior review on dropout from psychological treatments for gambling concerns (Melville et al., 2007). Client variables included sociodemographic characteristics, gambling-related variables, and other psychological symptoms. The sociodemographic characteristics were mean age, gender (percentage of the sample identifying as male), ethnicity (percentage of the sample identifying as White), education level, income, employment status (percentage of the sample who were employed), and marital status (percentage of the sample that was married). The gambling-related variables were age first gambled, current debt, gambling disorder severity, gambling frequency, gambling intensity, gambling duration, and duration of problem gambling/gambling disorder. The other psychological symptoms variables were comorbid anxiety symptoms, comorbid depression symptoms, and comorbid substance use symptoms (both illicit drugs and alcohol use). Treatment variables included the prescribed treatment length in number of sessions, treatment seeking status (i.e., whether or not the sample was treatment seeking), treatment modality (i.e., cognitive and/or behavioral therapy, motivational intervention, or their combination), and treatment format (i.e., individual, group, or their combination). Participants not seeking treatment were included if they were sampled in the context of randomized controlled trials. We also extracted the methods used by study authors to define dropout. There were three definitions of dropout. The first definition was labeled “treatment completion,” which represented when authors reported the percentage of participants attending all prescribed treatment sessions. The second was labeled “sessions,” which represented when authors reported the percentage of participants discontinuing treatment before attending a minimum, prespecified number of sessions different from the prescribed number of treatment sessions for the entire treatment protocol. The third was “therapist judgment,” which represented when study therapists judged participants to be dropouts or when the authors reported the percentage of participants who “completed” treatment without specifying the number of sessions that participants were required to attend. Symptom-based definitions of dropout were not examined because no studies used those definitions.

Data Analysis Plan

When authors did not report sufficient information to calculate the overall dropout rate (i.e., the total number of clients who dropped out divided by the total number of clients who started the intervention), we contacted them by email and requested data. If authors did not respond to our initial request for data, we attempted to contact them again two weeks later. We requested data from a total of 14 authors. Three authors fulfilled our requests, and 11 authors either did not respond or could not fulfill our data requests (e.g., data were destroyed, authors no longer had access to the data).

Comprehensive Meta-Analysis v. 3.3070 was used to perform all effect size calculations. Heterogeneity in dropout rates was tested using the Q-statistic. A significant Q value indicated that dropout rates varied significantly around the overall summary dropout rate of all studies (Hedges & Olkin, 1985). The I2 statistic indicated the degree of heterogeneity and the magnitude of heterogeneity between studies was interpreted with the conventions of small (≤ 25%), medium (≤ 50%), and large (≤ 75%; Higgins et al., 2003). A random effects analysis was employed because considerable heterogeneity in dropout was expected. Publication bias was not formally evaluated in this study because no statistical test of publication bias exists for single-proportion data (Lappan et al., 2020).

We conducted random-effects meta-regressions and mixed effect subgroup analyses to determine the possible influence of covariates and moderators on dropout rates. Variables were only used in meta-regressions and subgroup analyses if there were at least 10 studies reporting sufficient statistical information (Higgins & Green, 2011). For each covariate (age, gender, marital status, employment status, gambling disorder symptom severity, prescribed treatment length, and publication year), separate regression coefficients were calculated and then tested for significance using a Z test. For each moderator (study location, dropout definition, randomized versus nonrandomized study, treatment format, treatment modality, and treatment-seeking status) we used subgroup analyses with a mixed effects model. We also conducted a subgroup analysis in dropout rates between the studies overlapping with Melville and colleagues (2007) past review on dropout and studies unique to the present review. The Q-statistic was used to test for potential moderators in which a significant Q-value indicated a difference between groups of the categorical variables for treatment dropout. Given that there were a combined 13 variables used in the meta-regressions and subgroup analyses, we used a Bonferroni-adjusted p-value of 0.004 to determine statistical significance.

Results

Study and Sample Characteristics

A total of 24 studies representing 2,791 participants were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis (Table 1). Five studies (21%) overlapped with Melville and colleagues’ (2007) past review on dropout, and 19 studies (79%) were unique to the present review. Most studies were randomized controlled trials (75%) and the remaining studies were either cohort studies or quasi-experimental trials (25%). The overall sample sizes of the 24 studies ranged from 7 to 846 (M = 116.3, SD = 205.3, median = 45.0). The 24 studies were conducted in Australia (k = 7), the United States (k = 6), Canada (k = 5), China (k = 2), Spain (k = 1), Norway (k = 1), Sweden (k = 1), and the United Kingdom (k = 1). The publication years ranged from 1997 to 2018. The average age of participants ranged from 20 to 51 years (M = 40.2, SD = 8.3, median = 43.0). The percentage of males ranged from 0% to 100% (M = 70.3, SD = 25.8, median = 74.0). Twenty samples of participants (83%) were actively seeking treatment and four samples (17%) were not.

Across the 24 studies, there were a total of 31 dropout rates (Table 1). About three-quarters (77%) of the treatments were cognitive and/or behavioral therapies, 19% were a combination of those treatments and motivational interventions, and 3% were motivational interventions. Approximately 65% of treatments were conducted in an individual format, 32% in a group format, and 3% in a combination of individual and group formats. The prescribed number of sessions ranged from 4 to 30. The most frequently occurring definition of dropout was “therapist judgment” (39%), followed by “treatment completion” (32%), and “sessions” (29%).

Overall Dropout Rate

The overall weighted dropout rate was 39.1%, 95% CI [33.0%, 45.6%]. The studies were highly heterogeneous in their dropout rates, Q(30) = 235.29, p < .001, I2 = 87.25. The dropout rates ranged from 0% to 72%. Figure 2 displays a forest plot of the weighted dropout rates with error bars for each included study. The degree of heterogeneity suggested that the overall weighted dropout rate may not be the most appropriate estimate and that dropout rates may differ depending on the potential covariates and moderators.

Covariates and Moderators of Dropout Rates

Several client and treatment variables that could covary with or moderate dropout rates were examined in this review. Table 2 presents those variables along with the number of studies reporting them and how they were reported. Table 2 also presents a rationale for why the variables could or could not be meta-analyzed based on the recommendation that there must be 10 available data points to conduct covariate or moderator analyses (Higgins & Green, 2011). Overall, 5 of 21 variables – age, gender, marital status, employment status, and gambling disorder severity – were sufficiently reported to perform meta-analysis because those variables were frequently and consistently reported in the included studies. Gambling-related variables, other psychological variables, other demographic variables, and treatment variables were not reported frequently or consistently enough in the included studies (e.g., different standardized outcome measures, different categories of educational status) to allow for meta-analysis.

Meta-Regression of Dropout Rates

Meta-regressions of dropout rates were performed on the prescribed treatment length (in number of sessions), the study publication year, and several participant characteristics (i.e., mean age, the percentage of participants who identified as male, the percentage of participants who were married, the percentage of participants who were employed, and the severity of gambling disorder symptoms; Table 3). There was one significant association between the percentage of participants who were married and dropout rates (p = 0.001). The Z-value indicated dropout rates decreased as the percentage of participants who were married increased. There was no significant relation between dropout rates and the prescribed treatment length, the study publication year, participants’ mean age, the percentage of participants who identified as male, the percentage of participants who were employed, and gambling disorder symptom severity.

Subgroup Analyses of Dropout Rates

Subgroup analyses were conducted to test for differences in dropout rates when grouped by the study location, dropout definition, randomized versus nonrandomized study, study overlapping with Melville and colleagues’ (2007) past review versus study unique to the present review, treatment format, treatment modality, and treatment seeking status (Table 4). Dropout rates were significantly different based on the country where the study was conducted, p < .001. Dropout rates from studies conducted in Sweden, the United Kingdom, and the United States were significantly higher than dropout rates from studies conducted in Australia and China. Additionally, dropout rates from studies conducted in Sweden were significantly higher than dropout rates from studies conducted in Spain and the United Kingdom. Dropout rates were significantly higher when defined as attending all treatment sessions (64%) than when defined as attending a prespecified number of sessions different from the prescribed number in the treatment protocol (33%) and when defined as study therapists judged participants to be dropouts (27%), p < .001. Dropout rates were also significantly higher among non-treatment seeking samples (70%) than among treatment seeking samples (35%), p < .001. We found no significant differences in dropout rates among randomized versus nonrandomized studies, treatment formats (i.e., individual, group, or their combination), treatment modalities (i.e., CBT, motivational interventions, or their combination), or studies overlapping with the past dropout review versus studies unique to the present review.

Discussion

The goals of the present systematic review and meta-analysis were to determine the prevalence of dropout from face-to-face psychological treatments for problem gambling and gambling disorder and to examine the client, treatment, and methodological variables associated with increased dropout risk. A total of 24 studies reporting 31 dropout rates were included in the review, which comprised a total of 2,791 participants. The overall weighted mean dropout rate of 39.1% was consistent with the 38% median rate reported in a past review (Melville et al., 2007). In a statistical comparison in dropout rates between the five studies overlapping with the past review and the 19 studies unique to our review, we found no significant differences. Thus, the overall dropout rate appears stable over time.

Meta-regressions indicated that increases in the percentage of married participants were significantly associated with decreases in dropout rates. Given that many studies did not report whether significant others were concerned about the individual in treatment or whether significant others participated in treatment, it is not completely certain as to why the percentage of married participants was associated with lower dropout rates. A burgeoning literature suggests that individuals joined by a concerned significant other in gambling treatment evidence greater treatment attendance and lower dropout rates than individuals who are not joined by a concerned significant other (Jimenez-Murcia et al., 2017). Perhaps individuals who were married had some involvement from their significant other (e.g., social support or pressure), influencing their participation and maintenance of treatment. Future research should focus on understanding reasons for dropout among individuals of various marital statuses and how treatment might be tailored toward these individuals.

Subgroup analyses indicated that dropout rates significantly varied based on the country where the study was conducted. However, studies from Australia, Canada, and the United States represented the majority of our sample (75%) and possibly underrepresented dropout from various countries in Asia and Europe. Nonetheless, these findings raise essential questions about the possible influence of macro- and meso-level factors on dropout from problem gambling and gambling disorder treatment. Macro-level factors may increase dropout risk, like when a healthcare system does not provide insurance reimbursement of gambling-focused treatment and requires a client to pay out-of-pocket for services. Meso-level factors may also increase dropout risk, like when few gambling-focused treatment providers are available within a certain community and an individual must travel a long distance to access services. Future examinations of these factors will prove critical as they may not only enhance understanding of their influence on dropout risk but their influence on initiating treatment itself.

Subgroup analyses also indicated that dropout rates significantly varied based on dropout definitions, calling into question the practicality and usefulness of each definition. Dropout rates were higher when defined as attending all sessions of a treatment protocol (i.e., treatment completion dropout) compared to when defined as attending a prespecified number of sessions different from the number in the protocol (i.e., sessions dropout) and when defined as therapists/study authors judging participants to be dropouts (i.e., therapist judgment dropout). The treatment completion and number of sessions definitions may not be useful. Individuals may not need to attend all sessions of a treatment protocol or the sessions prespecified by the researchers to achieve clinically meaningful symptom change (Pfund et al., 2018). The definition based on therapists’ judgments may be subjective and influenced by biases. For example, the therapist judgment definition may be significantly lower than the treatment completion definition because therapists might overestimate their abilities, hold a personal investment in the effectiveness of their treatment, or overestimate their clients’ progress (e.g., Kruger, 1999; Tracey et al., 2014). Above all, none of these definitions are tied to participants’ actual symptom changes. The lack of ties to symptom change may result in misclassifying many participants as dropouts when they made symptom improvements and misclassifying many participants as completers when they did not make symptom improvements (Pfund et al., 2018; Swift et al., 2009). The significant difference in dropout rates based on the adopted dropout definition may have prevented the identification of other significant findings, such as the relation between dropout rates and treatment length. Future research should examine session-to-session symptom change to inform decisions about whether and when dropout occurred. Such studies would require the routine administration of a brief outcome measure (e.g., Gambling Symptom Assessment Scale; Kim et al., 2009) and may guide the field toward a more precise understanding of what constitutes an adequate dose of gambling treatment (Pfund et al., 2020).

Our review illuminated inconsistencies in how covariates and moderators were reported across studies. We found that only five variables – age, gender, marital status, employment status, and gambling disorder severity – were sufficiently reported to conduct meta-regression and subgroup analyses. Although many studies reported on gambling-related client variables (e.g., gambling frequency, intensity, and duration), we could not meta-analyze their covarying or moderating effect on dropout rates because the study authors utilized different measurements (e.g., percentage of income wagered versus dollar amount wagered) and time intervals (e.g., past 7 days versus past 30 days). We recommend that gambling treatment researchers report other gambling-specific variables in future studies (e.g., age first gambled, current debt, gambling frequency, intensity, and duration) and come to a consensus on how these variables should be reported.

Perhaps the greatest limitation of this review was that few variables could be included in the meta-analyses on dropout risk factors. The research on dropout from gambling-focused treatments currently lags behind the research on dropout from general psychotherapy and substance use disorder treatment. Studies in those other areas have now used meta-analysis to examine a much larger array of factors associated with dropout risk (Lappan et al., 2020; Swift & Greenberg, 2012). Not only should gambling treatment researchers report and agree on a set of gambling-specific variables, but they should also expand data collection to other important variables such as the therapeutic alliance and therapist factors (Meier et al., 2006; Roos & Werbart, 2013). An increasingly growing number of studies have attended to therapists’ effects on dropout and other treatment outcomes, in which low therapeutic alliance and specific therapist factors increase dropout risk (Miller & Moyers, 2015; Zimmermann et al., 2017). However, these issues have received little to no attention in studies of gambling treatment. In future studies, we suggest that researchers collect data on the therapeutic alliance and therapist factors (e.g., empathy, training, supervision) that might influence dropout and other gambling-related treatment outcomes.

Another limitation of the present systematic review and meta-analysis was that we may not have fully represented the literature on psychological treatment for problem gambling and gambling disorder. Our article search only used two databases, which may have resulted in missing studies that would have otherwise met eligibility criteria (Bramer et al., 2017). We could not include 11 studies because they did not provide sufficient information to calculate dropout rates. To minimize these limitations, we searched the reference lists of prior published reviews on gambling treatment, and we contacted authors to request data on dropout rates when they were not reported. Several authors did not respond to our requests, and several others indicated the data for their study were destroyed or no longer available to them. The inability to include many of these studies may result in a misrepresentation of dropout from psychological treatment from problem gambling and gambling disorder. However, the consistency of the weighted dropout rate identified in the current review with the rate in the last comprehensive review of studies increases confidence in the representativeness of the literature.

A third limitation was that we could not conduct meta-analyses on client or treatment variables between dropouts and completers. At this time, it does not appear standard for authors to report differences on these variables between completers and dropouts in studies of psychological treatment for problem gambling and gambling disorder. Only 4 of the 24 studies included in our review conducted analyses between completers and dropouts, preventing us from conducting meta-analysis. Therefore, covariate and subgroup analyses were restricted to participant demographics for entire study samples rather than true comparisons of completers and dropouts. This limitation should be borne in mind when interpreting the results, and future studies should report client and treatment variables (e.g., setting, intensity, population) between completers and dropouts to aid future meta-analyses.

A fourth limitation was that we did not examine dropout from remote treatments (e.g., telephone calls, Internet treatment, mailed self-help workbooks) for problem gambling and gambling disorder. We chose not to examine dropout from these treatments because the individuals completing these treatments may differ from individuals attending face-to-face treatments. For example, individuals completing remote treatments may not be motivated or able to attend face-to-face treatments. We believe a review of remote treatments would be best done separately. Such a review would be especially timely as clinicians turn to remote treatments at increasing rates during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Despite the limitations of this review, the findings provide clarity that dropout remains a challenge when conducting face-to-face psychological treatments for problem gambling and gambling disorder. Given that continued attendance to treatment is significantly associated with improvements in the therapeutic outcome (Pfund et al., 2020), it is vital to focus efforts on helping individuals persist until treatment completion. These efforts might involve adapting existing treatment components to culturally relevant variables (e.g., gender, race). Alternatively, these efforts may involve modifying existing systems to address the macro- and meso-level factors that hinder treatment-seeking (e.g., insurance reimbursement, proximal access to clinics). In future research, it is especially important to examine the session-to-session changes in gambling symptoms and behavior. These changes could inform when dropout occurs and how much treatment is “enough.”
