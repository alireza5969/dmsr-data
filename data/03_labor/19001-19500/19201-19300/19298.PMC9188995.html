The application of GIS in homelessness research and service delivery: A qualitative systematic review

Sara Semborski[*]

Harmony Rhoades

Laura Petry

Benjamin F. Henwood

 

University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA, USA

Author notes:

Abstract

GIS is increasingly popular in the study of complex social issues, such as homelessness. This study aims to assess how GIS has been leveraged and applied to homelessness research and service delivery. Systematic searching of sixteen databases was completed between January and March of 2021 using the terms “homeless” and “Geospatial Information Systems (GIS).” A final sample of 19 sources were identified from a total of 1719 identified sources. Through quantitative and qualitative methods, the included sources examined 1) static location characteristics associated with homelessness, and 2) mobility of homeless persons.

Introduction

1.

Homelessness as a spatial problem

1.1.

Homelessness is an enduring public health challenge faced by many countries throughout the developed world. Quantifying homelessness on a global scale is difficult due to cultural differences in definition, causes, and meaning. The present review considers homelessness as the experience of not having private space or property. Individuals experiencing homelessness have no option but to live on public land (Essert, 2016). Definitions of homelessness vary from context to context and even within the same context dependent on governing authorities. Often focused on the space one occupies or their accommodations (Taylor, 2013), individuals experiencing homelessness are seen as lacking a home (Amore et al., 2011) or a “fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence” (HUD, 2021), and often reside in areas “not meant for human habitation” (NAEH, 2012), such as a car, park, abandoned building, bus/train station, etc. Other definitions have included people who couch surf or double up, at times staying in hotels or motels (Curry et al., 2017). Sheltered homeless individuals are those occupying emergency or transitional housing (NAEH, 2012). Public spaces have been deemed “not meant for human habitation” (NAEH, 2012); and thus, determining whether a person is homeless requires an assessment of the space they occupy. Further, the European Typology of Homelessness and Housing Exclusion (ETHOS), advocated as providing a basis for measurement of homelessness across Europe and East Asia (Kennet and Mizuuchi, 2010; Iwata, 2021), provides additional support for these operational definitions and living situations by conceptually defining the living conditions of those living outside or in emergency shelter as “roofless,” those staying in temporary living situations such as hostels, transitional housing, or institutions as “houseless,” those staying temporarily with family or friends (i.e., couch surfing) as “housing insecure,” and those living in structures not meant for human habitation such as cars and abandoned buildings as “inadequately housed” (Amore et al., 2011).

Application of mapping in homelessness research and service delivery

1.2.

The application of GIS in the social sciences has been occurring since the early 1990s (Anselin, 1992; Felke, 2014). From its initial use, GIS technologies have been utilized to identify gaps in social services, determine access to care, and for program evaluation. Utilizing spatial statistics – most commonly spatial autocorrelation and spatial regression – in social science research has increased the overall use of GIS, as it allows for the relationships between geographic space and other variables to be examined (Felke, 2014). A common way to capture geographic space in social science research is through measuring activity space. Activity space is defined as “local areas within which people move or travel during the course of their activities during a specified time period” and functions as a measure of an individual’s spatial behavior (Rai et al., 2007). Though these methods are increasingly featured in social science literature, the scope of geospatial applications in homelessness research and service delivery, in particular, is not entirely clear. Nevertheless, because homelessness is, in part, an inherently spatial issue drastically impacted by the immediate environment, communities across the globe have begun to leverage geospatial technology for measurement of homelessness and to inform interventions. Mapping can support the development of policies and interventions that are most efficient and effective for the community (Loubert, 2010). In fact, several groups have utilized GIS to create maps to track the rates of local homelessness (Lambert, 2015; MacFarlane, 2017), support intervention in times of duress (Hembree, 2019; Wong and Hillier, 2001), and organize prevention efforts (Loubert, 2010). The goal of this systematic review is to assess the extent to which GIS has been applied to homelessness research and service delivery, including both substantive and methodological contributions.

Methods

2.

This systematic review adopted the method suggested by Moher, Liberati, Tetzla, Altman and the PRISMA group (2009) and was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42021225659) on February 19, 2021. The researchers did some initial searching to identify relevant terms and scope the project. No systematic reviews on this topic were identified. This initial searching allowed the researchers to define inclusion and exclusion variables with the knowledge gained from keyword searching with terms Homeless and Geospatial Information Systems (GIS).

Sixteen databases were identified as relevant and searched between January and March of 2021. The broad research question that guided formulating systematic searching was “How has GIS been leveraged and applied to homelessness research?” with the intent to increase knowledge of people experiencing homelessness. More specifically, the research question became more succinct as it aims to fill the knowledge gap by assessing the state of the evidence and research that has applied GIS to issues of and related to homelessness across disciplines.

Inclusion variables

2.1.

The date range inclusion variable was 2010–2021. The age of the population was restricted to 18 or older. This decision was to ensure thorough scoping of research on our population of interest. This research team believes a systematic review of the unhoused population under 18 years of age requires its own systematic review that can sufficiently cover articles on this population while carefully extracting data relevant to minors, such as foster care and other aspects associated with the unique developmental stage. Further, research must have focused on the actively unhoused or formerly unhoused populations. The research must have used GIS (location Geocoded), but intervention did not have to be GIS specific. Both qualitative and quantitative empirical and peer-review articles were included. International and national studies published in English in both urban and rural settings were included.

Exclusion criteria

2.2.

Books, dissertations, opinion papers, and editorials were not included. Studies of populations under 18 of age were excluded. Articles that have a focus of comorbidity were excluded and/or natural disasters studies were excluded. Like restrictions based on age, unhoused populations due to natural disasters were found to have their own specific set of variables. While this research team believes GIS could be a valuable tool for homeless populations due to natural disasters, the body of literature is substantial and warrants a distinct review. The purpose of this review was to have a well-defined narrow set of exclusion variables to ensure comparable studies such that the aggregated results provide actionable data-based decision making.

Search strategy

2.3.

A systematic search strategy following PRISMA and PROSPERO protocols were utilized. Both MeSH and PICO terms were used, and keyword searching was used in databases without thesauri. In databases that provide a thesaurus and do not use MeSH or PICO keyword searching was used to find the correct term used in the specific database thesaurus. One Zotero library was set up for this research project with all researchers using the same library during strategic searching. Systematic searching details can be seen in Table 1, including databases and search terms.

A total of 1719 sources were uploaded from Zotero into Covidence for the de-duplicating, abstract screening, full text screening, data extraction and quality assurance processes. Covidence immediately de-duplicated articles removing 402 resources. The total number of resources that advanced to abstract screening was 1227.

Data screening

2.4.

All researchers participated in the abstract screening of the 1227 resources in Covidence. A minimum of two independent researchers were required to screen each abstract for evidence of inclusion variables in titles and abstracts, with a third researcher brought in to screen any abstract that did not have consensus. As a result of the abstract screening process 1161 resources were eliminated due to exclusion variables, leaving 66 studies to move to full text review screening. Two independent researchers were required to screen each full text article for evidence of inclusion variables. See Table 2 for inclusion variables. Forty-five articles were removed for not meeting inclusion variables. See Fig. 1 for the PRISMA Chart.

Data extraction and quality assurance

2.5.

Information from the remaining resources of evidence were included in the final sample that was extracted and collated using Excel for Microsoft 365, Version 2106. Tables summarized information on the study sample, setting/context, data collection methods, analytical techniques, measures, results, and conclusions. Additionally, quality was assessed and recorded, considering methodological quality (i.e., were the methods appropriate for aims/questions), precision, and internal and external validity. Two members of the team completed a summary and quality review for each article, independently. Tables were then used to collate data for the presentation of results and interpretation. Inductive content analysis was used in the analysis of the sources of evidence due to the inclusion of both quantitative and qualitative results in our final sample (Silverman, 2010). This qualitative approach to analyzing included sources in systematic reviews offered a framework to conceptually organize the extracted data and make evidenced-based inferences (Finfgeld-Connet, 2014) which resulted in two primary axes of analysis: 1) focus (i.e., static location characteristics or mobile location tracking) and methodology (i.e., quantitative or mixed methods).

Results

3.

A total of 19 sources of evidence were included in our final sample. Seven articles utilized exclusively quantitative methods while 12 articles included a qualitative component, taking a mixed methods approach. All included sources utilized GIS and gathered information regarding the experience of homelessness. GIS allows for location data to be integrated with other descriptive information; therefore, increased understandings of location is inherent to GIS. Ten articles specifically sought to understand static location and neighborhood characteristics associated with various facets of homelessness. In addition to gaining insight into location, GIS can also support the analysis of mobile location, often obtained from GPS tracking devices. Nine sources were focused on mobility. Some achieved this by tracking participants with GPS devices, while other gained mobility data through self-report. These articles, too, aimed to understand location, but built on the basic analysis of static location by considering mobility and movement through multiple locations. See Appendix 1 for sources focused on static location characteristics and Appendix 2 for sources focused on mobile location tracking. Articles will now be discussed by their focus and methodology to get a better sense of how GIS was utilized. Further, sources are displayed by their focus (i.e., static location characteristics or mobile location tracking) and methodology in Table 3.

Static location characteristics

3.1.

Quantitative.

3.1.1.

Of the ten articles aimed at understanding location and neighborhood characteristics associated with various facets of homelessness, six utilized exclusively quantitative methods. Four articles examined location in reference to services, such as where individuals were more likely to become homeless, receive homeless services (Leopold et al., 2017), or pass away (Rowen, 2017), and community characteristics associated with supportive housing, that is housing coupled with supportive services, as a homeless intervention (Tsai et al., 2011) and medical clinics for homeless veteran women (Gawron et al., 2017).

Leopold et al. (2017) sought to explore movement patterns of individuals experiencing homelessness, comparing urban, suburban, and rural environments. Abt Associates conducted secondary data analysis of the Homelessness Management Information System, commonly referred to HMIS, in Iowa and Michigan. The last known address prior to entering homelessness was geocoded and compared to census data. Data showed urban residents were more likely to become homeless and suburban individuals tended to move to urban areas to receive homeless services. Factors affecting neighborhood homelessness were found to vary by state, but included inexpensive rental housing, proportion of single mothers, percent of African American and Hispanic households (Leopold et al., 2017).

Likewise, a paper published by Rowen in 2017 examined the locations of where homeless women died in the southwest United States to effectively tailor services to locations where they may be needed. Using the records of the coroner in Orange County, California, hot spot analysis was completed to identify spatial clustering. Findings show that deaths were concentrated in areas where homeless service providers were also clustered, indicating that service providers attract women experiencing homelessness that are vulnerable and in need of services (Rowen, 2017).

The goal of the paper produced by Tsai et al. (2011) was to describe how one’s neighborhood changes in the transition from homelessness to housing and gauge whether neighborhood characteristics impact clinical and subjective status of individuals at 11 supportive housing sites across nine states. Addresses provided at baseline and 12-month follow up were geocoded and community characteristics by census block were gathered from U.S. census data and other community indexes. Findings indicated that despite being placed in supportive housing, formerly homeless residents were not being placed in improved communities that are safer and have a higher quality of life index. Rather, the communities where supportive housing was located resembled communities where participants were actively homeless. However, after one year in housing, the average participant moved into communities with larger population densities, larger White populations, and smaller Black populations than when they were homeless. Black participants reported higher social support and lower subjective distress in communities with larger Black populations, while White participants reported greater neighborhood satisfaction in neighborhoods with higher education levels (Tsai et al., 2011).

Gawron et al. (2017) assessed geographic availability of Veterans Association Medical Centers (VAMC) and family planning clinics for women veterans experiencing homelessness in Texas, U.S. between 2002 and 2015. This study utilized secondary data analysis and geocoded addresses to compare to locations of VAMCs and clinics using drive distances, ArcGIS network analyst “closest facility” method, cluster and outlier analysis (i.e., spatial autocorrelation) using Moran’s I and hot spot analysis (i.e., getis-ord gi*). Authors found publicly funded family planning clinics were more geographically accessible than VAMCs for homeless women vets and longer drive time to be associated with attrition from VHA health care in women Veterans (Gawron et al., 2017).

The final two articles focused on location using exclusively quantitative methods and examined aspects of the physical built environment, such as geographic proximity (Nakagawa and Iizuka, 2020) and the weather (Siordia et al., 2014). Using a fly over drone, clusters of homeless encampments were identified and measured in terms of their size and proximity to one another along the banks of the Tama River in Tokyo, Japan. Additionally, the drone was able to capture the temperature of the encampment using a thermal camera. Similarly, atmospheric temperature was examined in relation to the number of sheltered homeless individuals (i.e., those staying in emergency or transitional housing) using county-level data from the U.S. Census Bureau and North America Land Data Assimilation System (Phase 2). A positive correlation between the number of sheltered homeless individuals and temperature, a positive correlation between homelessness rates and total population, and a negative correlation between percentage of non-Hispanic White persons and homelessness rates were identified (Siordia et al., 2014).

Mixed methods.

3.1.2.

Four articles took a mixed methods approach to understanding location, often using a combination of secondary data and interviews or geocoded locations derived from qualitative interviews. One article identified groups of people in need of improved access to clean needles to improve harm reduction methods. Though the sample consisted of intravenous drug users, one of the two sampling sites was a homeless service provider, thus warranting inclusion. Through qualitative interviews, locations where individuals borrowed needles from others or gave needles to others were identified and geocoded. Heat maps were then generated showing response density, empirically identifying viable areas to target for satellite needle exchange programs. This is important as a number of obvious weaknesses have been identified with fixed site needle exchanges. Maps were stated to have been used to target services, in conversations with local officials and other agencies regarding funding, as well as needle provision and policy (Davidson et al., 2011).

The second article using qualitative methods to gain insight into static location was a piece produced by Alexander-Eitzman et al. (2013). These authors examined neighborhood characteristics in Saint Louis, Missouri, U.S. as individuals moved from homelessness into housing. Initially recruited from shelters, the study interviewed 400 people three times: once at baseline and then twice in the two years following. A total of 257 people (69%) completed all three interviews. At each interview participants provided the location or address of their usual place of residence over the previous year and found sleep locations to be more dispersed after entering housing, whereas sleep locations while homeless were found to be concentrated at the highly urbanized urban core of the city. Additionally, areas participants move to after homelessness were characterized by less housing availability and less economic distress and material deprivation (Alexander-Eitzman et al., 2013).

An additional neighborhood characteristic, green space, was analyzed in a small city in Poland finding the majority of the city’s homeless population resided within an urban green space. This was done by compiling a map of where people who lived on the streets from data recorded by the Municipal Social Welfare Centre. Then semi-structured interviews were completed with street workers to obtain information regarding the use of urban green spaces and the activities of the unhoused individuals spending time in those places. Results present heterogenous experiences in urban green spaces. For some folks, the utilization of urban green spaces occurred out of necessity, but for others urban green spaces have positive associations as attractive spaces to socialize and rest. Authors introduce the concept of environmental justice and discuss implications of urban renewal projects which often lead to displacement driven by informal movements, such as NIMBYism (i.e., “not in my back yard”) (Koprowska et al., 2020).

One final article relied on field notes that were then geocoded, rather than GPS devices, to assess use of bicycles by the homeless populations in California, U.S. Utilizing what is known as “mobile ethnography,” researchers walked pathways frequented by individuals experiencing homelessness while recording field notes of conversations, behaviors, and location of observed behavior. Locations were then geocoded for analysis and findings focused on the manner and usefulness of biking within the population as bicycles enable travel with flexibility often low in cost with fewer police patrols. Finally, authors suggest the concept of interactive accessibility to describe how bicycles seem to promote accessibility for the population, “rooted in the dynamic and unfolding social context (Parker, 2019).”

Mobile location tracking

3.2.

Quantitative.

3.2.1.

A single source utilized novel methods referred to as Geographic Ecological Momentary Assessment (GEMA) to track individuals smoking cessation attempts in reference to location, specifically proximity to a homeless shelter in Dallas, Texas, U.S. GEMA was utilized to collect data on mood in real time from one week pre-quit through one week post-quit finding that closer proximity to the shelter was associated with negative affect only during the post-quit week suggesting that the shelter environment may increase challenges associated with smoking cessation (Reitzel et al., 2014).

Mixed methods.

3.2.2.

Eight articles took a mixed methods approach to mobility and tracking. Three sources utilized GPS tracking devices, coupled with qualitative interviews. In one study, GPS devices were deployed for 24 h, and pre- and post-deployment interviews were conducted in order to compare the predicted, recorded, and reported travel distances. Wide discrepancies between GPS-measured and self-report distances were identified, but deployments were overall successful (North et al., 2017). Further work sought to examine spatial mobility of homeless people in urban areas in two cities in Czechia and explored if mobility is related to their housing situation, finding that those with better housing situations traveled farther than those explicitly homeless (i.e., street homeless) (Šimon et al., 2020). Another article with the same sample from Czechia used GPS to create activity space measures and compared the size of activity space to geographic traits as well as individual characteristics. Geographic findings suggest individual differences alone cannot explain differences in activity space size, but activity space size is also significantly driven by city size (Šimon et al., 2019).

Four mixed methods studies had similar aims of examining mobility but utilized participatory mapping to obtain and geocode locations. Participatory mapping is a qualitative technique where semi-structured interviewing includes a drawing portion. Locations drawn on maps and discussed in the qualitative interview are then geocoded for analysis. One of these studies examined accessibility by use of Euclidean Distance and the Multiple Ring Buffer tool to identify the proximity of various community features to locations of supportive housing sites. Initial participatory mapping elicited locations, activities, and resources that 37 formerly homeless adults utilized and engaged in over the prior three months. Activity spaces were generated, and larger activity spaces were found to vary based on the number and specific types of community features identified (Chan et al., 2014). Further, with the same sample of 37 formerly homeless individuals in another article, activity space was compared with a community integration measure, reinforcing that the number of locations identified was indeed associated with larger activity spaces (Chan et al., 2014). Again, with the same sample an additional paper examined service use after receiving supportive housing finding the use of case management to positively correlate with larger activity spaces (Chan, 2014). Likewise, an article by Townley et al. (2016) also utilized participatory mapping to examine activity space and location compared to community and psychological well-being, finding well-being to be positively associated with number of locations but not the size of one’s activity space (Townley et al., 2016).

The final source is an article that is a descriptive piece about study design using Geographic Ecological Momentary Assessment (GEMA), a novel but increasingly popular method that merges GPS tracking and Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) that allows for survey delivery in real time. This article put forth by Henwood et al. (2019) describes the Log My Life study, a mixed methods study that provides cell phones to young adults experiencing homelessness as well as formerly homeless young adults residing in supportive housing. Those enrolled in the Log My Life study completed seven days of EMA whereby location was recorded in 60 s epochs while the phone was on, including locations where surveys were completed. The article described not only study methodology that includes both quantitative and qualitative methods, but potential analysis that may be performed on such data and its use in guiding practice and policy work (Henwood et al., 2019).

Discussion

4.

Results of this systematic review identify how GIS has been harnessed to better understand homelessness. Additionally, this review also collates information gathered through GIS regarding experiences associated with homelessness and service provision. Articles were recognized as using GIS to 1) pinpoint characteristics of static locations associated with homelessness or 2) track mobile locations of individuals experiencing homelessness. The articles included in this review used GIS in some capacity; meaning, all articles included a quantitative component. Nine (47%) of the included sources also included a qualitative component, making for mixed methods studies. This is important because it indicates that GIS can be leveraged to accomplish an array of objectives in a diversity of disciplines. Certainly, some sources utilize GIS in a more traditional way, examining aspects associated with urban planning and mapping, such as green spaces (Koprowska et al., 2020), transportation (Parker, 2019), and environmental temperature (Siordia et al., 2014; Nakagawa and Iizuka, 2020). Many articles also appealed to social science literature, identifying locations where interventions should be targeted (Alexander-Eitzman et al., 2013; Rowen, 2017; Leopold et al., 2017), behavior associated with specific locations (Reitzel et al., 2014; Davidson et al., 2011) and mobility (Chan, 2014; Šimon et al., 2019, 2020), and other environmental exposures (Tsai et al., 2011; Gawron et al., 2017; Leopold et al., 2017) that have the potential to impact health outcomes (Townley et al., 2016).

Overall, these articles show that GIS provides the opportunity to use community-level variables in analysis and highlights why spatial understandings of homelessness are important for thoughtful service delivery and the implementation of policies that affect service delivery. Articles featured in this review utilized community-level variables to identify the likelihood of becoming homeless in certain geographic areas (Leopold et al., 2017) and identify factors protective against becoming homeless, including features associated with increased community integration (Chan, 2014; Chan et al., 2014; Chan et al., 2014) and psychological well-being (Townley et al., 2016). Many articles had direct implications for service delivery, including where services should be located to best meet areas of higher risk and need (e.g., higher mortality rates (Rowen, 2017), and in need of harm reduction interventions (Davidson et al., 2011)). It may be particularly important to consider the impacts of location on service delivery, as it is common to have homeless services geographically clustered. While clustered services may increase utilization (National Academies of Sciences, 2018), it also concentrates the number of vulnerable, high-needs individuals in a geographic area. Rowen (2017) identified higher mortality rates in such areas, in line with previous literature that indicated higher sexual assaults in areas with more homeless services (Heslin et al., 2007). Sources identified in this review appear to heed the call of previous research that indicated a need for large-scale homelessness identification efforts, as well as the identification of geospatial gaps (Wong and Hillier, 2001). Other service-related implications include correlates of, and spatial factors related to service use, like migrating to urban centers to receive services (Leopold et al., 2017) and the positive correlation between longer travel times to service locations and attrition (Gawron et al., 2017). These geographic assessments and others indicate mobility relates to service utilization (Parker et al., 2011; Šimon et al., 2019, 2020; Chan, 2014; Chan et al., 2014; Chan et al., 2014) and ultimately well-being (Townley et al., 2016). Other contexts associated with homelessness, such as emergency shelter and supportive housing, were evaluated identifying potential issues with their location (Tsai et al., 2011) and how proximity to their locations impact behaviors such as smoking cessation attempts (Reitzel et al., 2014). Understanding geospatial gaps and barriers to services allows providers to aim interventions where they are most needed, for example by implementing mobile services that reach people where they are or providing transportation support. Additional possible impacts of this work include city planning to address homelessness beyond shelter and housing, including clean-up stations.

Finally, articles were identified as either being purely quantitative in their methodology or taking a mixed methods approach. Quantitative methods included geocoding addresses from survey (Tsai et al., 2011), use of a fly over drone to take pictures (Nakagawa and Iizuka, 2020), and geocoded momentary surveys known as Geographic Ecological Momentary Assessment (GEMA) (Henwood et al., 2019; Reitzel et al., 2014). Perhaps the most utilized quantitative approach was utilizing secondary data to either map geocoded addresses or merged with census/zip code shapefiles for geographic analysis (Gawron et al., 2017; Siordia et al., 2014; Rowen, 2017; Leopold et al., 2017). Analyses included hotspot/outlier analysis (Rowen, 2017; Gawron et al., 2017) and closest facility to estimate proximity (Gawron et al., 2017); however, the spatial statistics included were found to be largely descriptive in nature.

Qualitative methods were utilized in nine of our included sources. One qualitative approach was to use interviews to gather addresses which were then geocoded for quantitative use (Davidson et al., 2011). Other approaches generated maps from secondary data sources and then used interviews to supplement the quantitative information (Koprowska et al., 2020). Likewise, survey data was collected followed by qualitative interviews (Alexander-Eitzman et al., 2013). On one occasion, mobile ethnography, also known as behavior mapping, was used to examine the use of bicycles (Parker, 2019). GPS tracking was used in several sources, sometimes combined with mobility interviews (Siordia et al., 2019; Henwood et al., 2019) and used to compare self-report maps (North et al., 2017; Šimon et al., 2020). Comparisons of GPS yielded mixed results. North et al. (2017) indicated wide discrepancies between GPS measures and self-report, while Šimon et al. (2020) declared participatory mapping to be valid and feasible, but did not state the degree to which qualitative mapping matched GPS. Other articles use the participatory mapping process to geocode locations (Chan, 2014, 2016; Chan et al., 2014a,b; Townley et al., 2016), an innovative method with a limitation of self-report.

Other limitations often associated with examining the experience of homelessness spatially deal with the fact that often those experiencing homelessness are highly mobile. Meaning, where someone is currently homeless often does not indicate where they became homeless. Leopold et al. (2017) attempts to deal with this by using the last known address prior to homelessness. Further represented in terms of methodology is a paper that discussed how to innovatively utilize GIS to gather real-time location data within a social science framework (Henwood et al., 2019). This piece, though the only methods paper included in the results, provides further support for the feasibility of incorporating GIS in social science research and in researched focused on homeless populations. Overall, articles in this review also support the feasibility of GIS in social service delivery research and successfully tracking the mobility individuals experiencing homelessness to get a better sense of the contexts associated with homelessness.

Limitations

4.1.

This work comes with limitations. Due to the numerous definitions of homelessness and the international scope of this review, there is the chance that some work may have been missed due to a misspecification of the population. This speaks to the fact that homelessness, though global, may have regional facets unique to the local context. However, use of the commonly applied operational definition of homelessness (HUD, 2021) provides some relief from this limitation. Further use of MeSH and PICO terms in systematic search methods allowed us to pinpoint verbiage to effectively capture homelessness and offered uniformity in searching across databases. Additionally, there is the potential geospatial methods utilized may not be limited to GIS and classified alternatively as well. Further, we may have missed relevant material due to only conducting our search in English, b) utilizing search terms common to experiences of homelessness in the United States, and c) the decision to exclude dissertations. However, systematic searching with the use of PICO, MeSH, and database thesauri for standardized terms, mitigate the limitation of any missed relevant resources. Furthermore, the systematic review protocols that were followed provide the most robust internal validity available.

Implications for practice, research, and society

4.2.

GIS remains an innovative method in the social sciences with much insight to offer, but only when applied critically. The innovation of the method in and of itself does not justify its use, but rather, its application should be justified by the research question, access to the defined population, and the data researchers can collect. Results have clear implications for practice, research, and policy. Findings show feasibility of including a geospatial component in social science research, and with homeless populations including specific sub-population such as older adults, those experiencing chronic homelessness, families and youth. Specific findings have the capacity to directly inform service delivery in terms of outreach and engagement with persons experiencing homelessness. Local governments may rely on spatial statistics to inform policy as communities are deciding on the types of services to offer and where to place those services within the community. Beyond use at a regional or local level, GIS could be harnessed to examine mass displacement and homelessness due to global warming at an international or global level (Kidd et al., 2021). Further, homeless counts are sometimes done using location and/or census tracts but have the potential to be done using GIS. Likewise, it may be possible to utilize GIS to examine the role of institutional racism in homelessness in terms of redlining (Paul et al., 2020) and other forms of environmental racism (Goodling, 2020). Finally, there exists potential in real-time data capture, as discussed in Henwood et al. (2019), but in terms of intervention. Ecological momentary intervention (EMI) (Heron & Smyth, 2010) may be able to include a geographic component and provide key data regarding health behaviors and intervention efficacy. Beyond ecological momentary assessments and interventions, there seems to be enormous potential to combine GIS and spatial statistics with other innovative research methods, like social network analysis, for instance.

Conclusion

5.

Nineteen articles utilizing GIS and focused on homelessness were published between the years of 2010 and 2021. These articles intended to inform research and practice with individuals experiencing homeless by gathering information about locations and neighborhoods associated with homelessness and tracking the mobility of those experiencing homelessness. While using GIS to better understand the experience of homelessness can certainly be used for benevolent purposes (e.g., understanding where services should be located), it can also be used for more nefarious purposes (e.g., identifying places where camps are located for the purposes of sweeps or “clean-ups” which displace people experiencing homelessness). Vulnerable populations are often put at risk when the spaces they access are disclosed; thus, additional care and precautions ought to be exercised. However, this review shows that GIS is a promising approach and can be seen as an underutilized method given the inherent spatial dimension of homelessness. More research is needed to figure out how to best leverage given specific concerns related to use with vulnerable populations. Future studies may want to consider incorporating a geospatial component in conjunction with additional protections for human subjects in place to properly assess how the environment associated with homelessness influences the aspects of homelessness. This systematic review may be reproduced in the future with different inclusion and exclusion variables and/or as more research using GIS emerges. Overall, this systematic review can serve as a starting point for researchers interested in how GIS has been used to study homelessness and service delivery.
