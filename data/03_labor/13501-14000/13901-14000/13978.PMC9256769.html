Abstract

Background:

Mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices, such as ventricular assist devices (VADs) and total artificial hearts (TAHs), have become a vital therapeutic option in the treatment of end-stage heart failure for adult patients. Such therapeutic options continue to be limited for pediatric patients. Clinicians initially adapted or scaled existing adult devices for pediatric patients; however, these adult devices are not designed to support the anatomical structure and varying flow capacities required for this population and are generally operated “off-design”, which risks complications such as hemolysis and thrombosis. Devices designed specifically for the pediatric population that seek to address these shortcomings are now emerging and gaining FDA approval.

Methods:

To analyze the competitive landscape of pediatric MCS devices, we conducted a systematic literature review. Approximately 27 devices were studied in detail: 8 were established or previously approved designs, and 19 were under development (11 VADs, 5 Fontan assist devices, and 3 TAHs).

Results:

Despite significant progress, there is still no pediatric pump technology that satisfies the unique and distinct design constraints and requirements to support pediatric patients, including the wide range of patient sizes, increased cardiovascular demand with growth, and anatomic and physiologic heterogeneity of congenital heart disease.

Conclusions:

Forward-thinking design solutions are required to overcome these challenges and to ensure the translation of new therapeutic MCS devices for pediatric patients.

Background & Motivation

Severe heart disease in adult and pediatric patients is the leading cause of death around the world, taking 17.9 million lives each year.[1] Heart failure (HF), a consequence of severe heart disease where the heart muscle is unable to drive blood to provide sufficient end organ perfusion, impacts world health, affecting 64 million adults and pediatric patients annually.[2] The pediatric population faces unique challenges as compared to the adult population. Studies place the global pediatric HF incidence between 0.97 and 7.4 per 100,000 children.[3] In the US, 1.3 million children are born with a congenital heart defect, and over 40,000 of them require immediate undergo surgical intervention.[4] Infants with complex malformations of their heart chambers or surrounding vasculature may require multiple open-heart surgeries to build a cardiovascular anatomy and physiology that can sustain life.[5] These complex cases of congenital heart disease necessitate continued clinical care over the patient’s life span, as pediatric patients with heart defects are at higher risk of developing premature congestive HF.[5] Children may also develop non-congenital cardiac diseases at an early age, such as cardiomyopathy, leading to depressed ventricular function. Pediatric cardiac diseases can also manifest developmental setbacks, including neurological impairment and growth delays.[6]

Although pharmacological agents are prescribed, these only slow the progression towards end-stage HF.[6] Approximately 15,000 children in the US are hospitalized with HF-related conditions each year, and there is a mortality rate of 7–15%.[7–9] Cardiac transplantation is the standard of care, but each year, the number of patients in need of a transplant exceeds the number of donor organs available; the eligible patient population continues to increase annually, ensuring lasting difficulty in donor procurement.[10] Donor-recipient size matching is also more complex for pediatric patients due to anticipated growth.[10] It is encouraging that 74% of children are matched and undergo transplantation within 90 days of listing [11]; however, the mortality rate for those waiting ranges from 5–39% worldwide.[8,12,13] There remains an unmet clinical need to innovate safe and effective MCS for pediatric patients with HF for use as bridge therapies or long-term chronic care solutions.

Pediatric mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices, such as extracorporeal pumping circuits, ventricular assist devices (VADs), percutaneous blood pumps, and total artificial hearts (TAHs), have been and are being developed as bridge-to-transplant (BTT), bridge-to-recovery, and long-term support alternatives. Analysis from the UNOS database correlates the adoption of this technology with a 50% reduction in waitlist mortality.[10] Despite this progress, there remain critical aspects in which these devices can be improved. Here we studied published data of established and developing pediatric MCS devices to assess progress and form a grounded vision of the field’s trajectory. We identified challenges in pediatric MCS and how such challenges influence device design. We also compare key metrics against research-derived design criteria to 1) emphasize the benefits and shortcomings of designs and 2) highlight devices that could provide MCS over growth cycles from infancy to adolescence.

Systematic Review Methods

We utilized a systematic review process whereby combinations of keywords were entered into electronic databases and used to locate relevant articles. Databases searched included PubMed, Web of Science, ProQuest, and OVID MedLine. Figure 1A illustrates the review process, and Figure 1B shows the multi-stage filtering procedure. The search was constrained to articles that are electronically available and published 2010 or later. The Boolean structured search string for each database is given:

“Pediatric AND Heart AND (Impeller OR Axial OR Centrifugal OR Design) AND ((“TAH” OR “Total Artificial Heart” OR “Artificial Heart” OR “VAD” OR “Ventricular Assist Device” OR “Mechanical Circulatory Support” OR “MCS”) AND (Testing OR Study OR Development OR Update OR Preliminary OR Progress OR New)) NOT Review NOT Commentary NOT Opinion NOT Case Study NOT (Case AND Long-term) NOT (Case AND Long Term) NOT (Case AND Year) NOT (Case AND Established) NOT (Case AND Program) NOT (Outcome AND Long-term) NOT (Outcome AND Long Term) NOT (Outcome AND Established) NOT “Abstracts From””

The search criteria were chosen to concentrate on commercially available designs and new devices in the development pipeline. Database search results were then screened by titles and abstracts to remove sources that could be objectively identifiable as beyond the scope of the review. 764 of the 988 papers were removed from the first screening, leaving 224 (22.6%) remaining. This screening was performed in each database individually, to ensure search independence.

The remaining articles were then reviewed for relevance and quality against inclusion criteria. The criteria were: the article must (1) focus on pediatric MCS devices, or mention use in pediatric patients; (2) contain quantifiable test metrics, from simulation or testing; (3) discuss the benefits and drawbacks of the design; and (4) be published in a peer-reviewed, English-language journal. Articles that did not meet these criteria were removed, and each database was checked against the others to find and remove duplicates. The remaining 109 articles were reviewed in their entirety to identify the articles most focused on the topic and remove articles that met the inclusion criteria but did not provide duplicate information. 39 articles remained after this final screening.

This process was supplemented with a review of devices approved for pediatric use by the Pediatric Interagency Registry for Mechanical Circulatory Support (PediMACS) database. In certain cases, the literature from the systematic review did not contain enough operational data; thus, additional device specifications were found through FDA documents or supplemental published sources.

Pediatric Mechanical Circulatory Support

Pediatric MCS has seen continual growth in implementation and development, from implantable devices to percutaneous pumps. This review revealed several pediatric-centric design challenges: pulsatile vs. continuous blood flow; acute vs. chronic support; anatomical fit; pediatric blood reactivity; dynamic pressure and flow requirements due to growth and development; and “off-design” usage, as discussed here:

  • Pulsatile vs. Continuous Flow: The latest clinical findings for adults indicate that chronic continuous blood flow conditions with diminished pulse pressure could result in deleterious side effects and adverse impacts to outcomes.[14] In contrast, conditions of continuous blood flow are tolerated for short-term or acute MCS durations, and may even provide superior performance over pulsatile devices.[14] It is currently not well understood how pediatric patients tolerate chronic MCS, in either pulsatile and continuous flow scenarios. Therefore, the development of pediatric VADs that are able to generate continuous and pulsatile flows would be advantageous.[15,16]

  • Acute vs. Chronic Support Needs: Of the cases entered into PediMACS, 19% of patients were supported with short-term or acute devices, which makes acute support an important aspect of pediatric MCS.[17] These have demonstrated improved patient outcomes over extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), which has led to increases in adoption rates; median support time for short-term devices has risen to 19 days.[17,18] Certain short-term devices have been employed as long-term devices with favorable outcomes, but limit mobility and extend hospital stays.[18] Device designs which can operate safely and effectively in both acute and chronic care scenarios are ideal.

  • Anatomical Fit: Pediatric patients have a range of requirements for anatomical fit and cannula connections. Newborn patients on average have 1/10 of the body surface area (BSA) of a young adult, and BSA rises dramatically as the infant grows and develops (Figure 2).[19] Therefore, the anatomical fit of a pediatric MCS device is challenging for neonates and infants. Paracorporeal devices must also contend with heterogeneity in patient anatomy which affects cannula connections. For example, the Berlin Heart EXCOR has 3, 6, 9, and 12 mm pediatric outlet cannulae sizes.[20] The rapid increase of BSA with age means that devices that accommodate younger patients should also accommodate older patents with regards to device size.

  • Dynamic Flow and Pressure Requirements: Pediatric patients also have a rapid increase in capacity and pressure requirements during growth.[20] The cardiac output for a 50th percentile pediatric patient doubles from birth to age 1 and doubles again by age 10 (Figure 2).[20] While accommodating fit, the size of the device generally impacts the ability of the pump to produce a wide range of pressure and flow capacities with acceptable fluid shear stresses.[21] This provides a second design-based challenge since device size, and thus, anatomical fit, is often at odds with pressure and capacity generation. It may be necessary in long-term care scenarios to bridge an infant to another MCS device to accommodate growth, introducing further potential for complications.[21] Design versatility could be integrated such that multiple pumps could be used to widen the pressure and capacity potential in a single MCS device.

  • Pediatric Blood Reactivity: Pediatric patients also have distinct differences in blood rheology and behavior.[22,23] Hematocrit, which affects blood viscosity, is highest in neonates and lowers rapidly to a steady level as the infant reaches adolescence.[22] Congenital or acquired pediatric heart disease also impacts hematocrit levels, and blood rheology impacts fluid dynamics, particularly in low-shear scenarios.[22–24] Adult MCS devices are not tuned for pediatric blood, and further investigation is needed to elucidate the impact of blood rheology on pediatric pump performance.[22]

  • “Off-Design” Device Usage: The aforementioned challenges in pediatric device design, combined with the reduced availability of targeted pediatric treatment options, have led to the “off-design” usage of adult devices in children, or the usage of a blood pump outside the device’s optimal operating window. As pediatric patients exhibit different hemodynamics and have lower required pressure and flow requirements compared to adult patients,[25–27] studies show that “off-design” usage in the pediatric population negatively affects outcomes; higher frequency of irregular flow caused by “off-design” usage correlates to an increased incidence of hemolysis and thrombosis.[28,29] A direct study of the Berlin Heart EXCOR against the HeartMate II in vitro demonstrated reduced hemocompatibility.[25,29]

These design considerations underscore the unique challenges of developing pediatric MCS technology, the importance of effective device sizing and achieving pediatric design requirements.

Device Evaluation

Pediatric MCS devices were evaluated in light of these design challenges, split into five categories: extracorporeal pumping circuits, VADs, TAHs, percutaneous blood pumps, and disease-or-patient- specific devices for congenital heart defects. Here, the design attributes of each are discussed. Figure 4 illustrates several of these devices.

Extracorporeal Pumping Circuits

The earliest technology used for pediatric MCS, which still sees usage today, is ECMO.[30,31] The ECMO circuit connects to the patient by either veno-arterial cannulation (via the femoral artery and vein) or veno-venous cannulation (via the right atrium or jugular vein and the femoral artery). ECMO devices circulate and oxygenate the blood, replacing the function of both the heart and the lungs (if necessary). It is intended for short-term support, requires immobilization and often sedation, and is known to produce adverse events, such as thromboemboli.[30,31] When ECMO was compared to pediatric VADs, the study revealed an improvement in outcomes for those who received a pediatric VAD. This also revealed a lowering of waitlist mortality from 38% (ECMO) to 13% (VAD) and an improvement of post-transplant survival from 80% to 92% for pediatric patients receiving a VAD over ECMO support.[31]

For cardiac assistance only, extracorporeal and paracorporeal devices, such as the Abbott Laboratories PediMag (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL, USA), Jostra RotaFlow (Maquet, Rastatt, Germany), and TandemHeart (CardiacAssist, Inc., Pittsburgh, PA, USA), have been employed for short-term MCS without an oxygenator as extracorporeal VADs. These compare favorably to ECMO in short-term care scenarios.[31] Since such devices rest outside of the body, anatomical fit considerations are less of a challenge, barring cannulation considerations, and the implementation of extracorporeal devices is characterized as less invasive.[32] Additionally, transitioning a patient to another device is generally less complex with extracorporeal / paracorporeal devices.[32] In long-term care scenarios, however, extracorporeal and paracorporeal MCS devices face challenges. These designs hamper patient mobility, increase thromboembolic events, heighten neurological risks compared to implantable designs, and limit hospital discharge due to the complexity of home care.[5,33]

Ventricular Assist Devices

In contrast to ECMO, VADs solely supplement the cardiac output, assisting the left ventricle (LV) or right ventricle (RV). Early clinical pediatric VADs were adult devices implemented in children; the first usage of a pediatric VAD was in 1991.[5] In 2004, the NIH National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) established the Pediatric Circulatory Support Program, a predecessor to the recent PumpKIN (Pump for Kids, Infants, and Neonates) program. These programs supported the development and translation of implantable pediatric devices.[34] Phase I designs in the PumpKIN program included 1) an implantable device with mixed flow characteristics, intended for patients up to 2 years old (PediaFlow VAD); 2) an implantable, mixed-flow VAD that can be maintained either intravascularly or extravascularly, depending upon patient age and size (PediPump); 3) a pediatric cardiopulmonary assist system (pCAS), 4) an axial flow VAD to be implanted apically (Infant Jarvik); and 5) a pulsatile-flow PVAD.[35–37] Leveraging knowledge gains, Phase II of the PumpKIN program continued support of three devices (PediaFlow, pCAS, and the infant Jarvik) and an ECMO system known as PediPL.[37] The infant Jarvik VAD continued to receive funding into animal studies, and began clinical trials in 2012.[37]

These programs sparked awareness and stimulated innovation to address the shortfalls of pediatric MCS devices. The field rose to the challenge. Some devices have achieved clinical approval for pediatric usage, and several are in various development stages, including clinical trials. These designs, their advantages, and their limitations are discussed next.

Clinically Approved Pediatric VADs

An FDA-approved pediatric blood pump that has been employed with frequency is the Berlin Heart EXCOR (Berlin Heart, Berlin, Germany) (Figure 4A), a paracorporeal device that has been clinically available since the early 1990s,[38] and received FDA approval for pediatric use in 2011.[39] The EXCOR is a displacement pump with a flexible diaphragm having chamber volumes ranging from 10 mL to 80 mL and capacity ranges from 0.4 to 5 L/min; a 15 mL pump was developed to address an identified gap in capacity options.[38] All provide sufficient pressure rise for pediatric support.[40] While multiple options allow for better device-patient matching, the operating range of each pump is narrow (Figure 3). A retrospective study of 80 children found that 31% of patients on EXCOR support were supported on a blood pump that was not optimally sized.[38] It was also shown that children supported on these oversized pumps experienced a statistically significant increase in thromboembolic events.[38] The Berlin Heart EXCOR has become a popular choice for paracorporeal VAD support, but it is subject to the drawbacks of paracorporeal devices in long-term care scenarios.

Two other VADs that have been FDA approved for pediatric patients are the implantable HeartMate III LVAD (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL, USA) (Figure 4B) which supports children and adolescents with body surface area (BSA) greater than 0.7m2 for extracorporeal support and 1.4 m2 for implantable support,[41] and the extracorporeal Abiomed BVS 5000 (Abiomed Inc., Danvers, MA). On a case-by-case basis, the Jarvik 2000 (Jarvik Heart Inc., New York, NY) and HeartMate II axial flow pump (Thoratec Laboratories Corporation, Pleasanton, CA) may be employed for pediatric compassionate use.[41] MicroMed-DeBakey Child VAD (MicroMed Technology, Houston, TX) was the first device to be granted Humanitarian Device Exemption (HDE) in 2004;[42] however, the large device size limited usage, and it had a high prevalence of thromboembolic events.[5]

The Abbott Laboratories HeartMate III received an FDA labeling update to allow usage in pediatric patients with advanced refractory LV HF in December 2020.[43] This magnetically-levitated pump demonstrated favorable outcomes in the adult population (with a 79% two-year survival rate) and gained pediatric approval for patients of a BSA above 1.2 m2 after positive results captured by the ACTION Learning Collaborative.[43]

Medtronic’s implantable HeartWare HVAD was FDA approved in 2012 for adults and has been employed in pediatrics. A retrospective study of 14 pediatric patients who were supported by the HVAD demonstrated a statistically significant rise in blood trauma and thromboemboli.[27] In June 2021, the sale of HVADs was suspended due to a high frequency of adverse neurological events.[44]

As part of the PumpKin program, Jarvik Heart designed VADs for infants and adults with potential usage in children and adolescents.[37,45] The infant Jarvik VAD could generate flows from 0.27 to 3 L/min, while the child VAD produced flow from 0.5 to 3 L/min. The infant VAD has a 10.5 mm diameter and a 52 mm length, and it is intended to support patients having BSAs of 0.22 m2 to 0.65 m2.[46,47] FDA feedback prompted development of the current Jarvik 2015 infant VAD, which is intended to fit in patients from 2 to 25 kg (0.2–1 m2).[37,48–50] The device received FDA approval for a clinical trial in 2012,[45] but was recalled from late 2018 until May of 2020, due to an issue with connectors on external cables.[51] The VAD has not yet received device approval.[51] Meanwhile, the PediaFlow device (HeartWare International, Framingham, MA) (Figure 4C) continued independent development after the PumpKin program despite not achieving FDA approval; the most recently published 4th generation iteration of the device is a compact design (17 mm in diameter by 50 mm in length) which can provide support for infants as low as 3 kg and flows as low as 0.5 L/min.[52]

Pediatric VADs under Development

Several VADs were identified in different phases of development and are highlighted here:

  • DeltaStream DP3: The DeltaStream DP3 (Xenios AG., Heilbronn, Germany) is a diagonal pump, a blend of an axial and centrifugal blood pump. This extracorporeal device easily exceeds necessary pressure generation (240 mmHg of systemic pressure rise) and flows up to 8 L/min. In vitro testing demonstrated the pump works in patients having a BSA from 0.18 to 0.61 m2.[53]

  • DON-3: This axial pediatric VAD, developed by the Russian Federation Ministry of Healthcare, has been tested in vitro and in sheep studies. It is able to provide up to 135 mmHg of pressure rise with a blood flow range of 0.5 to 4 L/min. The design is relatively compact, with a 25 mm diameter and a length of 60 mm. Results of animal tests, with a wide range of subjects corresponding to pediatric patients ages 3 to 10 (0.62 to 1.1 m2) show promise.[54]

  • Drexel Dragon 1S and 1P: Drexel University is developing hybrid, continuous-flow VADs that combine a magnetically levitated axial pump with a centrifugal pump to increase the device operating window.[28] There are two design concepts: the series design places the axial pump in front of the centrifugal pump while the parallel concept splits the axial pump and centrifugal pump into two separate domains. Switching from one pump to another is feasible. Maximum systemic pressure rise in initial benchtop testing is 120 mmHg, with a blood flow range from 1–5 L/min.[28]

  • iCor VAD: The iCor pump (Xenios AG., Heilbronn, Germany) is a paracorporeal centrifugal flow VAD that can produce over 100 mmHg of pressure rise in testing, with flow between 0.2 and 1.8 L/min.[15]

  • Inspired Pediatric VAD: Inspired Therapeutics LLC (Melbourne, FL) are developing the Inspired Pediatric VAD, a paracorporeal device that utilizes magnetic levitation for its impeller to reduce friction and wear and thus lower blood cell trauma. This blood pump is able to deliver a maximum pressure rise of 150 mmHg and flow rate of 3.5 L/min and is designed for patients having a BSA as low as 0.10 m2.[55]

  • NIPRO VAD: The NIPRO pediatric VAD (NIPRO Medical Corporation, Miami, FL) is another paracorporeal pulsatile VAD. With a flow capacity range of 2–4 L/min, and a maximum pressure of 150 mmHg, the device can provide pulsatile flow with beat rates of 50–130 bpm. Blood damage levels were low during in vitro pumping trials.[56,57]

  • PSU Infant VAD: Penn State University has been developing a pump focused on smaller BSA patients with its Infant VAD, a pulsatile paracorporeal device with a pneumatically actuated valve concept that was developed from the adult Thoratec pneumatic VAD. It is a small pump with a 12–14 mL stroke volume. Having an upper flow range of 1.6 L/min, the device is able to support patients up to 10 kg (< 0.5 m2), with low blood trauma levels in animal studies.[58,59]

  • Sputnik PRBP VAD: The Sputnik PRBP VAD, created by a team of researchers at the Russian National Research University of Electronic Technology, is an implantable axial pump device that is slightly larger than other pediatric pumps, with a current diameter of 51.5 mm and intended support for patients above 12 kg (BSAs greater than 0.6 m2). The device provides a wide flow range from 1 to 5 L/min and can provide up to 160 mmHg of pressure.[60,61]

  • TORVAD (Figure 4D): The pediatric TORVAD (Windmill Cardiovascular Systems, Austin, TX) is a non-traditional pump design, which utilizes two independently controlled pistons inside a toroidal chamber. One piston serves as a “virtual” valve, remaining still while the other piston moves to provide vacuum-mechanistic pumping action. Experimental testing demonstrated a capacity range of 1 to 8 L/min, pulsatile flow capability up to 4 L/min, and up to 300 mmHg of pressure rise. This device is expected to be 30% smaller than the adult TORVAD, supporting patients with a BSA between 0.6 and 1.5 m2.[16]

Total Artificial Hearts

Compared to VADs, there are even fewer clinically approved pediatric TAHs, and there are a limited number under development. The SynCardia TAH (SynCardia Systems, Tuscon, AZ), is currently FDA approved for pediatric implementation in the US, and only six of those devices were implanted in the country between September 2012 and December 2019.[29] There is ongoing effort to develop new pediatric TAHs, as described here.

Clinically Approved Pediatric TAHs

The SynCardia TAH has 2 sizes: 50cc and 70cc.[62] Both devices have received FDA approval as BTT (70cc device in 2014, 50cc device in 2020).[63] The 50cc device is contraindicated for patients with BSA greater than 1.85 m2, while the 70cc device is contraindicated for patients having BSA less than 1.7 m2.[62] Able to provide flow up to 7.5 L/min and match physiologic pressures without difficulty, the 50cc device has seen usage in pediatric patients with 22% of implantations in patients under 21.[64] A study of device usage from 1986 to 2004 reviewed all 68 pediatric patients who received a SynCardia TAH; patient survival was 68%, and there was an increase in the number of implants each year.[64] Its relatively large size (77 × 66 mm for the LV and 75 × 80 mm for the RV) precludes its usage in smaller patients[65], and the smallest patient implanted with the SynCardia 50cc device had a BSA of 1.16 m2.[66]

Pediatric TAHs Under Development

There are TAHs under development specifically focused on the pediatric patients. These devices seek to widen the operating range for improved clinical outcomes in pediatric patients.

  • Drexel Dragon Heart TAH (Figure 4E): Drexel University is currently developing a hybrid TAH that consists of an axial pump for the pulmonary circulation (RV) surrounded by a centrifugal pump for the systemic circulation (LV).[21] This minimizes the footprint of the device while allowing separate capacity optimization for each circulation. The pumps produce blood flow rates from 1–6.5 L/min with a pressure rise range of 10–92 mmHg for the pulmonary circulation and 24–150 mmHg for the systemic circulation. The device could be used paracorporeally for smaller patients and is implantable for patients having a BSA greater than 0.75 m2.[21]

  • Rolling Heart TAH: The Rolling Heart TAH utilizes two discs rotating in a spherical cavity to produce blood flow and pressure generation.[67] At a set maximum target of 5 L/min flow, the device was able to generate 175 mmHg of systemic pressure rise. The current design uses 14 W of power, which could produce heating challenges. Identical stroke volumes could make it challenging to balance blood flow conditions. The next-iteration device size of 80 mm diameter is rather large for smaller patients.[67]

  • Cleveland Pediatric TAH (Figure 4F): Researchers at the Cleveland Clinic have multiple TAHs under development: a standard sized device for adults and adolescents, and a pediatric device, which is 1/3 the total volume of the adult device.[68,69] Both use a single motor that provides power to the systemic and pulmonary circulations, can produce pulsatile flow through pump speed modulation, and passive adaptive filling, meeting capacity demands for either circulation.[70] The pediatric pump, at a length of 70 mm and a diameter of 45 mm, can flow from 0.4–4.7 L/min with a pressure rise of 100 mmHg.[68] The port size and configuration of the pediatric pump also remains identical to the adult pump. Used either paracorporeally or in vivo, the pediatric device was developed to support patients from 0.3 m2 (greater than or equal to 1 m2 for the standard device) up to 50 kg (BSA of about 1.5 m2).[70]

Percutaneous Blood Pumps

In contrast to more invasive devices such as VADs or TAHs, percutaneous blood pumps are considered to be less invasive. Device deployment is similar to a cardiovascular stent, utilizing vascular access. The AbioMed Impella (Abiomed Inc., Danvers, MA), as an example, operates with a shrouded rotating impeller, commonly used in short-term MCS support.[71] Other percutaneous devices that have seen pediatric implementation are intra-aortic balloon pumps (IABP). Rather than utilizing a spinning impeller, synchronized inflation and deflation of a balloon in the aorta augments ventricular pumping. Synchronizing balloon pumping to the high pediatric heart rates, however, is challenging; thus, IABPs are not used in younger children.[5] Mortality rates during IABP in pediatric patients have been reported as being greater than 35%, and IABP support has the lowest long-term pediatric survival statistics.[72]

Percutaneous Pumps in Use

Percutaneous blood pumps for emergency cardiac support, such as the Impella device, have been employed to support pediatric patients. All sizes of the Impella, which are inserted percutaneously across the aortic valve, (i.e. Impeller 2.5, 5.0, CP, and LD) were granted HDE for adults and children in February of 2015.[73] A retrospective study investigating 39 pediatric implementations of the four different Impella devices from 2009–2015 found the majority of patients supported with the 2.5 and CP sizes, a 90% survival rate in the patient pool, and a successful rate of explant to ventricular recovery at almost 40%.[71] The Impella is only intended for short-term MCS (approximately 6 days), which limits its prospects as a long-term therapy for pediatric patients during growth and development.[71] The TandemHeart (CardiacAssist, Inc., Pittsburgh, PA) has also been used as a percutaneous MCS device for pediatric patients; however, percutaneous support requires piercing the atrial septum and patient movement can affect device placement. Thus, this device is less frequently used as compared to the Impella.[74]

Disease-and Patient-Specific Innovation for Congenital Heart Disease

A new category of MCS devices (mechanical cavopulmonary assistance) has emerged in the last decade; these devices are being developed for pediatric patients with severe and complex heart defects, such as single functional ventricle.[75] Surgical modifications are performed in a series of separate open-heart procedures, and the result is a non-native palliative (Fontan) physiology. Here, the single ventricle drives blood flow through the systemic and pulmonary circulations without a subpulmonary power source.[75] This significant alteration of venous return contributes to early and late pathophysiology.[75,76] Over time, these patients will develop premature congestive HF. Thus, blood pumps are being developed to boost pressure and flow in the inferior and superior vena cava, cavopulmonary surgical connection, or in various locations in the pulmonary arteries.[77–79].

Implantable Assist Devices for Congenital Heart Disease

  • Penn State Long-Term Fontan Blood Pump: Researchers at Penn State University have developed a new implantable axial Fontan pump placed between the inferior and superior vena cava. With a maximum flow rate of 9 L/min, and a maximum pressure rise of 80 mmHg, this pump is more than sufficient to mechanically support the Fontan circulation. In vivo animal tests generated power readings within acceptable values (less than 4.8 W), and blood damage metrics were also acceptable.[80]

  • Double Inlet-Double Outlet Fontan Pump (Figure 4G): Researchers have been developing a Fontan pump with two inlets and two outlets that is implanted at the junction of the vena cavae and the pulmonary artery. The pump accepts flow from both the superior and inferior vena cava and drives flow into the pulmonary arteries. The compact pump (30 mm in diameter × 19 mm in length) has a robust flow window between 2–10 L/min at a maximum of 50 mmHg pressure rise.[79]

  • VentriFlo TPP: The VentriFlo True Pulse Pump (TPP) (Ventriflo Inc., Pelham, NH) is a paracorporeal pulsatile device used in experimental testing to supplement the Fontan circulation. The connection to the Fontan circulation is through cannulae and the pump remains outside the body. By placing the device in “series” with the circulation (by complete restriction of the Fontan baffle), the study was able to produce a maximum flow of 2.25 L/min at a maximum pressure rise of 28 mmHg.[81]

Percutaneous Assist Devices for Congenital Heart Disease

  • Drexel UniPCAD: This catheter-based blood pump is placed into the vena cava, deployed similar to a stent, and the rotating axial impeller is supported in the vessel by a cage of filaments. The pump was able to produce a maximum pressure rise of 28 mmHg in simulation, and flow from 1 to 4 L/min.[76]

  • Viscous Impeller Pump: The catheter-based viscous impeller pump (or VIP) is a biconical pump that is initially contracted on a catheter which is inserted percutaneously into the cavopulmonary connection. Once in place the device expands, allowing for minimally invasive device placement. Computational and in vitro testing demonstrated that the pump could provide up to 4.1 L/min of flow at a maximum of 38 mmHg in pressure rise. A hemolysis index of 0.07% was well within acceptable levels. This unique minimally invasive design should allow for the VIP to be usable in infants and neonates.[77]

Discussion of Findings

The findings of this review demonstrate that the pediatric MCS field has markedly advanced. Continued innovation in pediatric MCS shows new therapeutic options inching closer toward clinical translation. Approximately 27 devices were studied in this review: 8 were established or previously approved designs, and 19 were under development (11 VADs, 5 Fontan assist devices, and 3 TAHs). Design constraints for pediatric MCS, such as target size, are generally determined based on target support duration (acute or chronic) and clinical goals of extracorporeal or implantable MCS. Cannulae connector selection is driven by options and anatomy. Table 2 captures target benchmark performance expectations for a pediatric MCS device.[82–84] Using these review data, we assessed how well these pumps satisfied the pressure-flow requirements for pediatric patients. Figure 5 illustrates the ranges of pressure and flow generation for each pediatric VAD. As seen here, most devices address the pressure rise requirement well (Figure 5A), but there is a performance gap when the reported flow capacity ranges (Figure 5B) are considered. Factors, such as rotational speed, may be modulated to produce higher flows, but it is likely that these changes would move the device to “off-design” operational states, posing physiologic risks (hemolysis and thrombosis). This evaluation establishes that there is not yet a pediatric pump that satisfies the distinct design constraints and requirements due to the wide range of patient sizes, increased cardiovascular demand with growth, and anatomic and physiologic heterogeneity of congenital heart disease.[83,84] Per this review, Throckmorton et al.[82] recommend potential design innovations that could be integrated into new pediatric devices and thus address these design shortcomings in which we have expanded in detail:

  1. Personalized Disease-Specific Pediatric Design: The landscape of research and development in pediatric MCS has sparked the use of medical imaging data to virtually evaluate device fit, device design, component placement, implantation strategy, and cannula sizing / design and connection. This approach, in conjunction with the revolutionary benefits of 3D printing or additive/advanced manufacturing technology, has shortened the innovation development cycle of design-build-test-translate, while providing the means for a human-centric and compassionate design approach. We have the means now to innovate and develop devices specifically for pediatric patients and their pathologies, and we have the ability to readily employ science and engineering to inform both device design and clinical decision-making in the treatment of pediatric patients with heart disease.

  2. Adaptive Design Components: Conventional rotary pump designs are generally rigid devices with fixed-bladed impellers that generate streamlined blood flow at a singular design operating point. Components and blades are not adaptive, except in rotational speed. Fixed bladed pumps are known to suffer from diminished flow control at off-design operating conditions; blood flow in the pediatric cardiovascular system is dynamic and transient such that blood pumps would not operate on-design for most of the cardiac cycle, which increases risk for hemolysis and thrombosis. Blood flow control may be improved by implementing controlled pitch-adjusting blade characteristics into the impeller designs (i.e. wind turbines, sail boats). Dynamic adjustment of blade pitch during patient support could reduce irregular blood flows and enhance pressure generation during off-design operation. Novel pediatric device designs are also emerging, which feature innovations that incorporate unique mechanisms of action, such as flexing membranes and microfluidic based pumping mechanisms.[85]

  3. Power Drive Systems, Wearables & Wireless Technology: Design innovation in AC and DC power drive systems will continue to enable pediatric pump technology to reduce size and power consumption. The realization of new motor-magnetic bearing drive systems allows bearings to have dual function in both motor dynamics and rotor stability / support. Hybrid or combined motor-magnetic bearings continue to push the boundaries of innovation, and hydrodynamic and ceramic bearings are also becoming increasingly viable options for blood pumps. These bearings reduce wear and tear, thus extending operational lifespans of the devices, and afford wider clearances between rotating and stationary surfaces, thereby mitigating fluid stresses and lowering risks of thrombosis and hemolysis.[86,87] The field of materials science and engineering continues to innovate light and more portable integrative materials into wearable components (i.e. controller, battery pack, support holster). Wearable electronic devices could be integrated into pediatric pump components to detect, analyze and transmit vital signs and activity data for improved clinical management. Moreover, wireless power systems using coplanar inductive coupling energy systems or high efficiency resonant coupling circuits via magnetic fields could eliminate abdominal drivelines.[88,89]

  4. Contact Surface Coatings and Modifications: The reactivity of pediatric blood cells to fluid stresses in blood pumps is still largely unknown. It is understood that hematocrit can vary among pediatric patients with cardiovascular disease states, and thus blood viscosity and cell volume could also range significantly. Innovation in biocoatings, biomaterials and surface modifications could improve hemocompatibility. Recent efforts have considered superhydrophobic surface characteristics and roughness surface modifications based on aerospace innovations to encourage cellular slippage along walls. Biocoatings with drug eluting and immune-engineered properties are newly emerging options. Alternative strategies for surface modifications include biomimetic surfaces (including heparin, nitric oxide, and thrombin inhibitors), biopassive surfaces with phosphorylcholine or albumin, and endothelialization of the blood contacting surface.[90]

  5. Configuration and Operational Versatility: To provide versatility in use, pediatric devices could be configured to house multiple blood pumps in series or parallel. These pumps could be activated at different times during the growth cycle in order to appropriately boost pressure and flow capacity for the child or young adult.[28] Since the necessity of pulsatile blood flow or the implications of chronic continuous flow MCS in pediatric patients is not well understood, devices having the ability to deliver both pulsatile and steady flow are desirable.[20,72] New control algorithms can be incorporated to modulate the rotational speed of pediatric blood pumps and thus produce continuous or pulsatile pressure and flow conditions.[15,29]A pediatric MCS device with multiuse capabilities (i.e. function as an RVAD, LVAD, and TAH in both implantable and paracorporeal support cases) would provide multi-dimensional treatment strategies for pediatric patients facing a range of cardiovascular challenges.[82]

While progress has been made to develop pediatric MCS technology, more research is required to inform the broader scientific and medical community and to inspire innovation of technology and medical devices for these patients. As a community, we recognize the importance of this mission, promote its awareness, and are committed to this effort to innovate, improve, and deliver quality patient care to our children.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this systematic review demonstrates that the field of pediatric MCS has progressed in the last 10 years and that the landscape of the field’s emerging innovation is promising with new therapeutic options coming to clinical realization. 27 devices were studied: 8 were established or previously approved designs, and 19 were under development (11 VADs, 5 Fontan assist devices, and 3 TAHs). Despite significant progress, there is still no pediatric pump technology that satisfies the distinct design constraints and requirements to support pediatric patients across the entire age range, and the anatomic and physiologic heterogeneity of congenital heart disease. We must continue to persevere for the thousands of children who are in desperate need of safe and effective therapeutic alternatives.
