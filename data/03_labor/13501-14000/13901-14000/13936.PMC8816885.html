A Systematic Review of Behavioral Couples-Based Interventions Targeting Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission in Low- and Middle-Income Countries

Abstract

There is increasing focus in HIV prevention and treatment on couples-based approaches. No systematic review has synthesized prospective behavioral couples-based HIV trials targeting prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) outcomes in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). We systematically reviewed published abstracts and articles reporting prospective comparative evaluations of behavioral couples-based HIV interventions delivered during pregnancy to both members of a self-identified heterosexual couple in LMICs following PRISMA. Citations, abstracts, and full texts were double screened for eligibility. References meeting eligibility criteria underwent double data abstraction, quality appraisal, and qualitative synthesis. We identified 295 unique publications. Of these, 5 randomized trials were deemed eligible and synthesized. Studies were conducted in 3 different African countries using three overarching intervention approaches: home-based; group workshops; and faith-based. Studies included various PMTCT outcome measures. We found evidence that behavioral couples-based approaches around the time of pregnancy can positively affect HIV testing among pregnant women and their male partners, infant HIV prophylaxis use, and HIV-free infant survival. The effects on other PMTCT outcomes were not well supported. There was a low to moderate risk of bias among the included studies. Few couples-based PMTCT interventions have been tested in LMICs. Of the interventions we located, workshops/group education and home-based couple counseling and testing were most commonly used to promote PMTCT. Research is needed on the role of relationship dynamics within such interventions and whether couples-based approaches during pregnancy can extend to health outcomes across the PMTCT continuum of care.

Introduction

Pregnancy is a critical time period for HIV diagnosis, care, and treatment among women living with HIV (WLWH) [1-3]. The prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) continuum of care refers to the essential steps needed to promote optimal maternal health among WLWH and their children during the pregnancy and postpartum time periods. The care continuum includes the following critical steps: HIV testing and diagnosis during pregnancy; uptake and adherence to lifelong antiretroviral therapy (ART); engagement in HIV care; facility delivery; safe infant feeding practices; infant HIV prophylaxis; and infant HIV testing and diagnosis [4].

In low resource high HIV prevalence settings, such as in sub-Saharan Africa, prenatal care is often when WLWH receive their HIV diagnosis, followed by the initiation of lifelong ART through integrated antenatal and HIV programs [5]. Unfortunately, although uptake is high, the risk of attrition and poor adherence increases over time placing WLWH and their infants at elevated risk for adverse health outcomes, including maternal disease progression and mortality, and infant HIV infection [6, 7]. Estimates across sub-Saharan Africa suggest that one in four women initiating ART during pregnancy are lost to follow-up within six months [8]. In eastern Africa, almost 40% of new HIV infections among children in 2018 were attributable to WLWH dropping out of HIV care during pregnancy/postpartum [9].

HIV prevention, care, and treatment services have often focused on the individual. Yet, there is increasing evidence that in order to maximize the benefits of PMTCT, inclusion of male partners in the form of couples-focused services may be required [10]. As such, the World Health Organization (WHO) has called for a renewed emphasis on couples-based interventions to improve HIV outcomes among pregnant WLWH and promote PMTCT [11].

Couples-based HIV approaches purport that partners within a couple influence each other’s health behaviors and that by targeting the couple as a unit, interventions can make a greater sustained positive impact on health behavior [12]. Numerous studies suggest, for instance, that male partner involvement improves certain PMTCT outcomes, including maternal ART uptake, decreased infant HIV infection, and increased HIV-free infant survival [13, 14]. Couple HIV testing and counseling (CHTC) has also become a widely utilized approach across various settings, which increases the uptake of HIV testing and mutual status disclosure in heterosexual relationships [15-18]. Further, psychosocial interventions that provide education and counseling to couples can improve relationship skills, such as communication and problem-solving [19], and lead to improved sexual and reproductive health behaviors like condom use and CHTC [15, 20-23].

Despite recent enthusiasm for couples within HIV prevention and treatment efforts [13, 24], to our knowledge, no systematic review has synthesized prospective comparative evaluations of behavioral couples-based HIV interventions delivered during pregnancy to both members of a self-identified heterosexual couple in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Such a review is needed to understand what types of couples-based approaches have been tested in what settings, and whether such interventions are effective at promoting specific PMTCT-related health behaviors and outcomes.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review of trials reporting prospective comparisons of behavioral couples-based HIV interventions to both members of a self-identified couple during pregnancy. Our aim was to assess the core components of couples-based HIV interventions delivered to pregnant women and their male partners, including the targeted behaviors and couple-level factors, outcomes, and mode and location of delivery. For this study, a couple was defined as any heterosexual dyad (of any length of time) and was based on the original references’ definition, including married partners/spouses, cohabitating partners, intimate partners, sexual partners, or dating partners. Our methods for the systematic review follow the recommended PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) reporting guidelines [25].

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion in this review if they met the following criteria: (1) evaluated a couples-based behavioral intervention that delivered counseling and/or education content to both members of a self-identified couple; (2) targeted pregnant women and their male partners (i.e., heterosexual dyads); (3) included pregnant WLWH and reported on a behavioral or biomedical HIV care or treatment outcome related to PMTCT (e.g., HIV testing, HIV medication uptake, ART adherence, safe infant feeding, facility delivery, infant HIV testing, infant diagnosis, maternal viral load or CD4 count or other biomarker of treatment adherence/disease progression); (4) had a comparison group that did not receive the intervention; (5) took place within a LMIC based on World Bank classification [26]; and (6) published in English language. We did not restrict the date of publication given that couples-based HIV interventions are a relatively new approach among behavioral HIV interventionists. The decision to focus solely on LMICs was made in order to limit the scope of this review to low resource settings, which have unique challenges and have been neglected in prior couple-based HIV reviews [27]. Both peer-reviewed published articles and scientific conference abstracts were included.

Search Strategy

On May 16, 2019, a content expert librarian searched five electronic databases (Ovid MEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, and Cochrane Library) using a combination of the following search terms: (couple* or partner*) and (therap* or counselling or center* or focus* or based*) and (HIV or Human immunodeficiency virus" or AIDS or "acquired immune deficiency syndrome") and (Pregnan* or prenatal or antenatal or Pregnancy or Prenatal Care). We additionally hand searched the reference lists of included articles to capture additional references not included in the original electronic database search.

Data Screening, Abstraction and Analysis

First, study titles and abstracts were independently double screened by two of the authors (KH, KP) for initial eligibility criteria, followed by double screening of the full texts, comparing results and resolving differences through discussion and consensus. The final inclusion of studies was based on consensus among all coauthors. Publications meeting our screening eligibility criteria underwent double data abstraction by two authors (KH, KP) and qualitative synthesis. Qualitative synthesis took place by two trained study members (KH, KP) working independently in Atlas. ti (Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin) using a structured a priori codebook. The following data were extracted and coded: author names, study setting, aim of study, study design, years of data collection, year of publication, sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria, description of participants (population from which participants are drawn, setting of recruitment), intervention group and key intervention components, comparison group, primary outcome(s), key findings, and limitations identified by the primary authors. Results were compared between coders and any differences in coding were discussed until consensus was achieved. Given the heterogeneity of studies and multiple reported outcomes, a meta-analysis was not conducted. Finally, two authors independently conducted a quality appraisal using the Version 2 of The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing risk of bias in randomized trials (RoB 2) [28], coming to consensus after resolving any differences in quality ratings through discussion.

Results

Key Features of Included Studies

Figure 1 displays the PRISMA flow of information chart for our systematic literature search. Our search strategy identified 302 publications with an additional four publications added from other sources (article reference lists). After duplicates were removed, 295 unique publications had titles screened, after which 107 records had abstracts screened, leaving 44 publications for the full-text review. Of these, 39 publications were deemed ineligible, leaving a final 5 unique studies for the data abstraction and qualitative synthesis. The most common reasons for exclusion during full-text review were lack of a control group (n = 20); no PMTCT-specific outcome (n = 9); and intervention content that was delivered to only one partner (e.g., invitation letters for a male partner to attend HIV testing and counseling without any component aimed at the couple together; n = 8).

Table 1 displays the key characteristics and outcomes of the final 5 included studies. Studies represented three different sub-Saharan African countries: Nigeria (n = 1), Kenya (n = 2), and South Africa (n = 2). Four of the studies included pregnant women regardless of HIV status [15, 29-31], while one study included only pregnant WLWH [32]. Sample sizes ranged from 127 pregnant women to 3,002 pregnant women recruited for participation with their male partners, with a median sample size of 549 pregnant women.

Home-Based Approaches

Two studies in Kenya tested home-based interventions that offered CHTC along with health education [15, 30]. Krakowiak et al. (2016) provided one scheduled home visit in which lay health workers offered CHTC to adult (≥ 18 years) pregnant women and their male partners along with health education, including the importance of facility delivery, exclusive breastfeeding, family planning, and HIV prevention [30]. The intervention restricted inclusion to married/cohabiting couples. No conceptual/theoretical framework for the intervention was cited. Participants included both HIV-positive (n = 117) and HIV-negative (n = 469) pregnant women and their male partners. Pregnant women were tested for HIV during antenatal care and then retested with their male partners during the home visit. Although the intervention had a positive effect on male HIV testing behavior and status disclosure (see Table 1), PMTCT and maternal and child health outcomes (facility delivery, postpartum family planning, and exclusive breastfeeding) did not vary by study arm. The authors attributed the null findings to a high prevalence of these behaviors in both groups with little room for improvement.

Turan et al. (2018) also used home-visits by lay health workers to deliver the Jamii Bora (‘‘better family’’ in Swahili) intervention [15]. Health workers visited adult pregnant women and their male partners in their homes three times (twice during pregnancy, once postpartum). The study included both HIV-negative (n = 63) and HIV-positive (n = 64) pregnant women and their male partners. Women were only eligible if they had not yet disclosed their HIV status to the male partner. Enrolled couples were required to be in a stable relationship and living together. The intervention included health education, couple relationship skills building exercises, offering CHTC, and linkage to facility-based HIV services. The study was based on the Interdependence Model of Health Behavior Change [12]. Health messages in the Jamii Bora intervention were specific to the stage of pregnancy/postpartum. Each visit also included a couple relationship skills building exercise, such as listening, communication, and negotiation skills. Couples were offered CHTC at any of the three visits. Compared to women in the control group, women in the intervention group were more likely to receive a maternal postpartum checkup (50% and 72%, respectively; RR 1.43; 95% CI 1.04–1.98) and to have undergone CHTC with their male partners (23% and 64%, respectively; RR 2.78; 95% CI 1.63–4.75). There was also a positive trend (marginally significant) towards more exclusive breastfeeding in the intervention group (91%) at three months postpartum compared to the control group (76%; p = 0.058). Giving birth in a health facility, infant checkups, and postpartum family planning did not vary by study arm.

Group Workshops

Two studies in South Africa recruited adult couples to participate in workshops (with some additional components) in an effort to promote PMTCT [31, 32]. Weiss et al. (2014) tested “The PartnerPlus” intervention to improve infant HIV prophylaxis adherence, which was based on adaptations from prior couples behavioral HIV interventions [33, 34]. Participants included both HIV-positive (n = 82) and HIV-negative (n = 157) pregnant women and their male partners. Couples in the intervention arm received four successive weekly gender-concordant group sessions led by trained gender-matched facilitators. Although the groups were gender-concordant, participants were given “homework” to work on as a couple outside of the group sessions and report back. The intervention was guided by the theories of Reasoned Action, Planned Behavior, and the Information-Motivation Behavioral Skills Model and used a cognitive–behavioral skill training approach. Intervention content covered during the group sessions included interpersonal and sexual communication/negotiation, conflict resolution, IPV and conflict, relaxation techniques, HIV medication adherence, HIV and PMTCT knowledge, male and female condom use, and HIV serostatus disclosure. Group strategies aimed to create a safe environment for sharing personal experiences, role-playing negotiation, problem solving and communication skills, and hands-on experiential training with condoms. The study found that infant medication dosing (i.e., self-reported percentage of HIV prophylaxis provided by mothers to their infants) was higher among women receiving the Partner Plus intervention (74%) compared to the control group (46%; χ2 = 4.69, p = 0.03). In addition, participants who received the intervention also had increased knowledge related to HIV and PMTCT (F1,474 = 13.9, p = 0.004). In a sub-analysis, antiretroviral medication detected through dry blood spots (DBS) was higher in the mothers in the intervention group (75%; n = 9) compared to mothers in the control group (50%; n = 6) but this was not statistically significant (p = 0.19). Similar findings were reported in the infants born to mothers in the intervention group (92% detectable ART; n = 12) compared to infants born to mothers in the control group (75%; n = 9) (p = 0.32).

In a two-phase RCT, Sifunda et al. (2019) tested the ‘Protect Your Family’ intervention, which included three prenatal weekly gender-concordant group sessions and one prenatal couple counselling session (or individual counseling session if the male partner was not present), along with two postpartum couple or individual counselling sessions [32]. During the sessions, women who had not disclosed their HIV status to male partners were invited to (1) disclose their status to their partner during a one-on-one couple session, or (2) not have a male partner attend the one-on-one session. This was the only study exclusively focused on pregnant WLWH (n = 1399). The intervention was a cognitive-behaviorally focused, manualized, closed, structured behavioral risk-reduction program. Intervention content covered prevention of vertical HIV transmission, adherence to PMTCT and medication use, HIV testing of family members, prevention of HIV transmission and stigma, HIV serostatus disclosure, partner communication, IPV, safe infant feeding, safer conception, family planning and dual method sexual barrier use. During the group sessions, participants received cognitive behavioral skills training, role played communication strategies, and completed partner communication and negotiation homework. Women’s and men’s group sessions were comparable but placed slightly different emphasis on gender-relevant topics. The couple/individual counselling sessions focused on enhancing motivation to adhere to PMTCT, as well as reduce unintended pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections (STI), and the use of dual sexual protection methods. At 12-months postpartum, women in the control arm (Phase 1) had significantly more HIV-infected infants than Phase 2 women who had received the ‘Protect Your Family’ intervention (specific proportions not reported; aOR 4.55; 95% CI 1.38—15.07). Phase 1 also had significantly higher rates of infant mortality and HIV infections than Phase 2 at 12-months postpartum (specific proportions not reported; aOR 1.98; 95% CI 1.33—2.94).

Faith-Based Community Approaches

In a cluster randomized trial, Ezeanolue et al. (2015) employed a community-based education and HIV testing intervention in Nigeria, the “Healthy Baby Initiative.” This intervention aimed to increase HIV testing among adult pregnant women and ART uptake among those diagnosed with HIV [29]. The study included pregnant women with any HIV serostatus (HIV-positive, HIV-negative, and unknown) and their male partners. No conceptual/theoretical framework for the study was cited. The “Healthy Baby Initiative” delivered health education to pregnant women and their male partners with on-site HIV testing at Christian churches. Both the intervention group and control group participated in a church-organized “baby shower” during pregnancy and a “baby reception” postpartum. The events included gifts and refreshments, as well as health education in the form of an educational game show (The Game of Love), which provided information about healthy pregnancy habits, important laboratory tests during pregnancy, HIV acquisition modes, and effective PMTCT interventions. The intervention group was additionally offered on-site HIV testing at the church with other on-site laboratory testing (hemoglobin, malaria, syphilis, sickle cell genotype, hepatitis B). The control group was not offered on-site HIV testing at the church but were given a referral to a health facility. The study found that a greater proportion of pregnant women tested for HIV in the intervention group (92%) compared to the control group (55%; aOR 11.2; 95% CI 8.77–14.25). A greater proportion of women were also referred to HIV care prior to delivery in the intervention (83%) compared to the control group (44%; OR 6.2; no confidence interval reported; p < 0.01). A greater proportion of women also accessed HIV care and received ART during pregnancy in the intervention (65%) compared to the control group (40%; OR 2.8; no confidence interval reported; p < 0.05).

Social Harms

Only two of the five studies described social harms as a result of study participation, such as IPV, or partner abandonment or divorce. First, Turan et al. (2018) reported no adverse social consequences in their home-based CHTC intervention. Second, Krakowiak et al. (2016) reported that out of a sample of 620 participants, at the 6-month postpartum visit, 16 women in the home visit arm (intervention group) and 12 women in the invitation arm (control group) reported physical IPV by their partner, and that 1 woman in each arm reported that the violence may have been due to study participation.

Quality Appraisal

Overall, the majority of studies included in this review were found to have a low risk of bias across selection of results, outcome measurement, missing outcome data, and the randomization process (see Table 2). Across studies, the largest area of concern came from our inability to determine in many of the articles if deviations from the intended intervention occurred; the majority of the studies did not report whether there was high fidelity or any deviations. None of the studies were considered to have a high risk of bias overall or to have high risk of bias in any of the quality domains (see Fig. 2).

Discussion

The present review assessed couples-based behavioral interventions among pregnant WLWH and their male partners to promote PMTCT outcomes. This review is the first, to our knowledge, to systematically assess behavioral approaches with pregnant WLWH and their male partners to promote PMTCT in LMICs. The review contributes to the science of couples-based HIV interventions by analyzing what approaches have been tested with pregnant couples specifically, which PMTCT outcomes were targeted, and whether such interventions are effective and safe. We identified five unique prospective couples-based intervention studies—all within sub-Saharan Africa—with three overarching approaches but vastly heterogenous activities and PMTCT outcomes.

Interventions included in the present review differed in the mode of delivery and intensity, type of couples targeted, and health content; it is not clear how these variations may impact PMTCT outcomes. Interventions ranged from one counseling session during pregnancy [29, 30] to six sessions spanning across pregnancy and the postpartum periods [32]. Two of the studies [15, 30] specified that enrolled couples must be living together or married; other studies lacked details on the type of male partner targeted. It is feasible that different levels of commitment could result in varying effects of couples-based PMTCT approaches but we did not have enough information from the primary studies to test this hypothesis. All five of the interventions delivered some form of health education but differed substantially in other content. Health topics presented to couples varied widely with some interventions [31, 32] focusing heavily on HIV while others [15, 29, 30] targeted more general maternal and child health.

Given that four of the five studies included both HIV-positive and HIV-negative pregnant female participants, it is not surprising that much of the content in these interventions focused on the promotion of general maternal and child health. Although the inclusion of both HIV-negative and HIV-positive participants was designed by some studies to limit the potential for inadvertent HIV disclosure, stigma, or suspicion in communities [15], it may also result in a lack of cost-effective means of promoting PMTCT. On the other hand, it is arguable that HIV-negative pregnant women may also benefit from such interventions via prevention of sexual HIV transmission and improved maternal and child health through, for example, exclusive breastfeeding and facility deliveries.

There was a dearth of analysis and conceptual framing across the studies regarding relationship dynamics. Even among the interventions that included content on relationship skills [15, 31, 32], there was a lack of analysis into intervention effects on these factors and their potential mediating and/or moderating effects on study outcomes. This is unfortunate given that the “micro-social context” of relationships (e.g., relationship commitment, love, trust, closeness, interdependence, IPV, communication, decision-making) likely has significant effects on the results of couples-based HIV interventions [27, 35]. Indeed, a review by El-Bassel et al. (2010) that focused on couples-based HIV approaches in the United States found that the core components of interventions to promote HIV treatment adherence included improving communication and mutual caretaking [27]. Other studies with same-sex couples have similarly reported that relationship quality factors can influence ART adherence-related outcomes [36, 37]. Only one study in the present review [15] reported that the intervention strengthened couple relationships, but the researchers did not assess whether this was a mediating relationship with the study outcomes. Similarly, only one study [15] applied a relationship-focused theoretical framework to their intervention, while all others, if they referenced a theoretical model, relied on health behavioral theories that are not specific to the couple context. As other researchers have previously recommended [38], the findings from the present review focused on PMTCT indicate that a stronger theoretical basis for couples-focused HIV approaches is needed.

Prior reviews have shown that couples-based behavioral approaches not focused on the time period of pregnancy can reduce unprotected sexual intercourse and promote condom use [38, 39]; decrease partner concurrency [39]; reduce sexual- and drug-risk behaviors [35]; and improve some HIV care and treatment outcomes [35]. In addition, a series of systematic reviews by Takah and colleagues have found that male partner involvement enhances the uptake of some PMTCT interventions [13, 40-42].

Building off prior reviews, the studies included in this review reported significant associations, or at least trends, indicating that couples-based interventions can promote some positive PMTCT health behaviors. The couples-based interventions we evaluated reported positive effects on HIV testing and ART use during pregnancy [29], linkage to HIV care during pregnancy [29], exclusive breastfeeding (marginally significant) [15], maternal engagement in postpartum care [15], and infant HIV prophylaxis [31]. Null findings were reported, however, for facility delivery [15, 30], postpartum family planning [15, 30], infant postnatal checkups [15], and detectable levels of ART in mothers and infants [31]. Two studies also reported that the intervention had a positive effect on uptake of CHTC [15, 30], which is an established facilitator in the literature of PMTCT [14, 43-45]. Yet, CHTC was not directly linked to PMTCT outcomes in any of the studies included in the preset review.

The vast majority of studies in this review (four out of five) did not report a relationship between their specific couple-based intervention approach and any specific PMTCT-related health outcome. In particular, none of the studies examined maternal health indicators, such as viral suppression, which is surprising given that viral load is a key predictor of both mother-to-child transmission and HIV treatment success [46-49]. Weiss et al. (2014) reported null differences in detectable ART through DBS in mothers, but their sample size included only 15 WLWH. Only one study in this review reported on an infant PMTCT health outcome: Sifunda et al. (2019) found highly promising results that their intervention effectively reduced infant HIV infection and mortality at 12 months postpartum.

A final area of focus of the present review was to explore whether couples-based interventions around the time of pregnancy result in increased social harms. Unfortunately, this important consideration was missing in the majority of included publications. Among the studies that did report on social harms [15, 30], there is evidence that couples-based PMTCT approaches that offer home-based CHTC do not place women at higher risk of IPV than the standard of care. This supports prior work indicating that couples-based interventions do not increase adverse social events among women [50]. Nevertheless, women’s safety should always be a key consideration during all stages of intervention design and testing when working with couples [51]. Some of the couples-based intervention studies in this review excluded women if they reported IPV in the past month [30] or severe IPV in the past 6 months [15]. However, such interventions may be disadvantaging and marginalizing the most vulnerable groups of women who would benefit from intervention. A greater consensus is needed to better understand when women should be excluded from interventions that could promote their and their children’s health because they are the victim of IPV. In some settings, WLWH who have experienced IPV in their current relationship are in fact the majority of WLWH [52].

The findings of this systematic review should be interpreted within its limitations. First, this review focused on LMICs but only found relevant studies located in three sub-Saharan African countries. Thus, the findings of this review may not extend to other settings. Restricting papers to English language only may have resulted in studies from other regions being missed. In addition, there is likely a selection bias within the studies towards more functional and higher quality relationships being represented in terms of which couples want to participate in a couples-based study. There is evidence that engaging other members aside from male partners in women’s supportive social network may improve HIV-related behaviors [53]. This was outside of the scope of our review protocol but warrants additional investigation. We used a comprehensive database literature and manual search of relevant references, but our review cannot rule-out publication bias due to missing studies that had not yet presented their results outside of our search window. Across the included studies, there was insufficient data to enable the present review to synthesize how the approaches affected relationship dynamics within couples or were moderated by certain relationship dynamics. We were additionally limited by non-uniformity in the amount of data presented in the primary articles that was available to synthesize. There was unfortunately limited data in the primary studies on maternal ART adherence and viral load. Moreover, there is a need to examine whether couples-based interventions can effectively increase uptake or adherence to pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) among pregnant/postpartum women without HIV—an area of increasing public health interest and potential [54] but beyond the scope of this review.

Based on this review, we recommend several important considerations moving forward in couples-based PMTCT intervention research. As other reviews have noted [38], there is a need for conceptual frameworks that incorporate relationship theories within couples-based HIV interventions, including interventions during pregnancy that target PMTCT. Measurement of psychosocial factors, including mental health and relationship dynamics, in the context of couples-based PMTCT interventions is warranted. We recommend more research within the context of PMTCT to understand if couples-based approaches, which are more resource intensive, result in greater public health gains compared to targeting individual pregnant women. There is a need to examine whether couples-based PMTCT interventions extend to women’s ART adherence and maternal viral load, and whether the South African study findings regarding reduced infant HIV transmission after exposure to a couples-based intervention [32] are replicable in other settings. Lastly, greater emphasis is needed on the safety of couples-based HIV approaches during pregnancy and when it is appropriate to exclude women with a history of IPV.

Conclusions

This systematic review provided a synthesis of studies using behavioral couples-based approaches to enhance PMTCT efforts in LMICs. Couples-based approaches around the time of pregnancy in LMICs appear to be safe and somewhat effective at increasing the uptake of certain behaviors related to PMTCT, such as HIV testing of pregnant women and their male partners, uptake of maternal ART, provision of infant HIV prophylaxis, as well as the promotion of infant HIV-free survival. The theoretical basis for couples-focused HIV approaches for PMTCT is currently lacking. More research is needed on the role of relationship dynamics within such interventions and whether couples-based approaches during pregnancy can extend to other key PMTCT outcomes, such as maternal viral suppression.
