Are American Follow-up Recommendations in Endocrinology Actionable? A Systematic Review of Clinical Practice Guidelines

Abstract

Purpose:

Clinical guidelines include recommendations to guide patient’s longitudinal care. These recommendations may differ in content and quality of supporting evidence from those guiding diagnosis and treatment. We aimed to identify recommendations guiding the follow-up of patients with endocrine conditions, describe their content and quality of evidence.

Methods:

We systematically assessed the Endocrine Society and the American Thyroid Association clinical guidelines and identified recommendations guiding follow-up strategies to evaluate direction, content, strength and quality of evidence.

Results:

Out of 1540 recommendations, 159 (10.3%) guided follow-up strategies. From these, 109 (79%) recommendations included goal of follow-up, 121(97.7%) suggested follow-up methods, and 56 (40.6%) a specific monitoring frequency. A total of 76 (55.1%) assessed treatment response, 65 (47.1%) disease progression, and 30 (21.7%) side effects. A total of 90 (65.2%) described the use of laboratory studies, 30 (21.7%) clinical exam/history, and 27 (19.6%) imaging studies. Finally, 91 (65.9%) suggested a monitoring time-interval and 42 (30.4%) directed an action based on results. Most recommendations [88 (55.3%)] were based on low/very low-quality evidence. A total of 73 (52.9%) recommendations were labeled as strong, from which 12% were based on high-quality evidence.

Conclusion:

One out of ten clinical recommendations for endocrine conditions guide follow-up and their content is variable. More than half of the follow-up recommendations are supported by low/very low-quality evidence and the majority does not provide an action threshold. A specific framework for developing follow up recommendations can aid guideline panelist and support evidence-based monitoring.

BACKGROUND

Clinical practice guidelines play a crucial role in supporting optimal patient care. To this end, guideline panelists often provide follow-up recommendations that focus on long-term side effects, disease control and potential for treatment cessation.[1, 2] However, compared to recommendation for treatment or diagnosis, there is limited guidance about how panelist should formulate following up recommendations.[3]

Clinical practice guidelines can be developed following systematic and transparent methods, focused on determining the quality of the evidence supporting each recommendation and considering important factors in decision making to derive the confidence/strength associated by each recommendation. [3, 4] Examples of these methods include the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) and the American College of Physicians’ (ACP) method. The GRADE working group has developed guidance for evaluating recommendations for diagnostic tests and strategies.[3] The GRADE system, however, does not directly address the content of follow-up recommendation, which could include the goal of the follow-up, methods for monitoring (e.g. laboratory, imaging, and/or clinical exam/history), frequency, and threshold for action. As a result, it is possible that this limited guidance for formulation of following recommendation leads to a variation in content of these recommendations.

In 2005, the Endocrine Society (ES) adopted the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to develop evidence-based recommendations. Since then, it has produced 35 guidelines. The American Thyroid Association (ATA) clinical practice guidelines have been developed using the ACP’s method,[5–8] and more recently following the GRADE approach.[9]. In this study, we systematically examined the content of recommendations guiding clinical follow-up in endocrine guidelines and explore the grading (as stated by both ACP and GRADE frameworks) and associated quality of evidence associated with these recommendations.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Source (guidelines identification)

We conducted a systematic evaluation of the ES and ATA clinical practice guidelines to identify clinical recommendations that advise clinicians on the follow-up of patients and analyzed their 1) direction, 2) content, 3) grading and 4) strength and quality of the evidence supporting those recommendations. We reviewed the website of both societies to identify eligible guidelines.[5–52] We excluded guidelines that did not developed their recommendations following GRADE or the American College of Physicians’ (ACP) methods to assess confidence in the evidence. We focused on guidelines that adopted one of these two approaches because of their high rigor to evaluate the quality of the recommendations.

Identification of Follow-Up Recommendations

Working independently, four experienced reviewers systematically assessed the ES and ATA guidelines to identify follow-up recommendations. Follow-up recommendations were defined as those that advised the clinician or health care team on how to provide periodic evaluation of the patient after an initial episode of care. For this study, our operational definition of follow-up recommendations included those that aim to assess the response to therapy (medical or surgical) (Example 7, Table 1), the progression of disease (including associated disease status) (Example 8, Table 1) and the development of side effects or complications after medical/surgical therapy (Example 9, Table 1). We excluded recommendations in which the main goal was screening (systematic examination of an individual at risk of a specific disease or condition and who does not exhibit symptoms) and those in which the stem of the recommendation included screening as the objective (Example 24, Table 1). Similarly, best practice statements and those reporting on very specific lab/assay parameters to consider were excluded (Example 25, Table 1).

An initial pilot phase to ensure extractors understood the operational definition of follow-up recommendations was performed by evaluating five randomly chosen guidelines.[11, 12, 16, 22, 50] Afterwards, the selection process took place in three phases. First, each of the four extractors reviewed all the recommendations in the included clinical practice guidelines. At this stage, the most minimal suggestion of monitoring was sufficient to consider it relevant for inclusion; however, to be included, the four extractors had to agree that the recommendation was addressing follow-up. Second, a re-evaluation of the initially selected (potential) follow-up recommendations was performed independently by each of the four reviewers to re-assess eligibility with increased rigor. In this phase, all four reviewers had to agree to exclude a recommendation. Lastly, a second re-evaluation was performed along senior investigators in which all of them had to agree to exclude or include all of the recommendations.

Data Extraction and Classification

Using a standardized and pre-piloted form, and independently, each of the reviewers extracted the following data from each of the final selected recommendations: 1) direction of the recommendation (in favor, against, neutral), 2) content, 3) strength of the recommendation (strong or weak) and 4) quality of evidence (low, very low, moderate or high).

We evaluated the direction of the recommendation (in favor, against, or neutral), as less details might be required if no follow up intervention is suggested (Examples 1–3, Table 1).

In terms of content, for each recommendation, we noted if the population of interest was clearly defined for each recommendation (Examples 4 & 5, Table 1). We also noted if the goal and type of test were clearly defined in each recommendation (Examples 6 & 10, Table 1). Moreover, we categorized the goal of follow-up (response to treatment, progression of disease, evaluation of side effects; Examples 7–9, Table 1), the suggested method of evaluation (imaging, laboratory, clinical exam/history; Examples 11–13, Table 1), time interval (recommendation on when to start a follow-up intervention provided, yes/no; Examples 14 & 15, Table 1), frequency of follow-up (recommendation on the frequency of the intervention provided, yes/no; Examples 16 & 17, Table 1), end of follow-up (recommendation on when to end a follow-up intervention provided, yes/no; Examples 18 & 19, Table 1) action (includes action based on results, yes/no; Examples 20 & 21, Table 1), and comparison (more than one way of doing the follow-up, yes/no; Examples 22 & 23, Table 1).

The strength of the recommendation and the associated quality of the evidence were directly retrieved from each guideline. Multiple meetings (5 meetings) between the reviewers were performed to discuss conflicts of classification. If consensus could not be reached, a third and senior investigator was consulted (3 meetings).

Statistical Analysis

Chance-adjusted inter-reviewer agreement was measured with Fleiss’ kappa statistic at the first re-evaluation and in the categorization phases. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize results. All the analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA).

RESULTS

General characteristics

A total of 40 clinical guidelines were identified as of February 2020 and evaluated, 35 (87.5%) from the ES and 5 (12.5%) from the ATA including a variety of topics. From a total of 1540 recommendations, 159 (10.3%) recommendations met our inclusion criteria. (Figure 1) Chance-adjusted inter-reviewer agreement was substantial in both (first re-evaluation and categorization) phases (κ > 0.80).

Ten of the guidelines of the ES did not include any follow-up recommendations [18, 23–25, 29, 30, 33, 38, 43, 44] leaving 30 guidelines that provided recommendations for this analysis. (Table 2)

Direction of the Recommendations

Most recommendations were in favor of a follow-up practice, 138/159 (86.7%), whilst 14 (8.8%) were against recommendations and 7 (4.4%) were neutral (no recommendation was issued). From the total follow-up recommendations, 13 (8.1%) were not graded by the panels (Supplemental Table 2).

Content of the Recommendations

The population was clearly defined in 112/138 (81.1%) of the in-favor recommendations. Similarly, the goal was clearly defined in 109/138 (78.9%); the majority of which (76/138, 55%) intended to evaluate the response to treatment. Additionally, the type of test used to evaluate was clearly defined in 121/138 (87.7%) and the time interval for follow-up was provided in 91/138 (65.9%). Finally, less than half (42/138, 30.4%) directed an action based on the results of the follow-up evaluation; and almost none (2/138, 1.4%) compared or gave preference of one test over another for providing follow-up (Table 3). The characteristics of the against, neutral, and ungraded follow-up recommendations are described in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2. Less information in terms of timing, action threshold and comparison were found on the against and neutral recommendations.

Strength of Recommendation and Associated Quality of Evidence

Overall, 73 (52.9%) of the in-favor follow-up recommendations were labeled as strong, 36 (67.9%) from the ATA and 37 (43.5%) from the ES guidelines. Moreover, 11 (8.0%) of all in favor follow-up recommendations were based on high quality evidence, 37 (26.8%) were based on moderate quality evidence and 79 (57.2%) were based on low/very low quality evidence, while 11 (8.0%) were not graded.

Only 12% of the follow-up recommendations graded as strong were based on high-quality evidence, 39.8% were sustained by moderate and 48.1% by low or very low-quality evidence. On the other hand, only 3.2% of recommendations classified as conditional or weak were based on high-quality, 20.6% on moderate, and 76.1% on low/very low-quality evidence. (Figure 2)

DISCUSSION

Clinical follow-up is one of the most important clinical activities in the management of patients with chronic conditions.[1, 2] We found that ~10% of clinical practice recommendations in endocrinology focus on guiding follow-up and most of them are in favor of follow-up practice, rather than on giving neutral or no recommendation. This may be due to the fact that in favor recommendations have more evidence supporting them when compared to those neutral or those not providing any recommendation. Hence, panelists may be more confident in stating a recommendation with more evidence. In terms of content, most recommendations define the population of interest, the goal of follow-up and the test recommended for monitoring. Less commonly information on timing/frequency, action threshold and alternative test was provided. Moreover, most of the graded recommendations are based on low/very quality evidence (88/157, 56%) and only 12/157 (7.6%) are based on high quality evidence. Finally, only 12% of recommendations labeled as strong recommendations are supported by high quality evidence. These findings suggest variability in the content of follow-up recommendations, suggesting a possible gap in the guidance for development of actionable follow-up recommendations and limited availability of high-quality evidence supporting follow-up strategies.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that evaluated the quality of evidence supporting follow-up recommendations in other fields in medicine. Marciano et al., evaluated follow-up recommendations regarding cutaneous melanoma through a systematic approach.[53] They realized that most guideline recommendations in the follow-up of melanoma patients were based on low-level evidence or consensus expert opinion, some of which concerned the frequency of routine skin examinations and the use of imaging and diagnostic tests. Similarly, Johansson, et al., assessed follow-up recommendations within prostate cancer guidelines.[54] They found out that recommendations regarding when to perform a diagnostic test or perform clinical interventions where primarily based on consensus statements, low-quality evidence studies, or even without any supporting references.

Similarly, our findings on the quantification of strength and quality of the evidence of the follow-up recommendations are consistent with previous reports. Brito et al., conducted a study evaluating those strong recommendations within the ES guidelines that were based on low or very low-quality of the evidence. [55] They found out that from a total of 206 strong recommendations, 121 (59%) were based on low- or very low-quality evidence, the so called ‘paradoxical’ recommendations. Similarly, Bautista-Orduno et al., evaluated the existent proportion of these paradoxical recommendations within the ATA guidelines.[47] They found out that from a total of 355 strong recommendations, 151 (42.5%) were based on low-quality evidence. According to our findings, follow-up recommendations may contribute partly to these two proportions of paradoxical recommendations within the ES and the ATA, as we found that 55.2% of strong follow-up recommendations from the ES and 42.2% of the ATA were based on low- or very low-quality evidence.

Our study is strengthened by an independent evaluation by four experienced reviewers using pre-piloted forms and definitions, and a multi-staged selection process for all included recommendations. Substantial results in our inter-observer agreement tests also provide strength to our study. This strict process is important, given the lack of a universal definition for follow-up recommendations and the arbitrary content categories evaluated. Moreover, a novel contribution of our study is the variability noted in the content of follow-up recommendations, including a wide range of parameters (e.g. population, timing, action threshold, and comparison). Also, in this study we divided recommendations according to their direction; less information in terms of timing, action threshold and comparison were found on the against and neutral recommendations when compared to the in-favor. An important limitation to highlight is that our analysis is based on the stem of the clinical practice recommendation; therefore, it is possible that other information of interest could be found in the associated description. Our study is also limited by the fact that we only surveyed guidelines from two endocrine societies; hence, these results could not be generalized with certainty to guidelines from other endocrine societies or addressing conditions not included in our study.[53, 54]

To the extent of our knowledge there is no specific guidance on how to frame follow-up recommendations to assure they are actionable. In our assessment we focused on components (content) that are related to the follow up process such as: population, goal of follow-up, methods, action, and timing. Speaking of the latter, almost half of the follow-up recommendations identified (67, 42.1%) did not provide a time interval to begin or perform a follow-up intervention, while 103 (64.8%) failed to specify a frequency of follow-up and only 10 (6.3%) recommended an end to follow-up. To clarify this point we highlight a recommendation with very specific parameters in terms of timing (Example 18 in Table 1). This recommendation clearly states the time interval for doing the first measurement, the frequency of the continuing measurement, and it suggests when should the clinician stop measuring the specific parameter. In contrast, please see Example 15 in Table 1which provides a recommendation that does not specify the interval, frequency, or end of follow-up. We suggest this content variability can affect the degree to which recommendations are actionable in practice; although other factors such as strength and quality of evidence of the recommendation, clinician and patient characteristics, context and resources, are also important to consider. Similarly, it is possible that not all the content components that we evaluated need to be stated in all follow up recommendations for them to be actionable by clinicians; however, we believe this heterogeneity requires further evaluation.

The complex management of chronic or recurrent clinical conditions is primarily based on follow-up or monitoring of our patients. A significant amount of time and resources are invested in follow-up strategies by patients and clinicians.[56] Hence, it is extremely important to wisely allocate resources (time, human resources, financial) while assuring optimal monitoring. To reach this goal, it is imperative to guide monitoring strategies by evidence. Our findings demonstrate that, although follow-up is an extremely important component of clinical medicine, the majority of follow-up strategies and recommendations are not based on high-quality evidence. This highlights a necessity to drive the research agendas towards testing the efficacy of current and future monitoring strategies that could eventually optimize the outcomes of clinical follow-up and decrease the associated burden for patients, clinicians and the medical system.

Conducting research to improve and resolve evidence gaps identified by clinical practice recommendations addressing follow-up, could be another way forward. Unfortunately, research that could improve trust in effect estimates is ongoing for only one in five recommendations that are based on very low-quality evidence in a survey made on TES guidelines;[4] and it remains unknown how much research is ongoing to improve the quality of evidence associated with follow-up recommendations. Finally, shared decision-making could help reduce the uncertainty and improve health care quality while waiting for high-quality evidence to guide the follow-up of our patients.[57]

CONCLUSION

Despite being a fundamental aspect in the management of patients with chronic conditions, the content and structure of follow-up recommendations in American guidelines is variable, resulting in possible different levels of actionality for clinicians. Moreover, they are supported mainly by low-quality evidence and a high proportion of recommendations that are considered strong are based on low/very-low quality evidence. Our findings highlight the need to better understand the components that make a follow-up recommendation helpful and actionable and of investing in clinical research that provide high quality evidence comparing follow-up strategies in terms of benefits, risks, and burden.
