A systematic review of the number needed to screen for active tuberculosis among people living with HIV

Abstract

Background:

Systematic screening for active tuberculosis (TB) is recommended for all people living with HIV (PLWH), however case detection remains poor globally. We investigated the yield of active case finding (ACF) by calculating the number needed to screen (NNS) to detect a case of active TB among PLWH.

Methods:

We identified studies reporting ACF for TB among PLWH published from November 2010-February 2020. We calculated crude NNS for Xpert- or culture-confirmed TB and weighted mean NNS stratified by screening approach, population/risk group, and country TB burden.

Results:

Of 27,221 abstracts screened, we identified 58 studies eligible for inclusion, including 5 in low/moderate TB incidence settings and 53 in medium/high incidence settings. Populations screened for TB included inpatients, outpatients not receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART), outpatients receiving ART, CD4<200, children ≤15 years, pregnant PLWH, and PLWH in prisons. Screening tools included symptom-based screening, chest X-ray, C-reactive protein, and Xpert MTB/RIF. The weighted mean NNS varied across groups but was consistently low, ranging from 4 among inpatients in moderate/high TB burden settings to 137 among pregnant PLWH in moderate/high TB burden settings.

Conclusions:

ACF is a high yield intervention among PLWH. Approaches to screening should be tailored to local epidemiological and health-system contexts, and sensitive screening tools such as Xpert should be implemented where feasible.

BACKGROUND

Tuberculosis (TB) remains the leading cause of death for people living with HIV (PLWH) worldwide, yet is markedly underdiagnosed, with over 50% of cases going undetected each year.1 Active case finding (ACF) for TB has been promoted as a key strategy to increase early case detection and treatment initiation among PLWH, and has been shown to be effective in reducing TB prevalence, incidence, and mortality in several settings.2-5 Strategies for ACF among PLWH are wide-ranging, from community-based screening campaigns to intensified case finding during routine HIV clinical encounters.

While ACF has been used extensively for detecting TB among PLWH globally, ACF interventions are diverse and their yield is highly dependent on factors such as TB burden in the screened population and accuracy of the screening tool and diagnostic test used. Indeed, a 2013 systematic review by Shapiro et al. estimated that 30 PLWH would need to be screened to detect a single case of TB in low TB incidence settings, compared with just 10 in high TB incidence settings.6 Importantly, shifts in global TB/HIV epidemiology, advances in molecular TB diagnostics, and the development of novel screening algorithms have likely had a substantial impact on ACF yield in recent years, and understanding the landscape of ACF for PLWH is critical to establish high-yield, cost-effective strategies to improve case finding globally. We therefore conducted a systematic review of the literature to determine the number of people needed to screen (NNS) to detect one case of active TB among PLWH across a variety of ACF strategies.

METHODS

Systematic review process

This review was part of a larger systematic review of the NNS for ACF across multiple populations and risk groups that served as an update of the 2013 review by Shapiro et al,6 and was conducted on behalf of the WHO Guidelines Development Group (GDG) on systematic screening for TB. We searched PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library from November 2010-February 2020 to identify titles and abstracts for initial review. Search terms and subject indexing vocabulary included a combination of “tuberculosis,” “mass screening,” “mass chest x-ray,” “mass radiography,” contact tracing,” and “screening,” among other terms (Supplementary Table 1). Titles and abstracts were entered into a database and duplicates eliminated. Titles and abstracts for the remaining entries were independently screened in Covidence by two reviewers, with adjudication performed by a third reviewer if the first two reviewers were in disagreement. Initial review criteria required only that the publication be original research (i.e. not a review, commentary, or author reply letter), suggest that ACF had taken place, and be written in English, French, or Spanish.

Two reviewers then independently assessed full texts for eligibility, with adjudication by a third reviewer if the first two reviewers were in disagreement. We included studies that: 1) reported original research; 2) reported results of ACF for TB; and 3) were published in English, French, or Spanish. We excluded studies if: 1) the paper did not report original data; 2) no ACF was conducted; 3) Xpert or TB culture was not performed to confirm active TB (for study populations with adults >15 years); 4) the number of persons screened was not reported; 5) outcomes of active and passive case finding were not disaggregated; 6) microbiologic and clinical diagnoses of TB were not disaggregated (for study populations with adults >15 years); 7) only an abstract was published; 8) the paper was published in a language other than English, French, or Spanish; 9) the paper presented duplicate data from another publication; or 10) the paper was unavailable. For the analysis of NNS for PLWH, we then excluded papers in which the study population did not include PLWH and/or the data could not be stratified by HIV status. We excluded papers if the study population was pre-screened, unless the source population size was also reported. For example, a study that conducted ACF in PLWH reporting cough would not be included unless the total population size of PLWH screened for cough was also reported.

Two reviewers independently abstracted study characteristics and results from each included paper according to a standardized protocol. Abstracted data included the study location, demographic information about the study population, screening strategy, case definition for TB, number screened, and number of TB cases identified by screening strategy. If multiple screening strategies were reported, each was abstracted separately. Discrepancies in full text reviews and data abstraction were resolved by consensus and/or consultation with a third reviewer. Risk of bias assessment was performed using the AXIS tool for cross-sectional studies.7

Definitions

Definitions used for the review are shown in Figure 1. The study population was defined as the number of PLWH screened for TB according to individual study criteria, regardless of the availability of results. If the number of people screened for whom results were available was the only number reported, then that number was taken as the study population. Active TB was defined as a microbiologic diagnosis of TB using mycobacterial culture or Xpert MTB/RIF for people >15 years; if Xpert and mycobacterial culture were both reported, active TB was defined as culture-positive TB. For children ≤15 years, active TB was defined as any diagnosis of TB disease according to individual study criteria, including clinical diagnoses. A strategy was considered to be primary screening if it applied to all of the study population and secondary screening if only a subset of the studied population received it, following a primary screen. For example, a study that screened a population via symptom screening and followed up those with a positive symptom screen with chest X-ray (CXR) would be classified as using symptom screening as a primary screen and CXR as a secondary screen.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the NNS to detect one case of active TB for PLWH. For each individual study, we calculated this indirectly as the inverse of the prevalence of TB detected through screening. We calculated individual study NNS as well as weighted mean NNS values (due to substantial differences in study population sizes) with ranges. We calculated weighted mean NNS values by taking a weighted average of the prevalence detected in each individual study (weighted by study population size) and then calculating the inverse. A study that detected no TB cases had an undefined NNS; studies that detected no TB cases contributed to weighted estimates since these were calculated from study prevalences before inverting the value to generate the NNS. For weighted mean NNS estimates including studies that detected no TB cases, the upper bound of the range was reported as undefined. All NNS calculations were rounded up to the nearest integer value in order to better represent the concept of the number of individuals needed to screen.

We stratified analyses according to population characteristics (inpatients, outpatients not on ART, outpatients on ART, CD4<200 cells/μL, children ≤15 years, pregnant PLWH, PLWH in prisons) and primary screening approaches (symptom-based screening, Xpert, CXR, CRP) identified by the WHO GDG as priority groups and screening strategies for PLWH. Studies that reported multiple screening strategies (e.g. reported data on presence of symptoms and CXR results for all study participants) or risk groups (e.g. reported data on outpatients and inpatients, with results stratified) contributed to multiple NNS calculations. Because very few studies used a secondary screen (Supplementary Table 2), we only stratified analyses by primary screening strategy.

To account for the effect of underlying TB burden on yield of screening, we stratified results by World Health Organization (WHO) 2020 estimated country-level annual TB incidence: low incidence (<30 per 100,000), moderate incidence (30-100 per 100,000), medium incidence (100-300 per 100,000), and high incidence (>300 per 100,000).8

Risk of bias

Results of the AXIS risk of bias assessment are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Overall, there was a low risk of bias among the included studies regarding the identification of target populations, participant selection/enrollment, and methods for outcome assessment. Several studies had high non-response rates (e.g. studies with a large proportion of participants who did not complete the screening and/or testing because they could not produce sputum), potentially impacting outcome ascertainment.

Overall, NNS estimates were highly heterogeneous across HIV subgroups and screening strategies, with lower NNS estimates among populations at higher TB risk (e.g. inpatients, outpatients not on ART, PLWH with CD4<200) and for studies using more sensitive screening strategies (e.g. Xpert), and higher NNS estimates for lower risk populations (e.g. PLWH on ART) and less sensitive screening tools (e.g. symptom screening).

DISCUSSION

ACF is a pillar of the WHO’s global strategy for TB detection and mortality reduction among PLWH,2,3 however its implementation is highly variable and the impact of ACF interventions on TB burden among PLWH is unclear. In this systematic review, we evaluated the literature on ACF among PLWH to determine the number of people needed to screen to detect a single case of active TB across a variety of screening algorithms, HIV risk groups, and settings. We found that the NNS varied both within and across TB incidence settings according to risk group and screening tool used, demonstrating the heterogeneity in ACF interventions employed and populations screened. Nevertheless, NNS estimates were markedly low across all groups, highlighting the importance of targeting this high-risk population for screening.

Important considerations for ACF interventions include the costs and resources required for implementation. Identifying high yield populations to prioritize for screening is therefore essential for health systems in lower-income settings to efficiently allocate resources. PLWH have long been a focus for ACF due to their high TB risk and low NNS,2,3,6 however the costs and effectiveness of different screening algorithms are still of concern: While higher-resource tools such as CXR and Xpert may be difficult to implement in some settings, less costly screening tools such as symptom screening may fail to identify TB in PLWH due to variable sensitivity. Here, we found that NNS estimates with symptom screening were higher than those with CXR-, Xpert-, and CRP-based screening; nevertheless, the yield of symptom-based screening was still high compared with most non-HIV populations,6 emphasizing that even a tool with lower sensitivity may identify a large number of TB cases if implemented among PLWH. Moreover, the NNS estimates confirm the utility of consistently implemented symptom screening in settings where more resource-intensive case finding strategies are not currently feasible. However, our findings simultaneously highlight the importance of advances in molecular diagnostics, indicating that Xpert-based screening programs are among the highest-yield. The difference in NNS for Xpert-driven screening programs (NNS=12) vs. symptom screen algorithms (NNS=45) among outpatient PLWH not on ART in medium and high incidence settings (most often screening at the time of ART initiation) reflects the massive TB detection gap in this population. At a cost of approximately USD20 per test, Xpert—while expensive—may be cost effective in this extremely high TB burden population. Given both the significantly higher yield and sensitivity of Xpert compared to symptom screening, we recommend implementing Xpert-based screening for PLWH not on ART in high and medium-incidence settings. Efforts should be made to ensure widespread access to Xpert in lower-resource settings.

Our analysis showed that both CXR- and CRP-based screening strategies are high-yield, with very low NNS estimates. Though more resource intensive than other screening tools, CXR is highly sensitive; newer technologies such as mobile technology and computer-aided detection (CAD) may improve CXR accessibility in high TB incidence, lower-resource settings. CRP-based screening is also promising, given its low-cost and capacity to implement at the point-of-care. However, relatively few studies have evaluated the effectiveness of CRP for ACF to date, and the majority of high-quality CRP screening studies were conducted in outpatient, ART-naïve PLWH with low CD4 counts.35,62 Additional research will help resolve the role of CRP-based screening for ACF in PLWH.

ACF is crucial to identify TB among PLWH, who are at extremely elevated risk of TB compared to persons without HIV and who are also less likely to self-present to health facilities for diagnosis due to structural barriers to care and differences in TB presentation.64,67-69 Furthermore, not only can effective ACF interventions increase TB diagnosis and treatment initiation—thereby reducing TB morbidity, mortality, and transmission—sensitive screening algorithms are also important to rule-out active TB and identify PLWH eligible for TB preventive therapy.2,3 Global uptake of TB preventive therapy remains unacceptably low among PLWH,8 and well-designed ACF interventions that initiate persons screening negative on preventive therapy are essential to achieve TB control worldwide.

The primary limitation of this review is the heterogeneity of included studies. Given the major differences in case finding methodologies and study settings, direct comparisons between screening tools and risk groups are difficult to make; indeed, even within risk groups, there were substantial differences in screened populations. To capture this heterogeneity, we present weighted mean NNS results with ranges, stratified by risk group, screening strategy, and country TB burden. While the variability of the included studies makes identifying a single best screening strategy challenging, our NNS estimates provide valuable evidence to inform local guidelines. Furthermore, heterogeneity in ACF implementation is a global reality, and is an essential consideration when developing screening recommendations.

In conclusion, we found that PLWH remain a high yield population for TB screening and should continue to be prioritized for ACF, in accordance with past analyses.2,3,6 Specific screening strategies should be tailored to local epidemiological and health system contexts, with NNS estimates providing insights for cost-effectiveness and implementation of ACF interventions that maximize TB case finding. Forthcoming WHO guidelines on ACF for PLWH will provide valuable guidance for national TB programmes developing ACF recommendations.70
