Gene network profiling in muscle-invasive bladder cancer: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Background:

Determining meta-analysis of transcriptional profiling of muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) through Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets has not been investigated. This study aims to define gene expression profiles in MIBC and to identify potential candidate genes and pathways.

Objectives:

To review and evaluate gene expression studies in MIBC through publicly available RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) and microarray data in order to identify potential prognostic and therapeutic targets for MIBC.

Methods:

A systematic literature search of the Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed, and Wiley Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases was performed using the terms “gene,” “gene expression,” and “bladder cancer” January 1, 1990 through March 2021 focused on populations with MIBC.

Results:

In the final analysis, GEO datasets were included. Fixed effect model was employed in the meta-analysis. Gene networking connections and gene-set functional analyses of the identified genes as differentially expressed in MIBC were performed using ImaGEO and GeneMANIA software. A heatmap for the upregulated and downregulated genes was generated along with the correlated pathways.

Conclusion:

A total of 9 genes were reported in this analysis. Six genes were reported as upregulated (ProTα, SPINT1, UBE2E1, RAB25, KPNB1, HDAC1) and 3 genes as downregulated (NUP188, IPO13, NUP124). Genes were found to be involved in “ubiquitin mediated proteolysis,” “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum,” “transcriptional misregulation in cancer,” and “RNA transport” pathways.

Introduction

1.

Bladder cancer (BCa) carries a large societal burden, with over 400,000 people diagnosed worldwide with an anticipated 165,000 bladder cancer related deaths per year [1]. Bladder cancer is staged via the tumor-node-metastasis system, which describes the extent of invasion within bladder as Tis-T4 [2]. Patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), consisting of stage T2 and beyond (T2-4) have malignant cells that invade through the connective tissue into the muscle wall followed by spread outside the bladder wall or to local lymph nodes [3]. While MIBC represents only 25% of all bladder cancer at diagnosis; [4,5], it is characterized by many chromosomal alterations, as well as clinical progression to metastatic disease and limited (<50%) survival at 5 years despite aggressive treatment [6].

Radical cystectomy is the standard treatment for MIBC, it is associated with non-negligible morbidity and mortality [7]. Surgical complexity and high procedure costs remain significant obstacles in the treatment of MIBC and advances in treatment have stalled for the past several decades [8]. Only with the recent US Food and Drug Administration approval of immune checkpoint inhibitors, has there been a resurgence of molecular subtype reporting for therapeutic regimen allocation [9].

Therefore, to further understand the prognosis and develop new biological therapies, knowledge of novel biomarkers for MIBC represents an urgent need in this field. Interest has grown with respect to the use of microarray-based gene expression profiling to predict survival and long-term outcomes for individual cancer patients [10]. High-throughput microarray technology can investigate RNA expression levels of hundreds of thousands of genes and provide comprehensive insight at the molecular levels for potential causes and diagnoses of cancer [11]. Using this technology, numerous genetic markers have been found and validated as diagnostic and prognostic indicators [12]. While microarray-based analysis has proved to be predictive in measuring response to hormonal treatment and targeted therapy in breast [13,14] and colon cancer [15] in cases of MIBC, there are only sparse reports regarding the use of microarrays to predict survival rates. Past studies implicate candidate genes (USP18, DGCR2, ZNF699, IL1B, S100A8, S100A9, EGFR, USP18, DGCR2, NR1H3) in the prediction of MIBC progression [16,17]. It’s noteworthy to mention that none of the aforementioned genes were clinically validated and identification of MIBC-linked genes may lead to innovative clinical markers and novel molecular treatments. This systematic review and meta-analysis is therefore motivated by an unmet need in MIBC. We aim to identify gene expression levels associated with MIBC, as well as characterize possible gene interactions and functional pathways, with the goal of elucidating potential therapeutic targets and clinical biomarkers.

Methods

2.

A systematic literature review was carried out to identify publications investigating genetic contributions to the development of MIBC (T2-T4) bladder cancer and assess potential targets in a prognostic and and/or therapeutic clinical context. The search was performed in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews (PRISMA) guidelines [18] for systematic review. The study was preregistered in the international Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/w3sk8) to host our protocol and study data in a publicly available database [19].

Literature search

2.1.

A systematic search strategy was developed by an academic librarian at our institution to identify publications that evaluated the role of gene expression among MIBC. The search was conducted using the Ovid MEDLINE (segment: epub ahead of print, in-process and other non-indexed citations, Ovid MEDLINE daily and Ovid MEDLINE, 1946 to present), PubMed, and Wiley Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. Search results were limited to research published between January 1,1990 and March 30, 2021 in order not to omit pertinent articles. The search was performed using MeSH terms and Text Words such as “bladder cancer.” “genetic,” “gene,” and “gene expression,” The comprehensive search strategies are available (Supplemental Table 1). Two researchers (I.I. and P.M.) have reviewed the generated library using Rayyan [20] to find relevant studies that contributed to this review. Rayyan is a practical, cloud-based software aimed to improve the review process and selection of studies by assessing their methodological quality with initial input from the users in terms of key words and phrases. Users then confirm whether to include or exclude the returned studies.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

2.2.

Only English language publications were included for screening. Inclusion criteria were gene expression analysis of stage T2-4 BCa compared to experimental studies on samples from available non-cancerous human bladder tissue. Most of the included studies either had 1 or 2 healthy controls or used data from the genotype-tissue expression (GTEx) or gene expression database of normal and tumor tissues (GENT), which are repositories of normal bladder tissue. Studies did not report their controls were compared to existing controls to generate data. Besides, only conventional urothelial carcinoma cases were analyzed in the metanalysis. We also provide an example of assigning samples to each group (Supplemental Figure 1) to guide readers. Studies were included if gene expression was detected and quantified using an RNA sequencing methodology (RNA-Seq) and microarray in human bladder cancer tissues with Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) availability, while studies in vitro and utilizing animals or cancer cell-lines were excluded with the aim of elucidating differentially-expressed genes in humans only. The studies were selected from the homogeneous population to minimize possible genetic variations and environmental factors.

Quality evaluation of the included studies

2.3.

The included studies were evaluated using the Ottawa-Newcastle Quality Assessment Scale [21] and described conclusions using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) standards (Supplemental Table 2).

Data extraction and analysis

2.4.

Following selection and quality evaluation, the included studies were assessed based on availability of GEO database. GEO is a public functional genomics database repository of high throughput gene expression data and hybridization arrays, chips, microarrays [22]. A GEO accession number is required to retrieve data from the GEO repository database to run meta-analysis for the included studies. Included GEO datasets were recorded as platform and experimental design (Supplemental Table 3). ImaGEO is a useful resource that allows researchers to integrate and perform meta-analysis of GEO datasets for biomarker discovery studies [23]. Supplemental Figure 2 depicts the pipeline of the analysis. Normalization and integration were performed using the quantile method as different platforms and techniques were employed to collect datasets. Additionally, secondary confirmatory normalization method was employed by means of training distribution matching (TDM), which transforms RNA-seq and microarray data for use with models constructed from legacy platforms and nonparanormal transformation and a simple log2 transformation on biological datasets of gene expression [24-26]. A quality control based on the percentage of missing values was achieved for each dataset. The samples and genes with more missing values than allowed were removed and the expression of remaining missing values were imputed into their groups. The GEO datasets were uploaded into the software with a priori meta-analysis parameters. Fixed effect model was used where the estimated effect size in each study is assumed to come from an underlying true effect size plus measurement error. Z-value for effect size was utilized to measure the difference between an observed statistic, the gene expression levels, and its hypothesized population parameter in units of the standard deviation. P values for functional enrichment analysis [27,28] in genes reported (Appendix) in MIBC with default parameter set (minimum overlap 5, minimum enrichment 1.5, adjusted P value threshold was set (<0.05) to select differentially expressed genes). Specifically, P values were calculated according to the cumulative hypergeometric distribution for accounting for multiple testing. Kappa scores were used as the similarity metric when performing hierarchical clustering on the enriched terms, and sub-trees with similarity >0.3 were considered a cluster. The most statistically significant term within a cluster was selected as the one representing the cluster. A subset of the enriched term was selected and rendered as a network plot to capture further the relationship among terms, where edges connect terms with similarity >0.3. Fig. 1 illustrates the total manuscript selection strategy and flow diagram.

Given that microarray and RNA-seq gene expression data generally suffer from missing value problems due to various experimental reasons [29,30], missing data points can adversely affect downstream analysis. Since differences in data structures and statistical hypotheses across multiple studies may make the direct combination of effect sizes impossible or generate suspicious results, combining P values from various studies is often more appealing [31]. In this study, we used Stouffer’s aggregation method [32], an inverse cumulative distribution function of standard normal distribution.

Primary outcomes of meta-analysis were recorded by analyzing differentially expressed genes with false discovery rate (FDR) < threshold. A positive z-value represents gene overexpression, and a negative z-value represents gene underexpression. A heatmap was generated to depict the expression of the most significant over- and under-expressed genes in a set of samples of each dataset. The gene set enrichment analysis method for biological process, molecular function, and cellular component was used to identify classes of genes that are over-represented in a large set of genes and may have an association with disease phenotypes. The DAVID, a web -accessible program, was employed for functional annotation of genes [33]. In addition to primary outcomes, GeneMANIA, a user-friendly real-time multiple association network integration algorithm, was applied to generate gene set functional hypotheses (http://genemania.org/; accessed May 29, 2021) [34]. A list of the most significant differentially expressed genes was uploaded to the GeneMANIA server. The network of genes is generated showing co-expression, physical interactions, co-localization, shared protein domains, predicted interactions, and genetic interactions. The created network highlights genes and scores them based on relative connections to the original list. These results may be used to prioritize gene candidates for future functional assays. Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html) pathway analysis [35] was conducted to identify the pathway between the most significant genes based on log2 fold change cutoff of 1.3 found at the biologically functional level. To further identify enriched clusters from expression profiling data MetaScape 3 (http://metscape.ncibi.org/) bioinformatics tool was utilized. The related genes (upregulated/downregulated) and involved pathways were reported in MIBC.

Results

3.

The literature search returned 4,166 studies. Of these studies, 10 met the inclusion criteria for this review and were included in the final analysis with GEO accession numbers (GSE31189, GSE7476, GSE145261, GSE48276, GSE48075, GSE37317, GSE148079, GSE128192, GSE31684, GSE59483). Overall, the included studies had fair to good quality. For the 10 GEO datasets, 9,467 genes run in the meta-analysis showed MIBC bladder cancer related genes in BCa patients vs. non-cancerous controls. The heatmap represents the expression of 100 (over and under) most differently expressed genes in healthy controls and bladder cancer patients with a clear distinction representing red pixels as corresponding to increased abundance of mRNA in samples, whereas green pixels indicate decreased mRNA levels (Fig. 2). The main characteristic features of included GEO datasets were summarized in Supplemental Table 4.

Enrichment functional analysis

3.1.

Enrichment analysis (molecular, biological, and cellular component) results were reported as downregulated and upregulated genes (Appendix Excel sheet) along with their corresponding P values and GO annotations. GeneMANIA indicated the network connections between over/under-expressed genes. Interactions between genes were displayed as a network diagram (Fig. 3, supplemental Figures 3/4). A total of 9 genes were reported by this analysis. In summary, 6 genes were reported as upregulated (ProTα, SPINT1, UBE2E1, RAB25, KPNB1, HDAC1) and 3 genes reported as downregulated (NUP188, IPO13, NUP124) (Table 1, Fig. 4).

Functional analysis demonstrated that the majority of the upregulated genes share similar functions in the involvement of signal transduction involved in mitotic G1 DNA damage checkpoint and intracellular signal transduction involved in G1 DNA damage checkpoint (Fig. 3). Moreover, the downregulated genes have similar features in terms of nucleobase-containing compound transport and nucleobase-containing compound transport.

KEGG pathway analysis

3.2.

KEGG analyses reported significant pathways based on their log −(P) values. KEGG pathway analysis showed the most significant upregulated genes were involved in “transcriptional misregulation in cancer,” “ubiquitin mediated proteolysis,” “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum,” and “RNA transport” pathway. However, the downregulated genes were found to be involved in “RNA transport” pathway. The remaining important pathways can be considered as follows: “postmitotic nuclear pore complex (NPC) reformation,” “protein K63-linked ubiquitination,” “cytokine signaling in immune system” and “negative regulation of cellular catabolic process.” Meta-Scape displayed the top enrichment clusters for differentially expressed genes, which discretely colored to encode P values of increasing statistical significance (Fig. 5).

Discussion

4.

Several studies have proposed various approaches to select and combine genes to predict the prognosis of MIBC. As of yet, these genes sets have not been systematically compared with each other, and their performance over independent datasets is unsatisfactory [4]. This study aimed to detect a combination of genes development of MIBC. This systematic review and meta-analysis of RNA-seq and microarray through publicly available studies identified a total of 9 genes with potential involvement in the pathogenesis of MIBC.

Upregulated genes in MIBC

4.1.

ProTα (Prothymosin-α)

4.1.1.

ProTα is an acidic nuclear protein with significant cellular (i.e., cell cycle regulation, chromatin condensation, or transcription) and immunological functions [36]. Over-expression of ProTα has been reported in various malignancies including breast and lung cancer [37]. Expression of nuclear and cytoplasmic ProTα was linked to the occurrence of MIBC in multiple previous studies [38]. Since its discovery in 1984, the biological role of this protein has been controversial. While Prothymosin-α (ProTα) was first considered a thymic factor, molecular and cellular analysis led to conclude its vital role in cellular proliferation events [39]. In particular, this protein has been shown to be involved in the unfolding of chromatin fibers in a process mediated by the interaction of ProTα with histone H1. This relationship between the cellular proliferation, as well as potential role in apoptosis, makes it an attractive target in search for modulators of cell death (Fig. 4).

SPINT1 (Serine peptidase inhibitor, Kunitz type 1)

4.1.2.

SPINT1 also known as hepatocyte growth factor activator inhibitor 1 (HAI1), is a type II transmembrane serine protease inhibitor [40]. Mechanistically, SPINT1 inhibits the activation of pro-hepatocyte growth factor (HGF); HGF normally binds to c-MET receptors and promotes mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (c-MET) phosphorylation. Loss of SPINT1 expression is associated with many malignant tumors including ovarian [41], prostate [42], and endometrial [43]. Conversely, an inverse relationship is seen in melanoma and hepatocellular malignancies [44]. It is hypothesized that SPINT1 overexpression participates in the process of hypoxia-induced tumor cell migration and invasion [45]. Similar to breast cancer [46], SPINT1 expression was also identified as one of the prognostic genes in bladder cancer [47] (Fig. 4).

UBE2E1 (Ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 E1)

4.1.3.

UBE2E1 is a member of ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 class; it serves a regulatory role in the cell by selectively ubiquitinating target proteins [48]. The ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS) regulates the cell cycle at key check-points by targeting cell cycle regulators for proteasome mediated degradation. UPS requires ubiquitin activating enzyme (E1), ubiquitin conjugating enzyme (E2) and ubiquitin ligases (E3) to work in concert [49]. UBE2E1 is essential for cell cycle progression [49] and furthermore, modulates HOX transcriptional factor gene expression by ubiquitinating histones [50]. Unlike normal tissue, high UBE2E1 expression has been reported in lymphoma, leukemia, melanoma, breast, brain, and bladder cancers [51,52]. The knockdown of UBE2E1 ssignificantly decreased bladder cancer cell proliferation and colony formation. Therefore, UBE2T may be considered as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for bladder cancer [53] (Fig. 4).

Rab25 (Rab25, member RAS oncogene family)

4.1.4.

Rab25 is a member of Rab11 subfamily and constitutes a small GTP-binding protein that is associated with tumor formation and progression. Rab25 augments cancer cell invasiveness through a β1 integrin/EGFR/VEGF-A/Snail signaling axis [54]. Rab25 mediates recycling of proteins from the endosome to the plasma membrane [55]. One of the key steps in cancer metastases is the ability to undergo epithelial to mesenchyme transition (EMT), where tumor cells can detach from epithelial cells through downregulation of factors in adherens junctions to gain invasion of the surrounding extracellular matrix. Snail transcription factor, a key factor that contributed to EMT through downregulation of E-Cadherin, is induced by Rab25 [56,57]. Thus, overexpression of Rab25 contributes to metastasis of bladder cancer through induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition and is linked to a poor prognosis for bladder carcinoma patients [58].

KPNB1 (Karyopherin subunit beta 1)

4.1.5.

Increasing evidence shows that nuclear trafficking plays a critical role in multiple cell processes, including mitosis, migration, and cell transformation. This role is tightly regulated by series of protein-protein interactions in which Karyopherin superfamily plays a crucial role. The nuclear import receptor karyopherin β1 is involved in the nuclear import of most proteins and in the regulation of multiple mitotic events [59,60]. KPNB1 was suggested as a new drug target oncogene in epithelial ovarian cancer [61], prostate cancer [60] and cancer cell lines [62]. It is thought to upregulate phosphorylation of regulator of chromosome condensation 1 (RCC1), which plays a pivotal role in mitosis and nuclear envelope assembly [63], and targeting of RCC1 could induce apoptosis and cell cycle arrest [64]. In addition, a positive feedback cycle between c-Myc and KPNB1 to further accelerate the nuclear transporting of c-Myc. This stimulates the expression of c-Myc and collectively favors cell cycle progression [60].

HDAC1 (Histone deacetylase 1)

4.1.6.

HDAC1 is responsible for the deacetylation of lysine residues at the N-terminal region of the core histones (H2A, H2B, H3 and H4) [65]. Histone deacetylation provides a tag for epigenetic repression and plays an important role in transcriptional regulation, cell cycle progression, and developmental events [66]. Histone modification through reversible acetylation is a crucial event in gene expression, which are controlled by a balance between acetyltransferase (HAT) and histone deacetylase (HDAC) [67]. By removing the acetyl group from lysine resides on histone tails, HDAC play a critical role in transcription regulation. As a class I, HDACs typically promote cellular proliferation in tumors, while inhibiting differentiation and apoptosis. Furthermore, tumors are comprised of a highly heterogenous population of cells, and a small amount of them possess the ability to regenerate tumors in vivo. These cells also known as cancer stem cells (CSCs) play essential role in metastasis and cancer relapse as they are often highly resistant to treatment [68]. Recent evidence points to critical role of HDACs overexpression in cancer stem cells and may serve as potential therapeutic targets in treatment resistant or recurrent disease [69,70].

Downregulated genes in MIBC

4.2.

NUP188 (Nucleoporin 188)

4.2.1.

Maintenance of normal cellular function relies on heavily regulated cellular signaling between the cytoplasm and nucleus. Nuclear pore complexes (NPCs), which are embedded within the nuclear envelope (NE), act as such gatekeepers [71]. Comprised of more than 30 proteins knowns as nucleoporins or Nups, it allows molecules to pass through the nucleus either through nuclear localization signal (NLS), or nuclear export signal (NES) motifs [72]. NUP188 is one of the nuclear pore complex genes found on the nuclear envelope and is required for chromosome alignment in mitosis [73] in addition to the nucleocytoplasmic transport. Misalignment can lead to mislocalization of proteins in subcellular compartments and can reduce their primary function. While downregulation of NUP188 has been found to be prevalent in bladder cancer in our meta-analysis, this may be due to reduction in nuclear translocation of tumor suppression proteins, and thus enhanced oncogenesis. Mutation of nucleoporin resulting in carcinogenesis is sporadic, while some evidence shows promotion of oncogenesis, the direct role of NPCs and NUP in carcinogenesis remains elusive [74].

IPO13 (Importin 13)

4.2.2.

Importins are involved in nucleocytoplasmic transport and play significant role in development, differentiation, and transformation [75]. Importin 13 plays a major role in meiosis because of the ability to import ubiquitin conjugating enzyme 9 (UBC9). In addition, UBC9 is a sole conjugating enzyme for sumoylation, which regulates protein function and plays role in sumoylation mediated cellular pathways [76]. Unlike ubiquitination that targets proteins for degradation through proteosome pathways, sumoylation has been implicated in the regulation of protein stability, and transcriptional activity [77]. Upregulation of UBC9 has been shown to be associated with increased tumor drug sensitivity, and upregulation of UBC9 reduces tumor growth. In line with the previously published research, our meta-analysis points to downregulation of IPO13, a critical importin for UBC9 transport [78]. With the downregulation of IPO3, and simultaneous reduction of Ubc9 transport across nuclear membrane, there is a potential for protein dysregulation. In fact, previous studies show that the lack of UBC9 correlates to bladder cancer progression via epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and stem cell-like population formation [79]. Unlike many other members of importin-β like gene family, it acts as an importer and an exporter of cargos and may play a global function in tumorigenesis and drug responsiveness through SUMOylation and nucleocytoplasmic transport [80].

NUP214 (Nucleoporin 214)

4.2.3.

NUP214 is one of the main components of the nuclear pore complex in eukaryotic cells [81]. It is a component of a nuclear pore complex with key role in mRNA nuclear export. Downregulation of NUP214 results in elevated mitotic indices, with downstream enhanced chromosomal abnormalities and apoptosis. NUP214 exhibits dual citizenship being relocated from the nuclear pores at interphase to the spindles at mitosis. In addition to aberrant transport and mitotic arrest, NUP214 interacts with nuclear transport receptors (NTR) such as importin-β, and potentially regulates MYC signaling activity [82]. NUP214 expression was validated in bladder cancer cell lines, and its downregulation was linked to the progression of bladder cancer [82]. There is, however, paucity of data regarding NUP214 and bladder cancer, so future studies are needed to further delineate the relationship.

The most significant genes were selected via ImaGEO and GeneMANIA analyses. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to gather and highlight the genetic background of MIBC. Limitations of the study include in the small number of the included studies as a result of the paucity of publicly available RNA-seq data and microarray. ImaGEO only accepts studies with GEO accession numbers along with raw files [23]. We did not validate the ImaGEO findings using secondary similar web-based bioinformatic tools such as ExAtlas and Network Analyst to reinforce the study’s relevance further. Other limitations might include the exclusion of animal and in vitro studies as a majority of studies in the field result from RNA-seq and microarray data that are recorded utilizing cancer cell lines and collected as part of basic research. Another limitation may be exclusion of other molecular methods studying gene expression, such as, immunohistochemistry, western blotting, polymerase chain reaction, immunocytochemistry, etc. To the best of our knowledge, no optimized approach is established to run meta-analysis utilizing all molecular gene expression methods. Only system-accepted studies were analyzed for enrichment and pathway analyses. It should also be noted that large proportions of potential papers were excluded due to a lack of GEO data. This is a significant limitation, and hence, the study is not an exhaustive representation of existing literature and might not be considered a deep meta-analysis of actual available data. It is worth mentioning that obtaining normal bladder tissue samples is a potential challenge for gene expression studies and collecting data using different platforms may be a significant drawback. Despite these limitations, our meta-analysis is first to elucidate the differentially expressed genes in MIBC compared with healthy controls and has highlighted several important genetic alterations as candidate targets with available GEO datasets.

Several well-known oncogenes and tumor suppressors are commonly mutated in bladder cancer. We found a few reports demonstrating the relationship between the elucidated genes and commonly mutated oncogenes/tumor suppressor genes. Inactivation of tumor suppressor genes such as PTEN, CDK2NA, p53 are reported to promote bladder cancer progression. Deacetylation of HDAC1 has been shown to increase PTEN acetylation, which is commonly mutated tumor suppressor gene [83]. HDAC inhibition reported to facilitate expression of cell-cycle regulators and proapoptotic proteins, by activating CDKN2A [84]. Repression of HDAC1 leads to allowing continued proliferation and differentiation factors by promoting p53 ubiquitination and degradation [65,66]. KPNB1 mediated nuclear import contribute to dampening p53 activity in DNA damage [85]. RAB25 enhances cancer invasiveness through activation of EGFR known as an oncogene [54].

Genomic profiling studies have revealed that bladder cancer can be allotted into 2 molecular subtypes referred to as luminal and basal with distinctive clinical behaviors [86]. We did not run subgroup analyses for molecular subtypes, and further delving into how genes differ across these molecular subtypes could be an interesting future analysis. However, we report a relationship between highlighted genes and molecular subtypes of bladder cancer. RAB25 was reported to be a luminal subtype marker [87]. IPO13 was found to be downregulated in basal tumors and upregulated in squamous cell carcinoma [88]. HDAC1 was expressed at higher levels in bladder cancer cell lines with a luminal subtype [89].

Potential gene set candidate KEGG pathway analysis revealed important pathways for the genes reported in this study (“ubiquitin mediated proteolysis,” “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum,” “transcriptional misregulation in cancer,” and etc., (Fig. 5). A signal for 26S proteasome dependent protein degradation via ubiquitin mediated proteolysis pathway is one of the important pathways for bladder cancer [90,91]. Disruption of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) environment by altered glycosylation, calcium depletion, oxidative stress, DNA damage resulting in ER stress followed by accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins in the ER. The role of ER stress response is well known in tumorigenesis and cancer progression, including bladder cancer [92,93]. Transcriptional misregulation in a cancer pathway was identified to be significant (translocations and inversions increase or deregulate transcription of the oncogene) and found to involve numerous genes in bladder cancer [94,95]. In support of previous reports, further evaluation of the aforementioned genes and pathways listed in our meta-analysis would be beneficial for researchers and clinicians. Our study does not conclude any clinical significances based on the meta-analysis findings. Functional validation of potential targets and pathways needs to be further emphasized in vitro and animal models. A better understanding of genes (RAB25 [58] and SPINT1 [47]) and associated pathways (ubiquitin mediated proteolysis [90], transcriptional misregulation in cancer [94]) with poorer clinical outcomes might be prioritized for future targeting in clinical settings. Exploration of these specific pathways and their interconnected genes may be the key to unlocking the hidden mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of MIBC.

Immunotherapy has produced groundbreaking successes in bladder cancer over the past decade. There is a critical need to identify clinically useful biomarkers, singly or in combination, to determine optimal immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapies for patients with MIBC. Future studies will require evaluation to determine the association between immune-related and reported genes and their clinical significance by establishing a prognostic index for bladder cancer outcomes. The outcomes of this systematic review may lay the groundwork for the development of future targeted therapies and novel biomarker detection for treatments, early detection, and possible prediction and prevention of future development of MIBC in clinical practice.

Conclusions

5.

Comprehensive experiments and wide-genome analyses should be undertaken to further evaluate the differential expression of these genes. Although many of the genes in our meta-analysis are yet to be studies in the context of bladder cancer, the outcomes of this systematic review lay the groundwork for the development of future targeted therapies and novel biomarker of bladder cancer.
