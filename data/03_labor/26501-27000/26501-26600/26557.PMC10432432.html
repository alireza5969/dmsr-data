Is There any Difference in Clinical Outcome between Open and Arthroscopic Treatment for Tennis Elbow? A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis

Abstract

There is considerable controversy regarding the optimal approach (open vs arthroscopic) of releasing and/or debridement for the treatment of tennis elbow (TE). The aim of this study was to determine the clinical outcomes of the two techniques by quantitatively synthesizing outcome data. The study was performed by searching the PubMed, EMBASE, Ovid, and Elsevier databases between January 1995 and April 2022 for a minimum follow‐up of 6 months. The searching strategy was “(tennis elbow [Title/Abstract] OR lateral epicondylitis [Title/Abstract]) AND (open [Title/Abstract] OR arthroscopic [Title/Abstract] OR release [Title/Abstract] OR debridement [Title/Abstract] OR surgery [Title/Abstract])”. The quality of each study was investigated using the Coleman Methodology Score. In total, 1411 (693 open, 718 arthroscopic) elbows in 1392 patients who underwent releasing and debridement for tennis elbow were identified. The mean Coleman Methodology Score for the included studies was 55.2 ± 8.6 (open: 55.0 ± 9.4, arthroscopic: 55.8 ± 8.2). Improved clinical results were achieved after treatment with either open or arthroscopic treatment. The surgical success rate was 95.6% in open surgery and 92.4% in arthroscopic management. The complication rates were 2.2% and 1.5% for open and arthroscopic procedures, respectively. Similar subjective and objective outcomes, and surgical success rate were observed in patients with both techniques. Patients who had undergone arthroscopic release seemed to return to work earlier (5.3 weeks vs 7.1 weeks). To draw more definite conclusions, high‐quality long‐term follow‐up randomized controlled trials are needed.

Introduction

Tennis Elbow (TE), also known as lateral epicondylitis (LE), affects 1% to 3% of the general population, and as much as 10% of patients are over 40 years old in high‐risk occupations.1 “Tennis Elbow” or “lateral epicondylitis” is now used to describe a degenerative tendinopathy characterized by pain at the lateral epicondyle, mostly involving extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB), common extensor origin (CEO), or extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL).2, 3, 4, 5 Although there is considerable controversy on the pathphysiological change of lateral epicondylitis, the most well‐accepted theory, proposed and popularized by Nirschl and Pettrone,6 attributed it to angiofibroblastic hyperplasia that predominantly affects the ECRB, CEO, and ECRL, especially the ECRB.

There was no consensus or golden standard on the treatment of TE. Usually, the initial management of TE should start with non‐operative treatment for 3–6 months, including rest, physical therapies,7 extracorporeal shockwave therapies (ESWT),8 laser therapies,9 and platelet‐rich plasma (PRP) injections. It was believed that with non‐operative treatment, the symptoms of TE can be relieved in approximately 90% of the patients.10 Regardless of the controversial satisfaction rate of conservative treatment, at least 5%–10% of patients who benefit little from conservative treatment were finally advised to undertake surgical interventions.11

In the literature, several open techniques were proposed, including extensor release and/or debridement, extensor fasciotomy, denervation of the lateral epicondyle, epicondylar resection with anconeus muscle transfer, and the Garden procedure with lengthening of the ECRB.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Among them, extensor release and/or debridement which was originally described as “excision of diseased tissue at the origin of the ECRB tendon”17 is one of the most well accepted techniques. Recently, arthroscopic treatment gained increasing popularity as a less invasive alternative of traditional open surgery. The elbow arthroscopy has been applied in several elbow pathologies including medial and lateral epicondylitis, primary/posttraumatic osteoarthritis, impingement, loose body, osteochondritis dissecans of the humeral capitellum and elbow instability.18, 19, 20, 21 Similar as the open technique, arthroscopic extensor release and/or debridement also became a more and more popular and accepted technique for the management of TE with increasing number of literatures comparing the clinical outcome of open and arthroscopic approach. The advantages of the arthroscopic technique had been reported by various studies and reviews, including better visualization and assessment of the joint, a faster and earlier rehabilitation, lower complications rate, and patients' preference compared with open technique when both techniques can be indicated.11, 22, 23, 24, 25 It seemed that the arthroscopic technique is a perfect replacement of conventional open technique whose application tuned more and more limited.

However, in a recent randomized controlled trial by Clark et al.,26 at 12 months follow‐up, an improvement of pain and function was achieved by both techniques. The difference of open and arthroscopic approaches was not significant in terms of visual analog pain score, patient‐rated tennis elbow evaluation (PRTEE) score, and grip strength. Some authors claimed that arthroscopic approaches would violate the extensor aponeurosis to gain an intra‐articular visualization.27 In addition, Kim et al. reported that for patients requiring muscle strength or having severe pain at work, open surgery would be more effective in the treatment of refractory lateral epicondylitis.28 In another systematic review by Riff et al.,29 a greater proportion of patients were pain‐free in the open group than in the arthroscopic group (70% vs 60%). Because of the anatomic approximation of critical nerve and vessels and indefinite clinical outcome, there is still controversy regarding the choice of optimal approach for the treatment of TE.

In the literature, open and arthroscopic release/debridement of TE were the most acknowledged techniques11, 13, 14, 16, 22, 30 for the management of TE, and some systematic reviews were published to compare the outcome of conventional open and arthroscopic surgery,27, 31, 32 but very few of them have quantitatively synthesized the data. With the increasing number of studies published recently, an update of this topic is needed. The purposes of this study are (i) to compare the clinical outcomes and (ii) evaluate the safety between open and arthroscopic treatment on TE by synthesizing the data of recent published articles.

Methods

Searching Strategy

A computerized literature search was initially performed in databases of PubMed, EMBASE, Ovid, and Elsevier for related studies published between January 1995 and April 2022. The searching strategy was “(tennis elbow [Title/Abstract] OR lateral epicondylitis [Title/Abstract]) AND (open [Title/Abstract] OR arthroscopic [Title/Abstract] OR release [Title/Abstract] OR debridement [Title/Abstract] OR surgery [Title/Abstract])” in the first step. Manual search of the reference lists of the included articles, relevant reviews, gray literature, and key journals were also performed.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) any type of clinical trial, such as randomized clinical trials (RCTs), RCTs with or without blindness, comparative studies and case series studies; 2) release or debridement of tennis elbow by means of open or arthroscopic techniques; 3) published in English from 1995.1 to 2022.4; 4) for a minimum of 6 months follow up; and 5) with objective outcome. The exclusion criteria included: 1) reviews, meta‐analyses, case reports, or technical notes; 2) concomitant treatment with release or debridement, such as concomitant platelet‐rich plasma injection, tendon repair or reconstruction; 3) study on patients with worker's compensation.

Data Extraction

Assessment of quality of studies was then performed by two experienced clinicians and two senior surgeons, respectively. Clinical studies were assessed with the 10 criteria of the Coleman methodology score (CMS) system.3 The CMS ranged from 0 to 100, with a score of 100 indicating a well‐designed study avoiding the influence of bias, confounding factors, and chances. Each article was scored independently by the two surgeons, and the average value was considered as the score of the article. Disagreement between reviewers was resolved by discussion.

The following basic characteristics were extracted from each study: first author's name, publication year, level of evidence (LOE), number of patients, follow‐up duration, and methods used for outcome measures. For treatment characteristics, following information was extracted: percentage of female, age, dominant arm involvement, duration of symptoms, preoperative status, follow‐up outcomes, surgical success rate, and complications. Studies were categorized into open group and arthroscopic group. And further comparison of follow‐up outcomes, time to back to work and self‐evaluations were performed between the two groups.

Surgical Technique

Arthroscopic technique: patients were placed in lateral decubitus position on the operating table with the affected elbow at 90° of flexion. The limb was prepared and draped free in the usual operative manner. Four arthroscopic portals were set up: soft spot portal, accessory posterolateral portal, proximal anteromedial portal, and proximal anterolateral portal. On introduction of the arthroscope through the soft spot portal, a full intra‐articular joint inspection was performed and intra‐articular pathology, especially radial‐capitilum joint synovitis, was addressed. Synovectomy was performed by shaver through the accessory posterolateral portal when necessary. The lateral epicondyle and common extensor origin were visualized using the anteromedial portal. A 30° arthroscope allows excellent visualization of the lateral capsule and ECRB tendon, as well as the lateral epicondyle itself. The ECRB tendon was identified, release and debridement were performed from the most distal level of pathologic involvement proximally to the insertion site on the lateral epicondyle by radiofrequency through anterolateral portal.33

Open technique: patients were placed in lateral decubitus position on the operating table with the affected elbow at 90° of flexion. The limb was prepared and draped free in the usual operative manner. A curved incision, approximately 4 cm long, was made directly over the lateral epicondyle. The extensor origin was exposed, The ECRB tendon was identified, release and debridement were performed from the most distal level of pathologic involvement proximally to the insertion site on the lateral epicondyle. Decortication of the bone at the attachment site at the lateral epicondyle was performed with an osteotome. The subcutaneous tissue and skin were sutured and a compression bandage applied.30

Risk of Bias Assessment

Two authors assessed the risk of bias in each study based on the Cochrane Collaboration risk of bias independently. The methodologic quality of the studies was assessed according to the following items: selection of participants, allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and items subjective evaluations. Each item was scored as low risk, high risk, and unclear risk. To solve any disagreement, a consensus was reached after discussion or the involvement of a third author.

Statistical Analysis

The RevMan statistical software (RevMan version 5.1) was used to analyze the data extracted from the eligible articles. In this study, VAS and complication rate data was analyzed to provide the risk ratios (RR) and the 95% confidence intervals (CI). The VAS and complication rate data were expressed as means and standard deviations (SD), which were then pooled to a weighted mean difference (WMD) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity was examined by the I2 statistic. When heterogeneity was not statistically significant (p > 0.1, I2 < 50%), the fixed‐effect model was used. When heterogeneity was statistically significant (p ≤ 0.1, I2 ≥ 50%), the source of heterogeneity was analyzed, and the random‐effects model was used for analysis. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Methodologic Quality

The Coleman Methodology Score (CMS) for the included studies varied from 39 to 75, with a mean score of 55.2 ± 8.6. The mean CMS for studies concerning open and arthroscopic release were 55.0 ± 9.4 and 55.8 ± 8.2 respectively. Four studies were constructed as prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs), 11 retrospective case control studies, and 22 case series studies.

Surgical Procedure and Rehabilitation Protocol

Most of the studies concentrated on one operative technique only, with an “adequate” to “fair” description of the process according to the CMS system. Most of the open surgical techniques were based on the technique described by Nirschl and Pettrone6 with different minor modifications. The arthroscopic techniques were similar to the technique described by Baker at el.34

The descriptions of postoperative rehabilitation were inadequate in 12 studies according to CMS, and varied in the other 24 studies. In general, the rehabilitation protocol for open and arthroscopic technique were similar, with heavy or repetitive work initiated at about 4 to 8 weeks and sports allowed about 2 to 3 months after surgery. However, removal of sling and start of early ROM exercises and daily activities were a few days earlier in the arthroscopic group.

Outcome Measures

The most frequently used outcome measures were assessments of pain, elbow function and power, with the use of visual analog scale (VAS) pain score, Disability of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) score, Mayo Clinic Elbow Performance Index (MEPI), and grip strength. Other less frequently used outcome scores were also reported, including Andrews–Carson score, Roles–Maudsley score, Morrey score, Numeric Pain Intensity Scale (NPIS), American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons Elbow (ASES‐E) score, Patient‐Rated Tennis Elbow evaluation (PRTEE), and Upper Extremity Functional Scale (UEFS). Surgical success rate, time of back to work, self‐evaluation, and complications were also mentioned. Clinical outcome measurements were summarized in Table 2.

Pain

The preoperative average VAS for open and arthroscopic techniques were 6.47 (of 282 patients in nine studies) and 7.23 (of 577 patients in 10 studies), respectively. At 1‐year follow‐up, the VAS decreased to 2.67 in open group (143 patients in four studies), and 1.20 in arthroscopic group (245 patients in eight studies). At 2‐year follow‐up, the VAS was 1.1 in open group (96 patients in three studies), and 1.1 in arthroscopic group (269 patients in eight studies) (Figure 2).

Functional Scores

The preoperative DASH for open and arthroscopic techniques were 58.5 (155 patients in four studies) and 48.6 (142 patients in five studies) respectively. At 1‐year follow‐up, the DASH improved to 20.0 in open group (156 patients in four studies), and 23.8 in arthroscopic group (119 patients in four studies). At 2‐year follow‐up, the DASH was reported in only one study for open group (34 patients, 22.8 ± 15.9), and 36.6 in arthroscopic group (54 patients in two studies) (Figure 2).

Quick‐DASH was used in two studies of open surgery30, 35 and two studies of arthroscopic surgery22, 36; UEFS was performed one study of arthroscopic surgery.15 ASES was applied in one study of open surgery,37 one study of arthroscopic surgery38; NPIS was measured in one study of open surgery37; Nirschl score was used one study open surgery.37

The preoperative average MEPS for open and arthroscopic techniques were 55.4 (143 patients in four studies) and 55.5 (of 190 patients in six studies). The MEPS was 83.9 in open group (110 patients in two studies), and 94.3 in arthroscopic group (85 patients in three studies) at 1‐year follow‐up. The MEPS was reported in only one study for open surgery (96 patients, 95.7 ± 6.8), and 92.9 in arthroscopic group (240 patients in seven studies) at 2‐year follow‐up (Figure 2).

Only one study of open surgery (n = 37) reported the preoperative PRTEE score and 1‐year follow‐up result (56.3 ± 3.1, 26.4 ± 4.4, p < 0.05). Four studies of arthroscopic technique reported the average PRTEE in 157 patients which was 56.7 preoperatively and 16.3 at 1‐year follow‐up (Table 3).

Surgical Successful Rate

Only four of the 36 studies mentioned surgical successful rate. Surgical success was defined as not having poor outcomes,22 with no need for further operative intervention,39 without reoperation,22 and not considering surgery due to significant pain with activities of daily living8 in four different studies. The mean surgical successful rate was 95.6% (of 118 patients in two studies) for open group and 92.4% (of 360 patients in three studies) for arthroscopic group.

Time to Back to Work

The mean average time to back to work was 7.1 weeks (from 4 weeks to 5 months) for open technique and 5.3 weeks (from 6 days to 4 months) for arthroscopic technique. However, there was no detailed documentation of the workload or work category.

Patient‐Reported Evaluation

In open group, 97.7% (341/349) of patients and 90.6% (203/224) of patients in arthroscopic group considered the operation to be “excellent/good” or felt “very satisfied/satisfied” postoperatively for both techniques.

Complications

The complication rates were 2.2% (14/625) and 1.6% (12/758) for open and arthroscopic procedures, respectively, based on studies with clear statements of complications or no complications. Commonly reported complications for both techniques were neurologic problems (open: 3, arthroscopic: 5), limitation of ROM (open: 4, arthroscopic: 3), infection (open: 2, arthroscopic: 1) and wound issues (open: 2 synovial fistulae, arthroscopic: 1 mild erythema). Of the eight cases of neurologic problems, there were three local numb cases, one transient forearm paresthesia,8 two radial tunnel syndrome, one tourniquet neuropraxia, and one radial nerve palsy which was fully recovered in 12th month after surgery and one paresthesia. The prognosis of the limitation of ROM, infection, and wound issues were not described in any included study.

Meta‐Analysis of VAS and Complication Rates

The comparison of VAS and complication rate revealed that the difference between the open and arthroscopic technique was insignificant. The mean difference of VAS comparison was 0.13 (range, −0.28–0.55) (Figure 3), and the odds ratio of complication rate was 2.02 (range, 0.53–7.76) (Figure 4).

Discussion

The most important finding of this systematic review was the satisfied and similar long‐term subjective and objective outcomes, surgical success rate, and complication rate of both open and arthroscopic techniques for tennis elbow release and debridement. Patients who had undergone arthroscopic release seemed to return to work earlier. However, more high‐level comparative studies were needed to provide more evidence.

Subjective Evaluation of both Approaches

In this review, the improvement of elbow function was achieved in both groups. The improvement of VAS, DASH, and MEPS score seems to be similar for both techniques. However, we consider this impression not reliable because of the relative low quality of the included studies (mean CMS: open 52.4 vs arthroscopic 51.7). In addition, many articles were excluded due to absence of specific outcomes, which could lead to the unavoidable selection bias. Studies with enhanced methodologic quality were needed to verify this impression.

A high surgical success rate (open: 95.6%, arthroscopic: 92.4%) was accomplished for both techniques. However, different definitions of surgical success were miscellaneous among the included articles. Surgical success was defined as not having poor outcomes,22 with no need for further operative intervention,39 without reoperation,22 and considering surgery due to significant pain with activities of daily living8 in four different studies. Except for these four criteria, in the literature the Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) is another proposed criterion.23 As far as we know, there is no consensus on the definition of surgical success. More evidence is needed to reach a standard for the assessments of surgical techniques for TE.

A shorter mean average time to return to work was achieved by arthroscopic techniques (5.3 weeks) than open techniques (7.1 weeks). This might be attributed to the minimal invasion of the arthroscopic techniques. In this review, the mean average time to return to work for open technique was similar with previously reported 6.6 weeks. However, the time for arthroscopic technique was far longer than previously reported 9.6 days, which was based on two early studies40, 41 around the year 2000. It should be noted that the detailed workload and category of working were not described in the included articles, so there was some bias when interpreting these results.

Which Procedure Should we Choose?

We tried to find a superiority in one of the methods regarding relieving pain, improving functionality, accelerating return to work, and the safety according to the number of complications. However, in this study, comparable complication rates (2.2% and 1.5%) for open and arthroscopic were reported. Similar prevalence of neurologic problems, infection, and limitation of ROM were reported for both groups. Although historically, the open procedures were supposed to have a higher complication rate,42 this systematic review did not present a similar trend. In our study, the neurologic issues were the most common complications for both techniques. In addition, some recent studies11, 13, 22, 26, 28, 43 have suggested the neurologic issues might be more prevalent and diverse than the literature reported. As more attention was focused on these complications, many studies8, 44 had modification of techniques and cautions. A previous quantitative review42 had compared the function outcomes among studies, and concluded that there was a dearth of quality evidence available to be able to advocate one operative technique over another. To make an update, in the review we included more recent studies with reliable and more commonly used outcome measurements.

Strengths and Limitations

The strength of this study is the update of recent studies, and the meta‐analysis of VAS and complication rates. The main limitation of this review is the heterogeneous quality of included studies. Many methodological deficiencies existed in the studies: 1) most of the studies included were retrospective studies and case series, which provided the weakest form of evidence and unavoidable selection bias; 2) the heterogeneous surgical technique and rehabilitation protocol among different institutions were used in the articles, and difference evaluation system was used, which made the data synthesis difficult; 3) descriptions of demographic and clinical outcome data were not standard or absent in some studies. More RCTs or high‐level comparative studies with high methodologic quality were needed to evaluate the effectiveness of open and arthroscopic treatment in patients with tennis elbow.

Conclusion

This review suggests good long‐term results for both open and arthroscopic techniques for tennis elbow treatment. Similar subjective and objective outcomes, surgical success rate, and complication rate were achieved by both techniques. Patients who had undergone arthroscopic release seemed to return to work earlier. So, from our perspective, if failure cannot be completely avoided, the best way to treat this TE is the one with the most minimal surgical trauma among methods with equal failure rate. Although arthroscopic approach yielded an earlier return to work, it's still less economically favored compared with open approach, so the cost‐efficiency should also be taken into consideration in clinical practice.
