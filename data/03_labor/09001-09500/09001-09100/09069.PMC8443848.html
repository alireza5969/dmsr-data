Candidate Quality Measures for Orthopaedic Surgery Outreach Trips: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Introduction

Up to 30% of the global burden of disease is secondary to surgical conditions, the majority of which falls upon those in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC). Ensuring that the quality of care delivered during outreach trips to address these conditions is foundational. Limited work has been done to develop and implement tools to assess and improve the quality of care for these trips. The purpose of this study was to identify candidate quality measures that address orthopaedic surgery outreach trips in LMIC.

Methods:

We performed a systematic review of MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and other databases to identify candidate quality measures relevant to orthopaedic surgery outreach to LMICs. Quality measures were then categorized by system management, sustainability, or both system management and sustainability according to the Bido et al. framework and structures, processes, and outcomes according to the Donabedian domains.

Results:

Our initial search yielded 3,891 articles, 22 of which met inclusion criteria. Seventy-nine candidate quality measures were identified. With regard to the Bido et al. framework, 55 of 79 (70%) were related to system management, eight (10%) were related to sustainability, and 16 (20%) were related to both system management and sustainability. According to Donabedian domains, 43 of 79 (54%) were structure measures, 25 (32%) were process measures, and eleven (14%) were outcome measures.

Discussion:

Quality measures addressing orthopaedic surgery outreach trips are lacking in quantity and breadth, limiting the ability to assess and improve the safety and quality of care provided. The candidate quality measures identified disproportionately focus on systems management and structures, with few related to sustainability and few addressing outcomes. Patients receiving care on outreach trips would benefit from the implementation of the measures identified in this review, and from the development of quality measures that capture all domains of care and emphasize outcomes.

III

Introduction

Up to 30% of the global burden of disease is secondary to surgical conditions.1,2 This surgical burden disproportionately falls on those in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).3,4 Injuries (e.g. traffic injuries, falls) alone account for about five million deaths annually and an estimated $8 trillion of cumulative gross domestic product will be lost secondary to decreased economic productivity due to injury over the next fifteen years.3,4 Understanding that the role of the orthopaedic surgeon on a surgical outreach trip can be substantial in addressing this burden, there has been an increase in the number and amount of resources invested in trips to LMICs addressing musculoskeletal conditions.5–7

While the intent of outreach trips is to provide high-quality care to patients, without a method by which to evaluate safety and quality, this cannot be ensured. As such, quality measures are tools used to evaluate and inform improvement of quality of care provided, by assessing the structures, processes, and outcomes of healthcare delivery. For example, a common quality measure used in orthopaedic surgery assesses the process of administering preoperative antibiotics within one hour of surgical incision. In essence, quality measures are used to measure aspects of care that are deemed important for care, and when deficient, can identify areas to direct improvement initiatives. For example, a quality measure assessing reoperation rates within 90 days of an index surgery can inform a health system’s improvement efforts addressing an abnormally high reoperation rate. As such, these measures are used to evaluate quality of care, and at the same time, serve to direct efforts to areas in which opportunities for improvement exist. Quality measures have been developed and implemented in countries with robust healthcare infrastructures and resources, however their application during outreach trips to LMICs has not been adequately studied. It is possible that quality measures established and applied in developed countries, may not apply to LMICs. For example, a quality measure addressing the percentage of women age 50–85 years receiving a bone mineral density test or treatment for osteoporosis after a distal radius fracture may not be feasible or may be too burdensome or costly to patients and healthcare systems in LMIC.8,9 Conversely, there may be quality measures that can be implemented during outreach trips to LMICs that may not inform improvement in a developed country (e.g. presence of a back-up generator, completion of a needs assessment, and having appropriate sterilization equipment).

In the United States, agencies like the National Quality Forum (NQF) and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) develop quality measures while agencies like the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) use quality measures to assess quality of care and inform improvement efforts. While the World Health Organization (WHO), a specialized agency of the United Nations, has developed normative guidelines for maternal and fetal health that incorporates quality measures10 and global initiatives like the Surgical Safety Checklist11, there are no centralized agencies developing quality measures that can be applied to orthopaedic surgery outreach in LMICs. The purpose of this study was to identify candidate quality measures that could address orthopaedic surgery outreach trips to LMICs and determine what aspects and domains of care these quality measures address.

Methods

We used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Statement methodology to conduct our review (Figure 1).12 We searched MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases (August 2019) to identify candidate quality measures using explicit search algorithms (Appendix A), designed with the assistance of a research librarian (CS). Individual search strategies included, but were not limited to the following search terms: quality assessment, quality indicators, quality improvement, developing countries, surgery, and orthopaedic surgery. Additional search terms were also included to specify articles relevant to orthopaedic surgery and articles in the English language. No date range limit was applied. These same search terms were also used to review the World Health Organization (WHO) and Centers for Disease Control (CDC) websites for clinical practice guidelines and articles containing candidate quality measures pertaining to orthopaedic surgery outreach trips to LMIC.

The results of the database searches were uploaded to Covidence (Melbourne, Australia), a Cochrane-sanctioned web-based application that facilitates the screening and analyzing of studies for systematic reviews. Initial screening by title was independently performed by two authors (MOP, DJM). The abstracts and text of included articles were then reviewed by both authors. The references of included articles were reviewed for any missing sources. Discrepancies between these two authors were reconciled with the review of a third author (LMS).Quality measures addressing surgical outreach to LMICs were extracted from the remaining articles. Quality measures from WHO and CDC databases were reviewed and included if relevant to orthopaedic surgery care delivered on outreach trips to LMIC.

Candidate quality measures that were included were pooled and classified according to the conceptual framework laid out by Bido et al.13, and further by Donabedian’s model of structure, process, and/or outcome14 (Figure 2 and Table 1). The model set forth by Bido et al. aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of outreach trips to help quantify and improve the impact of trips on the host community(Figure 2).13 The Donabedian domains are components of the three-pronged paradigm commonly used to evaluate quality measures (Table 1).14

Results

Our initial search yielded 3,891 articles. Six hundred and twenty-nine duplicate articles were excluded. Twenty-two articles containing candidate quality measures from the literature search (n = 15) and from other databases (n = 7) were included for final evaluation (Figure 1). No current validated quality measures for the evaluation of quality of orthopaedic surgery on surgical outreach trips to LMIC were found. Seventy-nine candidate quality measures were identified that met inclusion criteria (Table 2–4).

Of the 79 measures identified, 55 of 79 (70%) were related to systems management (e.g. availability of a continuous supply of running water), eight of 79 (10%) were related to sustainability (e.g. presence of an educational component for local surgeons), and 16 of 79 (20%) were related to both systems management and sustainability (e.g. presence of a system for reporting adverse events), as classified according to Bido et al. domains (Table 2–4). With regard to the Donabedian framework, 43 of 79 (54%) were structure measures (e.g. access to a CT scanner), 25 of 79 (32%) were process measures (e.g. completion of a sponge and instrument count at the end of each surgical procedure), and eleven of 79 (14%) were outcome measures (e.g. number of perioperative deaths within 30 days of a surgical procedure).

Seven (32%) articles were published by the WHO. Nine (41%) articles were retrospective reviews of surgical outreach trips – all but one article reported on measures from one country. Three (14%) articles were survey studies and qualitative analyses. Two (9%) studies were cost effective analyses (one evaluating one trip to one country, the second evaluating 14 trips to 7 countries). One (5%) study was a review of the evidence. Eighty (50%) quality measures of 159 were published by the WHO. (This number of quality measures is discrepant from prior as many measures were mentioned in multiple sources – 79 is the number of unique quality measures, however 159 were mentioned in the literature). All quality measures were published in or after 2003, with a majority (83%) being published during or after 2011. Of note, no date range was applied to the search criteria. No outcome or sustainability only measures were published prior to 2011. From 2003 to 2019, outcome measures and sustainability measures showed the greatest increase in number (Figures 3 and 4).

Discussion

As the global burden of musculoskeletal injury increases and the number of orthopaedic surgery outreach trips to LMIC grows, we must ensure that the care provided is safe and of high quality. While quality measure development and implementation are growing in countries with robust healthcare systems, we found few and less robust candidate quality measures to apply to care provided by orthopaedic surgeons on outreach trips to LMICs. While this portfolio of measures serves as a foundation to benchmark and inform quality improvement on orthopaedic surgery outreach trips, we also identify an opportunity to validate these current candidate quality measures and develop measures to address areas inadequately measured (e.g. outcomes).

Notable in our results is the unequal distribution of measure domain (e.g. 10% of measures are related to sustainability, 20% of measures are related to sustainability and systems management, and 14% of measures are outcome measures), the heterogeneity of study type and quality, and the timing distribution of measure publication. Sustainability is a central tenant of global health and critical to the provision of care that develops the local healthcare system36,37 (e.g. providing care without taking the long-term effects into account may ultimately do more harm than good). Future development of quality measures should incorporate sustainability and capacity building aspects as these ensure care is delivered in a patient-centered manner that also benefits both the individual patients and the surrounding community. In addition to quality measures focusing on the delivery of care after volunteers have left, quality measures can also be ‘localized’ for specific contexts to ensure measures are applicable to specific populations and communities.38 For example, a measure assessing the presence of an operative microscope would be inappropriate for a rural healthcare setting that lacks the infrastructure for complex reconstruction. Moreover, candidate measures that address outcomes are lacking. While structure and process measures are important in understanding the resources, infrastructure, and procedures of a health system, outcome measures reflect the results of care as received by patients. While occasionally process measures serve as a proxy for patient outcomes, outcomes as experienced by patients are foundational to patient-centered care. Logically, measures addressing structures and systems management are easier to develop and assess, while measures addressing outcomes and sustainability are more difficult. For example, it is easier to understand and measure the importance of what we can see (i.e. an x-ray machine) and it is more difficult to understand and measure a patient’s function six months after surgery, given time, location, and resource constraints. Although understandably difficult, future work should strive to increase the study quality and evaluation and reporting of sustainability and outcome quality measures, as these are important to building the ability for the local community to provide care, and ensure patient safety.6,7,39

The results of this systematic review are better understood and supported in the context of the literature relating to other surgical specialties. Saluja et al. conducted a systematic review on the quality of essential surgical care provided in LMIC.39 While they identified 354 studies for inclusion, almost 80% of these were single-center, 80% were observational in nature, and about 50% did not specify the level of facility studied. Notably 52% of included studies did not specify their follow-up period. Although not specifically classified according to Donabedian domain, they noted great variation in which outcomes were reported. In accordance with our work, we posit that the current measurement of quality of care provided on outreach trips in LMIC is varied and can be improved upon with higher quality studies and the application of balanced quality measures that ensure safe and equitable care delivery.

The limitations of this study should be acknowledged. We developed a comprehensive set of search terms, however we recognize that we may have failed to capture all relevant articles and quality measures. To address this, we manually reviewed the citations used in each included article to capture any missing articles. We also manually reviewed the WHO and CDC websites to capture literature and quality measures meeting inclusion criteria that were not captured from the database searches. Of note, the author lists of the articles under review were not blinded. This is not an available feature on Covidence and not standard per systematic review, however it is possible this may have introduced some bias. We recognize as well that the definition of LMICs is constantly evolving. We used data from the World Bank (extracted at the same time as our search was conducted) which defines countries into categories based upon gross national income per capita. Quality measures typically undergo a process of validation by experts that agree that a measure addresses a concept that is associated with improved patient care, and are also typically tested to ensure their implementation actually leads to an improved patient outcome. The candidate quality measures described in our review serve as a foundation for this process. Lastly, we descriptively reviewed the quality of studies included, with a majority of them being publications from the WHO or retrospective reviews of measures from a single country or trip. The lack of validated and high quality studies is notable and serves to highlight areas for future study and publication.

In summary, quality measures addressing care provided by orthopaedic surgeons on outreach trips to LMIC are lacking in quantity and breadth. As the number of surgeons conducting outreach trips and the resources invested in such trips rise, quality measures are needed to ensure high-quality care is being provided.
