The relationship between hormonal contraception and cervical dysplasia/cancer controlling for human papillomavirus infection: a systematic review

Abstract

Objective:

Studies on the effect of long-term use of combined oral contraceptives (COCs) on cervical dysplasia and/or cancer risk have been inconsistent. Less is known about the effects of other forms of hormonal contraception (HC). We examine whether HC use increases the risk of incident cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) 2, 3 and/or cancer after accounting for preexisting human papillomavirus (HPV) infection.

Study Design:

Systematic review of prospective studies on HC use as risk factor for cervical dysplasia with HPV infection documented prior to outcome assessment including PubMed and EMBASE records between January 2000 and February 2020 (Prospero #CRD42019130725).

Results:

Among 9 eligible studies, seven described recency and type of HC use and therefore comprise the primary analysis; two studies limit comparisons to ever versus never use and are summarized separately. All seven studies explored the relationship between oral contraceptive (OC) use and cervical dysplasia/cancer incidence: two found increased risk (adjusted odds ratio, aOR=1.5-2.7), one found no association but decreased risk when restricted to women with persistent HPV (adjusted hazard ratio=0.5), and four found no association. None of the seven studies differentiated between COC and progestin-only pills (POPs) by use recency or duration. The only study that included injectable progestin-only contraception (DMPA) found increased CIN3 incidence among current versus never users (aOR=1.6). The one study that included Norplant found no association. Two studies included intrauterine device (IUD) use, but did not differentiate between hormonal and copper IUDs, and found no association.

Conclusion:

We found no consistent evidence that OC use is associated with increased risk for cervical dysplasia/cancer after controlling for HPV infection. There were too few studies of progestin-only injectables, implants or IUDs to assess their effect on cervical dysplasia/cancer risk.

Implications:

Use of single self-reported HC measures and insufficient distinction by hormonal constituent cloud our understanding of whether some HCs increase risk for cervical cancer. Methodologically rigorous studies with distinct HCs measured as time-varying exposures are needed to inform cervical cancer prevention efforts and improve our understanding of cervical cancer etiology.

Introduction

1.0

Cervical cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer among women globally, with an estimated age-adjusted mortality rate of 7.3 per 100,000 person years in 2020 [1]. Oncogenic strains, also known as high-risk strains, of human papillomavirus (hrHPV), such as types 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 45, 52 and 58, are a necessary but not sufficient cause of cervical cancer [2]. The risk of cervical cancer can be reduced by vaccination against hrHPV types and/or screening to detect and treat cervical dysplasia early [3], but not all women are vaccinated or screened. While these prevention methods are effective, outreach activities may be hard to implement in settings with weak healthcare systems including many lower- and middle-income countries [4]. Understanding exposures that increase risk of progression of hrHPV infection to cervical dysplasia and/or cancer can help target outreach activities to increase coverage of vaccination and/or screening.

One exposure hypothesized to increase the risk of progression of hrHPV infection to cervical dysplasia/cancer is combined oral contraceptive (COC) use [5]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified COCs as a cause of cervical cancer [6]. A meta-analysis by the International Collaboration of Epidemiological Studies of Cervical Cancer et al. from 2007 of individual data from 24 epidemiologic studies found that among current oral contraceptive (OC) users, the risk of invasive cervical cancer increased with duration of use (adjusted odds ratio, aOR=1.90, 95% CI 1.69, 2.13, for individuals with five or more years of use compared to never users) [7], suggesting that OCs may promote HPV-induced carcinogenesis. However, the extent to which these findings are affected by confounding factors, such as sexual and lifestyle behaviors including frequency of screening, is not clear [8]. Further, these studies did not differentiate between COCs and progestin-only OCs [5, 7], and the extent to which other hormonal contraceptives (HC) affect risk of progression of hrHPV infection to cervical dysplasia/cancer is also not clear.

There are biologically plausible ways by which HC methods could affect cervical cancer risk. The cervix is an estrogen-responsive tissue, highly sensitive to steroid hormone contraceptive usage [9, 10]. Estrogen has been suggested as a synergistic factor that is required to induce HPV-dependent cervical carcinogenesis [9]. Steroid hormones could increase the transcription of important HPV oncogenes, disrupting normal cell cycles and ultimately leading to cervical cancer [11]. However, different HC methods contain different progestins with or without estrogens in different combinations, concentrations and formulations, and have different routes of administration (oral, intravaginal, intrauterine, in a muscle, or underneath the skin). This results in different biological effects on endogenous hormone levels, local and systemic immune responses [12], and the cervicovaginal microbiome composition [13].

In addition to the 2007 meta-analysis showing an association between current OC use and invasive cervical cancer [7], a systematic review by Smith et al. in 2003 [8] and Gierisch et al. in 2013 [10] found that long-term OC use moderately increases the risk of cervical cancer in women. None of the previous reviews restricted analyses to prospective studies, and Gierisch et al. did not restrict analysis to HPV-positive women [10]. The 2007 meta-analysis included nine studies of progestin-only injectable use and found an elevated risk (aOR of 1.22, 95% CI 1.01, 1.46) among individuals using the injectable for 5 or more years compared to never users [7], but none of the reviews included other forms of HC. To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review to examine whether use of different forms of HC increases the risk of incident cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) 2, 3 and/or invasive cervical cancer (ICC; collectively referred to as CIN2/3+) in prospective studies of HPV-positive women or studies that adjusted for baseline HPV status. Results are summarized by HC type, recency and/or duration of HC use and study quality. Given how widely HCs are used globally [14], understanding how each HC method affects the risk of HPV-related cervical dysplasia and/or cancer among users is important.

Material and methods

2.0

The protocol was registered a priori with PROSPERO (record #CRD42019130725) and follows PRISMA guidelines [15]. Articles were identified using key term searches of two electronic databases: PubMed and EMBASE (Supplemental Table 1), and by reviewing the references of previous systematic reviews.

Inclusion/ Exclusion Criteria

2.1

Included articles were peer reviewed, published in English between January 1, 2000 and February 1, 2020, and tested for HPV status at baseline, prior to CIN2/3+ among users of HC methods compared with users of other methods or no contraceptive method. We restricted our search to articles published after January 1, 2000 as HC formulations and HPV tests have changed over time [16, 17]. All HC methods were included apart from emergency contraception, as emergency contraception is used in the short-term only and is typically used in combination with other contraceptive methods [18]. Articles were excluded if they: 1) did not have a prospective study design that adjusted for preexisting HPV prior to cytology/histology, 2) did not assess CIN2/3+ as confirmed by histology, 3) only assessed HPV or abnormal cytology persistence or recurrence, 4) did not adjust for confounders, or 5) were review articles. Two independent reviewers (E.A. and H.E.J., K.J.M., E.L.G. or L.R.) screened each abstract or full article using Rayyan software [19]; a third reviewer who had not previously reviewed the study resolved discrepancies.

Data Extraction

2.2

One reviewer (E.A.) extracted data, with independent review for accuracy by another reviewer (H.E.J.). Extracted information included: study design, participant age group, method of HPV assessment, type of HC method used, duration of HC use, exposure comparison group(s), cervical lesion outcome measured, outcome comparison group(s), confounders adjusted for, effect estimate(s), variance and significance level.

Study Quality

2.3

Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale [20], adapted to examine the relationship between HC use and progression of HPV infection to CIN2/3+ based on a previous adaptation [21]. Adaptations to the scale included standardized criteria specific to these studies as described in Supplemental Table 2. Two reviewers (E.A. and H.E.J.) independently rated study quality; discrepancies were resolved by discussion between reviewers.

Data Synthesis

2.4

Our primary outcome was CIN2/3+ incidence in women who had a confirmed HPV infection prior to outcome assessment. We aimed to examine findings by HC method used (i.e. OCs, preferably differentiating between COCs and progestin-only pills, POPs; progestin-only injectables; intrauterine devices (IUDs), preferably including progestin-only IUDs only; and progestin only implants). We used forest plots to examine variation in associations with OC use by duration and endpoint (CIN 2/3, CIN3, ICC). We decided post-hoc to divide eligible studies into primary and secondary articles based on HC use measures. Studies that included assessment of HC type, duration and/or currency of use were considered primary studies and are described in Table 1. Studies that categorized women into ever versus never users with no indication of recency or duration were considered at high risk for bias, and thus were considered secondary studies described in Supplemental Table 3.

Results

3.0

Our key term search resulted in 746 unique articles; 625 articles were excluded during abstract screening, and 121 full articles were retrieved and reviewed for inclusion/exclusion criteria (Figure 1). Three prospective studies were identified from one previous systematic review and considered for inclusion [7]. A total of 9 articles met the inclusion criteria [22-30]. Seven of the 9 eligible studies were primary studies (Table 1) [22-24, 26-29] and the other two secondary studies (Supplemental Table 3) [25, 30].

All seven of the primary studies included OC use as an exposure (Table 1) [22-24, 26-29]. None of the seven studies clearly differentiated between COC and POP use; one included a comparison of ever versus never POP users, without specifying whether POPs were excluded from their analysis of OC use by duration [24]. Two studies included IUD use (without specifying whether the IUDs were copper IUDs or progestin-only IUDs) [26, 28], one progestin-only injectable (depot medroxyprogesterone acetate, DMPA) use [23], and one progestin-only implant (Norplant) use [23]. All studies measured HC use through self-report at a single point in time. The participant age range varied: four studies included women aged 18 years to >60 years [22, 23, 27, 29]; one 35-70 years [28]; one 20-29 years [26]; and in one study the age range was not specified (median 30-34 years) [24]. Three studies were prospective cohort studies [26, 27, 29]; three nested-case control studies [22, 24, 28]; and one was a secondary analysis of a randomized control trial [23]. Four studies were conducted in Europe [24, 26, 28, 29], two in the US [22, 23] and one in Korea [27]. The exposure comparison group in five studies was never users of the HC method being analyzed for each method included [23, 24, 26-28]; two studies compared current users to non-users [22, 29].

Control for human papillomavirus infection

3.1

The presence of an HPV infection prior to outcome assessment was determined once (at baseline) in six studies [22-24, 27-29] and twice in one study (two years prior to baseline and at baseline) [26]. The length of follow up between the most recent HPV test prior to outcome assessment varied from 2 years [23] to 13 years [26]. Six of the seven studies focused analyses on oncogenic strains only (hrHPV) [22, 23, 26-29], while one included both low risk (lr) and hrHPV types in their definition of HPV-positivity [24]. There was also variation in whether studies restricted or adjusted for HPV status. Four of the studies restricted to women who had tested positive for any hrHPV type [22, 23, 26, 29], and one to women who had tested positive for a combination of low-risk (lr) and hrHPV types [24]. One of these four studies restricted the analysis in bivariate models, but adjusted for hrHPV results in multivariable models [29]. One study adjusted for baseline hrHPV in the main analysis, as well as performed a sub-analysis using controls who were hrHPV-positive at baseline [28]. The seventh study adjusted for baseline hrHPV status in the analysis, without restricting the analysis to hrHPV-positive women [27]. There was also variation in the tests used to determine HPV status; four used Hybrid Capture II only [22, 26, 27, 29], one used Hybrid Capture II and PCR [23], one used L1 consensus PCR amplification with the MY09/MY11 primer [24], and one used serology for antibodies to the HPV capsid protein L1 [28].

Control for confounding variables

3.2

Confounders included in analyses varied (Table 1). Seven studies adjusted for smoking status [22-24, 26-29], of which six adjusted for age and education [22, 23, 26-29]. Four studies also adjusted for sexual behaviors [22, 24, 27, 29], two for menopausal status [27, 28]; two for marital status [27, 28]; two for body mass index [27, 28]; one for physical activity [28]; and one for having a sexually transmitted infection other than HPV during the follow-up period [28].

Study quality assessment and risk of bias

3.3

The seven primary studies were judged to be of medium quality (range 4.5-5.5 out of 7, Supplemental Table 2). No study assessed HC use as a time-varying exposure and all seven based HC measures on self-report.

Cervical lesion outcome

3.4

One study classified CIN2, CIN3/Carcinoma in situ (CIS) and invasive cervical cancer (ICC) as the primary endpoints [28], one CIN3+ [26], three CIN3 [22-24], and two combined CIN2/3 into a single endpoint [27, 29].

Hormonal contraceptive exposures

3.5

Oral contraceptives

3.5.1

Table 1 displays results from the seven primary studies, highlighting mixed results. For OC use, two of the seven studies found a significantly increased odds, one for CIN3/CIS [28] and the other for CIN2/3 [27]. One study found no association among the entire sample but found a decreased CIN3+ incidence for a subsample with persistent HPV-infection, which was defined as positive for the same hrHPV type two years prior to baseline and at baseline [26]. Four studies found no associations with CIN 2/3 or CIN3 incidence (Figure 2) [22-24, 29]. The two secondary studies which compared ever to never users of OCs found no association between ever and never use (Supplemental Table 3) [25, 30], and the only study to assess progestin-only pills separately found no association between ever and never users for CIN3 among women who had tested HPV-positive at baseline (OR=1.29, 95% CI 0.48, 3.44) [24].

One of the two studies, that found an elevated risk of CIN2/3+, Roura et al. 2015, followed 1,827 women in 10 European countries with a median follow up duration of nine years, and took two approaches to adjusting for hrHPV status [28]. In their nested case control analysis, after adjusting for hrHPV serology at baseline, CIN3/CIS odds increased with duration of OC use (p=0.01) with the highest odds found in the 10-14 years use category compared to women who never used OC (aOR=1.6; 95% CI 1.0, 2.5) [28]. In analysis with invasive cervical cancer as the endpoint, the odds also increased with duration of use, although the results were not statistically significant, with the highest risks found in the 5-9 years (aOR=1.6; 95% CI 0.7, 3.7) and 10-14 years (aOR=1.5; 95% CI 0.7, 3.1) use categories [28]. Their second approach to adjustment for HPV using controls who had tested hrHPV-positive at baseline had similar risk estimates for ICC (5-9 years of use aOR=2.1, 95% CI 0.8, 5.5; 10-14 years aOR=1.8, 95% CI 0.8, 4.2) and for CIN 3/CIS (10-14 years aOR=1.6, 95% CI 0.9, 2.6) [28].

The second study, led by Oh et al. 2016, followed 678 women in Korea for up to 6 years and found increased odds of CIN2/3 among OC users [27]. The adjusted odds of CIN2/3 were 1.98 (95% CI 1.07, 3.69) times greater among women who were OC users (both current and past users) compared to never HC users, after adjusting for smoking, age, body mass index, marital status, menopausal status, alcohol consumption and baseline hrHPV status [27]. This study also found that longer OC use increased the odds of developing CIN2/3. The adjusted odds of developing CIN2/3 were 2.71 (95% CI 1.11, 6.59) times greater among women who had used OCs for at least 20 months compared to women who had never used OCs [27].

One study, led by Jensen et al. 2013, conducted among 1,353 hrHPV positive women in Denmark who were followed for 13 years found no increased CIN3+ incidence among OC users [26]. However, among persistent hrHPV positive women (n=312) who tested positive for the same hrHPV type at baseline and two years prior to baseline, they found decreased CIN3+ incidence. The adjusted hazard ratio for incident CIN3+ for OC users compared to never HC users (n=28) was 0.47 (95% CI 0.23, 0.96) for ≤ 4 years of use, 0.58 (95% CI 0.30, 1.12) for 5-9 years of use, and 0.53 (95% CI 0.25, 1.13) for ≥ 10 years of use [26].

Progestin-only injection

3.5.2

Only one of the seven primary studies, Castle et al. 2005, included participants who used injectable contraception (DMPA) [23]. This study enrolled 3,126 women in the US, who were hrHPV-positive at baseline of whom 515 were current and 353 former injectable users with a follow up duration of two years. Current DMPA users (aOR=1.6; 95% CI 1.2, 2.1) had higher odds of CIN3 than never DMPA users, with no difference observed for former DMPA users (aOR=1.1, 95% CI 0.8, 1.5) [23].

Intrauterine devices

3.5.3

Two studies also included IUD users: Roura et al. 2015 (with 1,125 participants in this analysis, of whom 340 had ever used the IUD) and Jensen et al. 2013 (with 1,165 participants of whom 384 had ever used the IUD). Both studies did not find an association between IUD use and incident cervical dysplasia and/or cancer [26, 28]. Neither study described which type of IUD participants were using (a copper IUD or a hormonal levonorgestrel-containing IUD), making it impossible to assess the individual effect of the hormonal IUD.

Norplant implant

3.5.4

Only the study led by Castle et al. 2005 included hormonal implants (Norplant). Among 3,126 hrHPV-positive enrolled women from the US of whom 75 were current and 124 former Norplant users, neither current (aOR=0.7; 95% CI 0.2, 2.0) nor former use (aOR=1.2; 95% CI 0.6, 2.5) was associated with the odds of CIN3 compared to never use [23].

Discussion:

4.0

We identified only seven studies with a CIN2/3+ incidence outcome that included duration/timing of HC use and accounted for HPV status (assessed between 2 and 13 years prior to outcome assessment). We found no consistent evidence that OC use is associated with increased risk for cervical dysplasia/cancer after controlling for HPV infection. Two studies found increased odds [27, 28], one study found no association, but decreased incidence when restricted to women with persistent hrHPV [26], and four studies found no association [22-24, 29]. None of the seven studies clearly differentiated between combined and progestin-only pills by recency/duration of use (one study did compare ever versus never users of progestin-only pills) [24]. There were too few studies of progestin-only injectables, implants or IUDs to assess their effect on cervical dysplasia/cancer risk. The one study, led by Castle et al. 2005, that included injectable progestin-only contraception (DMPA) found increased odds among current versus never users [23], as had been found in a previous meta-analysis [7]. This same study was the one study that included Norplant and found no association [23]. Two studies, Jensen et al. 2013 and Roura et al. 2015, assessed IUD use, without differentiating between hormonal and copper IUD, and did not find an association [26, 28].

A strength of the present systematic review is that findings are restricted to prospective studies that included HPV testing prior to outcome assessment. However, while five of the seven studies – Jensen et al. 2013, Castle et al. 2002 and 2005, Syrjänen et al. 2006, and Deacon et al. 2000 – restricted the analysis to women who had tested HPV-positive [22-24, 26, 29], one, Deacon et al. 2013, included non-oncogenic HPV types [24]. Further, the time of follow-up between baseline HPV status and outcome varied and all but one study tested for HPV at baseline only; the study by Jensen et al. 2013 which restricted analyses to individuals with two consecutive hrHPV-positive tests with the same oncogenic HPV type, two years apart, found a decreased risk for CIN3+ by duration of OC use [26]. This variation in length of follow-up and the use of a single baseline HPV measure may have contributed to the inconsistent findings for OCs. Other methodological challenges include not distinguishing methods by hormonal constituents (e.g. between combined and progestin-only OCs and between copper and progestin-only IUDs); relying on self-reported data for contraceptive use, which has known measurement error [31], and not measuring HC use as a time-varying exposure. Given that many contraceptive users switch methods over their lifetime [32], measuring HC use at a single point in time likely biases estimates. The use of “never users” as a comparison group may also increase the likelihood of residual confounding as this group may represent individuals with a wide array of different sexual and health behaviors. Finally, two studies – led by Oh et al. 2016 and Syrjänen et al. 2006 [27, 29] – specified CIN2/3 as an endpoint, when CIN2 has a strong chance of spontaneously correcting itself [33], thus serving as a less meaningful endpoint than CIN3+. Future studies should consider ways to strengthen study design such as through use of large health registries that link prescription data and clinical diagnosis, and account for HPV-vaccination and immunosuppression status.

Given the methodological challenges in the studies identified, especially the use of a single self-reported measure for HC use and lack of distinction between hormonal formulations, the findings are inconclusive. We found no clear evidence for an association between OC use and CIN3+, and insufficient data on other HC methods to assess. Methodologically rigorous studies examining the associations between HC use and progression of hrHPV to CIN3+ should examine specific formulations that are distinguished by type, dose, and administration schedule of hormonal constituents. Further, given the extent to which contraceptive use changes over time [32], HC use needs to be measured as a time-varying exposure. In 2019, IUDs (progestin-only and copper), oral contraceptives, progestin-only injectable, and progestin-only implants accounted for 17%, 16%, 8% and 2% of contraception methods used worldwide, respectively [14]. Given how widely HCs are used by women globally to prevent pregnancy (with subsequent reductions in maternal mortality) [34], understanding how different HC methods affect the risk of CIN3+ is important both for targeting screening and HPV vaccination, and for improving understanding of cervical cancer etiology.
