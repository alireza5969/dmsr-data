Abstract

Toxicological and epidemiological studies implicate exposure to dithiocarbamate (DTC) fungicides in adverse health outcomes. However, there is limited information about human exposure to these chemicals. This systematic review determined to which extent human populations worldwide, including children, pregnant women, and adults, are exposed environmentally or occupationally to DTC pesticides and how these exposures compare to the NHANES 2003–2008 population, using urinary ETU data as an outcome measure. PubMed, Embase, and SciFinder were searched using the keywords “ethylenethiourea” or CAS No.: 96-45-7, and urine or urinary. Duplicates and irrelevant studies were removed from the search results based on predetermined exclusion criteria. This screening process identified 17 relevant papers. One additional paper was found independent of this search. Data from studies were extracted using a pre-established data collection form. Ten, two, and five manuscripts reported urinary levels in environmentally exposed adults, children, and pregnant women, respectively. Median ETU levels ranged from 0.15 to 4.7 μg/g creatinine in adults (1994–2017), 0.24 to 0.83 μg/g creatinine in children (2011), and 2.6 to 5.24 ng/ml in pregnant women (2011). Eight studies reported urinary ETU levels in mostly agriculturally exposed populations, with median ETU levels ranging from 0.42 to 49.6 μg/g creatinine (1999–2011). With one exception, all studies were conducted between 1994 to 2011. ETU levels in the NHANES 2003–2008 population appeared to be generally lower than most studies identified in this review. This finding suggests that, historically, DTC fungicide exposures in the general population of high-income countries, such as the US, were low, whereas agricultural populations may have experienced higher exposure. Unfortunately, more recent exposure data are missing, especially in countries where DTC pesticides are not being phased out.

Introduction

1.

Dithiocarbamates (DTCs) are broad-spectrum fungicides often used in the agricultural setting to control fungal growth on plants. Dithiocarbamates make up a chemical group of fungicides that contain a carbamate group and two sulfur atoms. Examples of these fungicides include Ziram, Maneb, and Mancozeb. DTC fungicides are typically used in vineyards, potato farms, and the floricultural industry (USGS, n.d.). The global success of DTC fungicides is, in part, due to their multiple modes of fungicidal action (Thind and Hollomon, 2018). Briefly, DTCs release ethylene- bis- isothiocyanate sulfide, which disrupts metabolic processes by chelating metals from metal-containing enzymes and binds to sulfhydryl groups of cytoplasmic and mitochondrial enzymes. Unfortunately, this fungicide class requires application before the fungal infection because it is not taken up by the target crop and has no curative action (Thind and Hollomon, 2018). The use of Mancozeb and Ziram has remained steady over the last decade in the contiguous United States (US). However, other DTC fungicides, such as Maneb, are being phased out in the US. Because of public health concerns, Mancozeb lost its approval in the European Union as an active ingredient in plant protection products effective January 4, 2021, and its use is being phased out (Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/2087, 2020).

Occupational exposure to DTC fungicides can occur in an agricultural setting by dermal or inhalation exposure (Ekman et al., 2013; Kurttio and Savolainen, 1990; Savolainen et al., 1989). In addition, workers and the general population may be exposed environmentally to DTC fungicides or their transformation products, primarily via the diet. DTC fungicides are readily transformed in aqueous environments, soils, and aquatic organisms to form ethylenethiourea (ETU) and other transformation products (Downing, 2000). ETU is also a major metabolite formed in plants and mammals (Downing, 2000; EFSA, 2019) and during the processing of foodstuffs with residual DTC (Lentzarizos, 1990). Because of the rapid transformation of DTC fungicides, DTC fungicides and ETU levels in foodstuffs can be measured indirectly as CS2 (EFSA, 2019). Levels of DTC fungicides, measured as total CS2, that exceed the toxicological threshold level in food are rare, according to the EFSA (EFSA, 2011). However, the lack of fungicide-specific analytical methods represents a limitation of current risk assessments of DTC fungicides (EFSA et al., 2020). According to the US Food and Drug Administration, ETU is present in infant and toddler foods with an average detection frequency of only 6% (FDA, 2019). Therefore, the health concerns associated with dietary exposure to DTC fungicides are generally below health-based guidance values (EFSA et al., 2020; EPA, 2005).

Exposure to DTC fungicides or ETU can lead to thyroid issues, such as altered thyroid hormone levels or thyroid weight (EPA, 2005). A study in the Philippines reported an association between the size of solitary thyroid nodules and levels of ETU in the blood of plantation workers (Panganiban et al., 2004). In a study from Costa Rica, the children of women exposed to DTC fungicides were more likely to have lower respiratory tract infections or wheezing episodes (Mora et al., 2020). Higher ETU levels are also associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, such as poorer verbal learning abilities (van Wendel de Joode et al., 2016) or lower social-emotional scores in girls (Mora et al., 2018). ETU itself is classified as a carcinogen (Mutic et al., 2017). Occupational exposure to several fungicides, such as Maneb and Ziram, has been linked to a higher risk for neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease (Costello et al., 2009; Fitzmaurice et al., 2014; Moher et al., 2009; Rhodes et al., 2013; Ritz et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2011). The risk associated with these exposures is particularly high in those exposed at a young age (Costello et al., 2009) or those with chronic exposure (Meco et al., 1994). However, not all studies report an association between environmental DTC fungicide exposure and Parkinson’s disease (Brouwer et al., 2017). The inhibition of aldehyde dehydrogenase, mitochondrial dysfunction, or oxidative stress have been implicated as the mechanistic link between DTC fungicides and neurodegenerative diseases (Domico et al., 2007; Ockleford et al., 2017).

The available evidence implicates occupational and environmental exposure to DTC fungicides in adverse health outcomes. However, it remains unknown if human exposure to these fungicides or their transformation products is widespread. Although blood ETU levels are potentially a better biomarker of DTC exposure (Panganiban et al., 2004), urinary ETU levels are typically measured as a biomarker of exposure. Therefore, we adopted the Population, Exposure, Comparator, and Outcomes (PECO) framework (Morgan et al., 2018) to review human DTC exposures systematically. The objective was to determine to which extent human populations worldwide, including children, pregnant women, and adults (Population), are exposed environmentally or occupationally to DTC pesticides (Exposure), and how these exposures compare to the NHANES 2003–2008 population (Comparators), using urinary ETU detection frequencies and levels as an outcome measure (Outcomes).

Materials and Methods

2.

Search strategy

2.1.

The search strategy was adapted from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) best-practice guidelines. The search and data extraction process is summarized in the flow chart shown in Fig. 1 (Moher et al., 2009; Smith and Do, 2008). We systematically searched three electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, and SciFinder) on June 15, 2020, to find potentially relevant articles. The following keywords were used to search the databases: ethylenethiourea, urine or urinary, and CAS No. 96-45-7 to identify human biomonitoring studies reporting urinary ETU levels. A full description of the search strategy and exact search entries can be found in the Supplementary Material. All search results were transferred to a single EndNote X9 library. The library was screened for duplicates, and all duplicates were moved to a separate group. External to this search process, an additional study was found and deemed relevant. This study was added to the library for extraction and analysis along with the rest of the studies.

Study selection and data extraction

2.2.

Two investigators screened the remaining references independently based on pre-established screening criteria, and clearly irrelevant studies were removed. A third investigator acted as a tie-breaker, if necessary. Studies excluded from further consideration at this stage included in vitro studies, animal studies, modeling studies, conference proceedings, and patents. This screening identified 70 potentially relevant studies that were further assessed for relevance based on the following exclusion criteria: small numbers of study participants (n ≤ 10), sampling time unclear or not reported, controlled exposures, toxicokinetic studies, data not presented in a usable format, an article written in a language other than English, no ETU data reported, abstract only, a full-text reference not available, and duplicate studies reporting overlapping data (in this instance, only the most data-rich paper was included).

In the last step of the systematic review, data were extracted with a pre-established data abstraction form in Microsoft Excel (see Supplementary Material). The reviewer team classified data as environmental ETU exposure in adults, ETU exposures in children and adolescents, ETU exposures in pregnant women, and occupational exposures to DTC fungicides (Tables 1–4). Control groups from occupational exposure studies are also presented in Table 1, with urinary ETU levels reported only for data obtained at the beginning of the study. The discussion of the data focuses on the geometric mean (GM) and median ETU levels because of the small sample size of most studies; however, mean levels are discussed when GM or median levels were not reported. Detection frequencies are also summarized; however, these data need to be interpreted with caution because of the wide range of detection limits reported and the different analytical procedures used across studies.

Extraction of urinary ETU data from NHANES

2.3.

NHANES survey

2.3.1.

Following our PECO statement, the present study used urinary ETU data from NHANES 2003–2008 as a comparator, as appropriate. In 2003–2008, NHANES, a nationally representative survey of the non-institutionalized U.S. population, measured urinary ETU levels in samples from 4,613 US adults. This survey is administered by the National Center for Health Statistics at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). In addition to information about the demographics, socioeconomic status, diet, lifestyle, and medical conditions of study participants, NHANES includes extensive health examinations and performs laboratory tests, including the measurement of ETU and other pesticide residues in biospecimens (e.g., urine) collected from study participants. NHANES has been approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent is obtained from all participants. Detailed information about NHANES is described on the CDC website (CDC, n.d.).

Analysis of ETU in urine samples from NHANES

2.3.2.

As part of NHANES, spot urine samples were collected from 2003–2008, shipped to the National Center for Environmental Health at the CDC, and stored at −20 °C until analysis. Urinary concentrations of ETU were quantified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Details regarding the analytical method used by NHANES to quantify urinary ETU have been reported previously (CDC, 2009). Briefly, urine samples (800 μl) were spiked with ETU-d4 (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Tewksbury, MA, USA) as the internal standard and freeze-dried with a 96-well VirTis Genesis Lyophilizer (Gardiner, NY, USA). Samples were extracted with two aliquots of dichloromethane (900 μl) and concentrated to dryness. The residue was redissolved in acetonitrile (300 μl), and the acetonitrile was evaporated. The dried extracts were reconstituted with methanol (100 μl) and transferred to sample vials. Subsequently, ETU levels were measured by LC-MS/MS in the positive ion mode with selective reaction monitoring (SRM) using m/z 103→44 as quantification ion and m/z 103→86 as confirmation ion for ETU and m/z 107→48 for ETU-d4. ETU levels were quantified against a matrix-matched calibration curve, with a lower limit of detection (LLOD) of 0.21 ng/ml.

Analysis of NHANES data

2.3.3.

Following the NHANES Analytic Guidelines, we applied sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units in all analyses to account for the unequal probability of selection, oversampling of certain subpopulations, and non-response adjustment. For the source of the data, see the Supplementary Material. Detection frequencies and levels of urinary ETU are reported for US adults and children (Tables 1 and 2). To account for urine dilution, we also report urinary creatinine adjusted ETU levels.

Statistical analysis

2.4.

A two-sample t-test was used to compare a log-transformed measurement between occupational vs. environmental adults or environmental exposure of adults vs. children on RStudio (version 1.4.1106) with R (version 4.0.5). The R code of the statistical analysis is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Results and Discussion

3.

ETU levels in environmentally exposed adult populations worldwide

3.1.

A total of 10 studies were identified that reported urinary ETU levels in adult populations exposed environmentally to DTC fungicides, with studies being conducted between 1994/1995 to 2017 (Table 1). This number includes data from control groups reported in 6 studies of occupational exposures to DTC fungicides. In addition to these studies, data from NHANES 2003–2008 were included in Table 1. Detection frequencies in these studies, when reported, ranged from 0.9 to 100%. Median urinary ETU levels ranged from 0.12 ng/ml in the NHANES population to 1.4 ng/ml in a smaller US population from North Carolina in 2012 (Arcury et al., 2018b). Creatinine adjusted median ETU levels ranged from 0.15 μg/g creatinine in a cohort of Swedish adolescent samples in 2017 (Norén et al., 2020) to 4.7 μg/g creatinine in a population from rural Italy studied in 1994–1995 (Aprea et al., 1996). NHANES 2003–2008 is the largest adult population for which urinary ETU data are available (N=4,613). The detection frequency in this large cohort was only 8.19 %, and the GM urinary ETU level was 0.15 ng/ml. The media ETU levels in the NHANES population are the lowest median levels observed across all studies summarized in Table 1, which suggests that the exposure of the general US population to DTC fungicides was relatively low between 2003–2008. This observation is not entirely surprising because most other study populations lived near agricultural areas with known DTC use and are more likely to be exposed.

Two early human biomonitoring studies investigated DTC pesticide exposures in Italy. ETU levels in an Italian population (N=97) living close to agricultural areas reported detection frequencies ranging from 24–37% and GM levels of 5.3 μg/g creatinine in 1994–1995 (Aprea et al., 1996). Lower detection frequencies were observed in urban populations from Italy (N=167), with a detection frequency of 24 % and GM ETU levels of 2.1 μg/g creatinine. Diet was a major route of exposure in the rural and urban populations. Smoking and drinking wine were associated with higher urinary levels of ETU, consistent with early studies reporting the presence of ETU and DTCs in cigarettes and wine (Aprea et al., 1997; Casanova and Guichon, 1988). In the rural population, urinary ETU levels were higher in men due to their higher consumption of wine.

Approximately five years later, another study in Northern Italy detected ETU in 59% of urine samples (n=95), with a GM of 0.62 μg/g creatinine (Colosio et al., 2006). This study found no link between smoking and alcohol intake and ETU levels, consistent with an exposure of the general Italian population to DTC contaminated foodstuffs. The lack of an association between alcohol consumption and urinary ETU levels in this cohort is likely due to the variability of ETU levels in different alcoholic drinks, as suggested by several earlier studies of ETU levels in alcoholic beverages (Aprea et al., 1997; Casanova and Guichon, 1988).

Control populations from occupational exposure studies performed from 2002 to 2003 in Finland (mean, 0.9 ± 2.0 μg/g creatinine), Italy (mean, 1.3 ± 1.5 μg/g creatinine), the Netherlands (mean, 1.2 ± 1.1 μg/g creatinine), and Bulgaria (mean. 1.4 ± 2.9 μg/g creatinine) (Fustinoni et al., 2008) reported similar urinary ETU levels as the study in Northern Italy discussed above (Colosio et al., 2006). One exception was a study of Bulgarian greenhouse workers in 2004, which found mean ETU levels of 24.9 ± 66.5 μg/g creatinine (N=45) in the control group in 2004 (Fustinoni et al., 2008). Unfortunately, the authors could not link these comparatively high ETU levels in the Bulgarian cohort to other pesticide exposure, for example, in a second job or gardening activities.

A study of temporal trends of ETU levels in a Swedish population aged 17–21 years old suggests a near-ubiquitous exposure to DTC fungicides, with detection frequencies ranging from 97 to 99 % from 2000 to 2017 (Norén et al., 2020). This finding is not surprising because this study reported the lowest detection limit of all studies listed in Table 1 (0.016 ng/ml vs. 0.07 to 1 ng/ml). In addition, ETU levels in Sweden decreased from 2000 to 2017, but the change by year was small (3.9 %/year). Temporal changes in ETU levels were also reported in a small cohort of Latino or Hispanic immigrant men from North Carolina, US, in 2012 and 2013 (Arcury et al., 2018a). This cohort’s mean urinary ETU levels were 5.01 ± 4.22 μg/g creatinine in 2012 (N=129) vs. 0.4 ± 1.65 μg/g creatinine in 2013 (N=78), a difference that reached statistical significance. Similarly, the ETU detection frequency decreased from 20.2 % in 2012 to 5.1 % in 2013 in this cohort. Similarly, another study from North Carolina, US, reported a decrease in the levels and detection frequency of ETU in urine samples from Latina nonfarm workers, with median levels of 1.4 ng/ml in 2012 (N=55) and 0.9 ng/ml in 2013 (N=28) (Arcury et al., 2018b). Overall, the urinary ETU levels in these two populations are higher than the NHANES data, suggesting disparities in the exposure to DTC fungicides in the U.S. population. Unfortunately, the low detection frequencies of ETU in the NHANES population do not allow an in-depth investigation of racial/ethnic or other exposure disparities in the U.S. population.

Several factors that influence environmental DTC exposures have been proposed. These include changes in the need for fungicide treatment due to weather conditions and a changing global climate, regional dietary habits, food sources (e.g., homegrown vegetable, organic diet, etc.), drinking or well water contamination, and eating habits (Aprea et al., 1996); however, adequately powered, systematic studies of these factors have not been reported to date. There is evidence of dermal and inhalation exposure to DTCs (e.g., due to aerial application) in some populations with non-occupational exposure (Aprea et al., 1996; van Wendel de Joode et al., 2014). Moreover, farming practices and pesticide application techniques and modalities (e.g., pesticide drift), living close to farmland, and take-home exposures influence exposures in agricultural areas resulted in inter-individual and regional differences in DTC exposures (Panuwet et al., 2008; Runkle et al., 2013). As mentioned above, these various factors likely contribute to higher urinary ETU levels in some agricultural study populations summarized in Table 1. Importantly, climate change is expected to result in the increased use of pesticides, including fungicides, globally (Delcour et al., 2015).

ETU levels in environmentally exposed children

3.2.

Only two studies were identified that reported urinary ETU rates in children (Table 2). In addition, data from NHANES 2003–2008 are included in Table 2 for children aged 6–18. These studies collected samples between 2003 and 2011. The detection frequency in the NHANES population (N=2,708) was 10.64%. This observation indicates that the risk of exposure to DTC fungicides is comparatively low in children in the general US population. The GM and median ETU levels in U.S. children from the NHANES 2003–2008 population were 0.12 ng/ml and 0.15 ng/ ml, similar to the values observed in U.S. adults (Table 1). ETU levels across all studies listed in Tables 1 and 2 tended to be lower in children than adults (Fig. 2). In contrast, exposures of children to other environmental contaminants are typically higher than adults because of physiological (e.g., higher food and water intake and higher respiratory rates in children, and age-dependent differences in the disposition of environmental contaminants) and behavioral differences (e.g., hand-to-mouth activities) (EPA, 2011). Thus, the comparison between ETU levels in children vs. adult populations in Fig. 2 needs to be interpreted with caution as only a small number of studies reporting urinary ETU levels in children are available. In addition, except for the NHANES population, studies listed in Table 2 were performed in areas with likely DTC exposure.

ETU levels in other populations of children appeared to be higher than the NHANES cohort; however, a direct comparison across studies is impossible because ETU levels were expressed using different units. For example, a study in vine growing areas of France reported median ETU levels of 0.42 μg/g creatinine in samples collected in March-April 2011 from school children (N=96) (Raherison et al., 2019). Higher median (0.66 μg/g creatinine) ETU concentrations were observed in urine samples from the same children collected in May-June 2011 due to the pesticide application in vineyards. A considerable percentage of the ETU levels in French children (23.7% and 38.5% in the early and late sampling period) were above a value of 0.974 μg/g creatinine, the 95th percentile in the NHANES 2007–2008 cohort (N=382) (Raherison et al., 2019). A study in children living near banana plantations and plantain farms in Talamanca, Costa Rica, reported ETU detection frequencies of 96–97% (van Wendel de Joode et al., 2016). Median urinary ETU levels were 1.2 ng/ml (N=140), almost an order of magnitude higher than median ETU levels in NHANES children.

ETU levels in environmentally exposed pregnant women worldwide

3.3.

Five studies were identified that reported urinary ETU rates in pregnant populations (Table 3). These studies were performed between 1999/2001 and 2011. Two studies included pregnant women who were also occupationally exposed to pesticides, including fungicides (Handal et al., 2016; Runkle et al., 2013). The lowest ETU detection frequencies were reported for a population of pregnant women from California, US, in 1999–2001 (Castorina et al., 2010). The other three studies reported urinary ETU levels in pregnant women from Costa Rica and Ecuador and had detection frequencies > 96%. Median urinary ETU levels, when reported, ranged from 2.6 ng/ml to 5.24 ng/ml. These ETU levels are higher than levels reported for other adult populations with environmental DTC exposure (Table 1). This observation is consistent with these study populations living in areas with significant DTC use, such as near banana plantations in Costa Rica or working in the cut-flower industry in Ecuador.

A study of pregnant women living near banana plantations in Costa Rica provided insights into exposures to Mancozeb. The GM and median urinary ETU levels in this population (N=445) were 2.9 ng/ml, and 2.9 ng/ml, respectively (van Wendel de Joode et al., 2014). ETU levels remained relatively constant from the first to the third trimester. Several factors were associated with higher ETU levels. They included residential proximity to a banana plantation, washing work clothing from family members working in the plantations (i.e., take-home exposure), working in agriculture, being an immigrant, and diet, especially the frequent consumption of rice and beans. The exposures to Mancozeb result from the large-scale aerial application of this fungicide to protect the banana plants (Mora et al., 2018; van Wendel de Joode et al., 2014). Notably, the chronic estimated daily intake (EDI) of ETU was above the reference dose (RfD = 0.12 μg ETU/kg/day) for 72% of the women and above the chronic population-adjusted dose (cPAD = 0.18 μg/kg/day) for 23% of the women (van Wendel de Joode et al., 2014).

Median urinary ETU levels in pregnant women working in the rose industry and control workers from Ecuador ranged from 3.63 to 3.96 ng/ml and, thus, were also higher than levels reported for environmentally exposed adults (Table 1) (Handal et al., 2016). These levels did not change significantly from enrollment to delivery and, surprisingly, were not associated with work in the rose industry. The high levels of ETU in this cohort of pregnant women are possibly due to dietary exposures, especially from DTC-treated potatoes (Handal et al., 2016). High urinary ETU levels compared to the general population were also observed in two small cohorts of pregnant nursery workers from Apopka (N=5) and Pierson (N=13), Florida, US, with GM ETU levels of 7.79 μg/g creatinine and 1.68 μg/g creatinine, respectively (Runkle et al., 2013).

ETU levels in occupationally exposed populations

3.4.

A total of eight studies reported urinary ETU concentrations in adult populations occupationally exposed to DTC fungicides (Table 4). These studies were performed from 1999 to 2013. A previously published, scoping review of DTC exposures also identified eight studies (Montesano and Wang, 2011); however, most of these studies are not included in this systematic review because they did not meet our inclusion criteria (e.g., sampling time not reported or report duplicate data) (Colosio et al., 2002; Colosio et al., 2003; Colosio et al., 2007; Corsini et al., 2005; Steenland et al., 1997). The exposure in the populations identified in the present review typically came from agricultural work (e.g., work in the floricultural industry) and the manufacturing of fungicides. In addition to dietary exposures, these populations can have significant dermal or inhalation exposure to DTC pesticides that correlate well with urinary ETU levels (Kurttio and Savolainen, 1990; Savolainen et al., 1989). For example, in Lombardy, Italy, a study demonstrated that urinary ETU increased in pesticide applicators in vineyards following occupational exposure to DTC fungicides (i.e., Mancozeb) (Fustinoni et al., 2008). However, as discussed below, DTC fungicide exposures were influenced by specific work tasks, for example, the availability of engineering controls and personal protective equipment and the risk perception of agricultural workers. Collectively, the available exposure studies suggest that proper industrial hygiene practices can reduce exposure to DTC fungicide, as evidenced by lower urinary ETU levels. However, engineering controls or personal protective equipment (PPE) did not necessarily protect workers from DTC exposures, consistent with a recent criticism that PPE may offer limited protection against agricultural pesticide exposure (Garrigou et al., 2020).

Detection frequencies of ETU in these occupationally exposed populations, when reported, ranged from not detected to 100%. ETU was detected in all urine samples in a study of male pesticide applicators from vineyards in Italy (Mandic-Rajcevic et al., 2018) and female farmworkers from Florida, US (Runkle et al., 2013). The high detection frequency in both studies can be attributed to the relatively low limit of detection (LOD), especially in the study from Italy (LOD = 0.005 ng/ml).

Comparing ETU levels in occupational vs. environmental populations suggests that occupational exposures result in higher urinary ETU levels (Fig. 2). However, this comparison was only statistically significant for GM values adjusted for creatinine levels. Several studies reported that occupational exposures are higher than the respective control populations (Panganiban et al., 2004; Runkle et al., 2013) and are typically associated with the application of fungicides (Fustinoni et al., 2008). However, this trend was not observed by all studies. For example, a study of Latino and Hispanic immigrant farmworkers and nonfarm workers from North Carolina, US, found higher urinary ETU levels in the nonfarm worker than the farmworker population in 2012 (1.74 μg/g creatinine in 2012 vs. 0.86 μg/g creatinine in 2012) (Arcury et al., 2018a). The same study observed the opposite trend in 2013, with farmworkers having higher ETU concentrations than nonfarm workers (0.86 μg/g creatinine vs. 0.40 mg/g creatinine, respectively). A second study from North Carolina, US, reported similar ETU levels in female Latina farmworkers and nonfarm workers. Nonfarm workers had an average median concentration of 1.15 ng/ml, and farmworkers had 1.4 ng/ml (Arcury et al., 2018b).

Greenhouse farmers from Bulgaria had the highest ETU levels across all studies included in this systematic review. The mean ETU levels in samples collected in 2004 from this cohort were 182 ± 264 μg/g creatinine (N=55) (Fustinoni et al., 2008). The high exposure in this population is likely due to dermal exposure resulting from poor occupational hygiene procedures. This study also investigated DTC fungicide exposures in floriculture workers from the Netherlands in 2003 (Fustinoni et al., 2008). These workers did not apply fungicides and were mainly exposed to DTC fungicides through re-entry activities. Their mean ETU levels (1.1 ± 0.6 μg/g creatinine, N=42) were similar to the levels of the control population (1.2 ± 1.1 μg/g creatinine, N=42). This finding suggests that the re-entering of fields following a sufficient latency period after pesticide application did not increase urinary ETU levels, possibly because workers wore personal protective equipment. The ETU levels reported in several of the occupational exposure studies performed in 2002 to 2004 (Fustinoni et al., 2008) appear to be higher than in more recent studies, suggesting that occupational exposures may have decreased over time. Unfortunately, the number of studies identified in the systematic review is too small to assess changes in urinary ETU levels over time. In addition, the different analytical methods used in various studies and the different limits of detection make it challenging to compare the data across studies (for a review of analytical methods used for ETU analyses in urine, see Montesano and Wang, 2011).

Studies of occupational DTC exposures also indicate regional differences in urinary ETU levels. For example, a study of farmers in Thailand reported significant regional differences in the ETU detection frequency (Panuwet et al., 2008). The mean ETU levels in Pong Yaeng, a region growing temperate crops, was 0.43 ng/ml (0.42 μg/g creatinine; N=69) but below the LOD in Inthakhin, a region growing cash crops. Maximum ETU levels were 28.5 ng/ml (13.2 μg/g creatinine) in Pong Yaeng and 3.3 ng/ml (2.2 μg/g creatinine; N=67) in Inthakhin. These differences are likely related to the different crops grown in both regions and the different pesticides applied within both farming communities. Indeed, based on questionnaire data, the fungicide Mancozeb is frequently used in Pong Yaeng.

Behavioral factors, such as psychosocial stressors, knowledge of pesticides, and beliefs, affect pesticide exposures (Quandt et al., 2006). For example, a study of female farmworkers in Florida, US, assessed behavioral factors, specifically risk perception of pesticides, that affected the exposure to DTC fungicides in two different populations (Runkle et al., 2013). This study reported higher GM ETU levels in nursery workers (5.69 μg/g creatinine, N=50) than fernery workers (1.9 μg/g creatinine, N=50) in 2011. In this study, urinary ETU levels were not associated with the handling of pesticides. However, the work tasks and work environments (e.g., access to bathrooms and facilities to wash hands) differed between nursery and fernery operations, contributing to the different ETU levels. More importantly, the risk perception differed between both groups, with nursery workers worrying less about pesticide exposure than fernery workers. Consequently, nursery workers likely did not follow occupational hygiene procedures, resulting in higher exposures. Building on these findings, it is possible to implement interventions to mitigate exposure risks within occupational populations.

Conclusions

4.

This systematic review of human biomonitoring studies of ETU, a urinary biomarker of DTC exposure, identified only a few studies investigating DTC exposure in environmentally exposed adults, children, pregnant women, and occupationally exposed adults. Most studies have a small sample size, are over a decade old, and are typically performed in populations with expected DTC exposure. The NHANES population is the only large population for which ETU exposures are available (N=7,321). The NHANES survey reported low detection frequencies of ETU in the general population of the United States; however, these exposure data are over a decade old. In addition, a variety of analytical methods with a broad range of LODs were used to quantify urinary ETU levels, and factors resulting in oral exposure are poorly understood. For example, dietary DTC and ETU levels in foodstuffs, cigarettes, and drinking water remain poorly characterized.

Consequently, it is challenging to draw clear conclusions about trends in DTC exposures over time and differences in exposure of at-risk populations, particularly children and pregnant women. Overall, occupationally exposed populations and populations living near agricultural sites with heavy DTC fungicide appear to have higher detection frequencies and ETU levels than the NHANES population. The NHANES data suggest that DTC exposure of the general population of the US over the last decade was low, consistent with the low detection frequency of DTC fungicides in foodstuffs in the US and the European Union. These exposures are expected to decrease over time as DTC fungicides are being phased out. Historically, agricultural populations, especially in middle and low-income countries, have experienced higher DTC fungicide exposure. These exposures may continue, in particular in countries where DTC fungicides are still being used. Unfortunately, current exposure data for DTC fungicides are missing worldwide, as suggested by our systematic review.
