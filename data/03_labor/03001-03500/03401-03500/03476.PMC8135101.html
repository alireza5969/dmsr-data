Abstract

BACKGROUND:

Despite the significant societal burden of human papillomavirus (HPV)–associated cancers, clinical screening interventions for HPV-associated noncervical cancers are not available. Blood-based biomarkers may help close this gap in care.

METHODS:

Five databases were searched, 5687 articles were identified, and 3631 unique candidate titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by 2 authors; 702 articles underwent a full-text review. Eligibility criteria included the assessment of a blood-based biomarker within a cohort or case-control study.

RESULTS:

One hundred thirty-seven studies were included. Among all biomarkers assessed, HPV-16 E seropositivity and circulating HPV DNA were most significantly correlated with HPV-associated cancers in comparison with cancer-free controls. In most scenarios, HPV-16 E6 seropositivity varied nonsignificantly according to tumor type, specimen collection timing, and anatomic site (crude odds ratio [cOR] for p16+ or HPV+ oropharyngeal cancer [OPC], 133.10; 95% confidence interval [CI], 59.40–298.21; cOR for HPV-unspecified OPC, 25.41; 95% CI, 8.71–74.06; cOR for prediagnostic HPV-unspecified OPC, 59.00; 95% CI, 15.39–226.25; cOR for HPV-unspecified cervical cancer, 12.05; 95% CI, 3.23–44.97; cOR for HPV-unspecified anal cancer, 73.60; 95% CI, 19.68–275.33; cOR for HPV-unspecified penile cancer, 16.25; 95% CI, 2.83–93.48). Circulating HPV-16 DNA was a valid biomarker for cervical cancer (cOR, 15.72; 95% CI, 3.41–72.57). In 3 cervical cancer case-control studies, cases exhibited unique microRNA expression profiles in comparison with controls. Other assessed biomarker candidates were not valid.

CONCLUSIONS:

HPV-16 E6 antibodies and circulating HPV-16 DNA are the most robustly analyzed and most promising blood-based biomarkers for HPV-associated cancers to date. Comparative validity analyses are warranted. Variations in tumor type–specific, high-risk HPV DNA prevalence according to anatomic site and world region highlight the need for biomarkers targeting more high-risk HPV types. Further investigation of blood-based microRNA expression profiling appears indicated.

INTRODUCTION

Human papillomavirus (HPV)–associated cancers impose a substantial burden on society. HPV causes more than 30,000 oropharyngeal, cervical, and other anogenital cancers in the United States each year.1 HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer (OPC) has risen rapidly in incidence and has recently overtaken cervical cancer, and its incidence is expected to continue to increase over the next few decades.2,3 The treatment of HPV-associated cancers is associated with substantial morbidity, and more than 8000 patients die of the disease annually.4 These cancers are also expensive: the cost of cancer care totals approximately $1 billion, and population-level cancer prevention costs an estimated $8 billion annually.5

Ongoing primary and secondary preventive interventions targeting HPV-associated cancers effectively protect particular populations6 but fail to address all individuals vulnerable to HPV-related cancers. For example, prophylactic HPV vaccination is recommended only for females and males aged 9 to 26 years and is optional for adults aged 27 to 45 years. Furthermore, full series uptake is only 51% in US adolescents.7 The most commonly used vaccine, Gardasil 9, promotes immunogenicity toward the most common high-risk HPV types but does not protect against 4 rarer oncogenic HPV types.8 Moreover, the benefits of vaccination may not be realized for decades. The median age of presentation with HPV+ OPC is 58 years, so it may take 30 years until the most at-risk age group will have been vaccinated.

Secondary HPV-associated cervical cancer prevention, or screening, is the standard of care for women aged 21 to 65 years. However, screening for other HPV-associated cancers is not available in the United States.9 Current cervical cancer screening guidelines recommend 3 different screening modalities for women aged 21 to 65 years: cervical cytology (Papanicolaou [Pap] test), primary screening for high-risk HPV types, or both the Pap test and HPV screening (cotesting).9,10 With the widespread adoption of the Pap test in the 1960s in the United States, the age-adjusted cervical cancer incidence and mortality decreased more than 50% from 1975 to 2008.11 Screening is cost-effective in developing and developed countries.12–14 The efficacy and impact of cervical cancer screening should inspire efforts to develop secondary preventive interventions for other HPV-associated cancers.

Because of the significant economic and public health burden of HPV-associated cancers, which is driven primarily by HPV+ OPCs, there is a significant need for novel secondary preventive interventions. Blood-based biomarkers represent an ideal potential modality for the early detection or surveillance of HPV-associated cancers at all sites. Preliminary research has highlighted the potential application of HPV-16 antibodies and circulating HPV DNA.15,16 To date, HPV-16–based biomarkers have garnered the most attention because HPV-16 causes nearly 80% of all anal cancers and approximately half of all oropharyngeal and cervical cancers worldwide.17–19 However, several other blood-based biomarkers, including cytokine levels, vitamins and cofactors, and various gene polymorphisms, have been explored.

The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to identify and characterize the potential validity of candidate blood-based biomarkers of HPV-associated cancers by pooling and analyzing cohort and case-control study data.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Overview

The study protocol did not involve identifiable human subjects, was classified as exempt human subjects research, and did not require oversight from the Institutional Review Board. With search concepts including human papillomavirus, cancer, biomarkers, and blood, formal comprehensive search strategies were developed with the assistance of medical librarians (H.G.M. and K.L.). MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane Central were searched from their inception through October 26, 2018, with the search terms documented in the supporting information. In all, 5687 articles were identified and exported to EndNote 8; 2179 duplicates were removed, and 3631 candidate titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by 2 authors (S.B. and S.K.). Disputes were arbitrated by a third author (A.T.D.). Forty-three additional articles were identified on bibliographic review. Of the 3674 articles, 702 underwent a full-text review, and 137 were included (Fig. 1).

Inclusion Criteria

Eligible manuscripts included cohort and case-control studies involving at least 5 patients with text available in the English language. Cases were defined as patients with primary, invasive cancers involving the oropharynx, cervix, anus, vulva, vagina, or penis with blood samples collected before treatment. Controls were defined as patients without cancer or precancer.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies not evaluating a blood-based biomarker or studies without human subjects were excluded. Articles without appropriate cases were excluded; they included studies without invasive cancer cases (ie, patients with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia [CIN] or carcinoma in situ only), studies aggregating cancers and precancers (ie, studies not differentiating invasive cancer cases from dysplasia, CIN, or carcinoma in situ), and non–subsite-specific studies that did not sufficiently differentiate between HPV+ and HPV– disease (ie, head and neck mucosal squamous cell carcinoma patients with involvement of the oropharynx or another site [oral cavity, larynx, or hypopharynx]). Articles without appropriate controls were excluded; they included case-case studies, case-only studies (ie, absence of a control group), and studies aggregating controls with dysplasia or precancer (ie, inclusion of CIN patients with controls). Genotype and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) studies were also excluded. In addition, because of the high volume of HPV antibody studies in the cervical cancer literature, blood-based antibody studies involving this site with fewer than 500 patients (cases plus controls) were excluded. Conversely, all eligible blood-based antibody studies involving other anatomic sites were included. Studies that did not present data in a numerical form, did not specify units, did not specify the antibody type (eg, HPV-16 instead of HPV-16 E6), did not present data for both cases and controls, or evaluated only the association of a blood-based biomarker with another factor (eg, smoking) were also excluded. Antibody studies evaluating only seropositivity against L antigens were excluded because of their high prevalence in controls, the potential confounding association with vaccination, and a lack of correlation with neoplasia.20 Two articles were unable to be obtained after an extensive online search and assistance from the research librarian.21,22

Study Categorization

Study data were presented according to anatomic site (ie, oropharyngeal, cervical, anal, penile, vulvar, and vaginal sites) and biomarker type (antibody, circulating DNA, and others). HPV was deemed a cause of OPC in 2007 and now accounts for 70% of OPCs.23 Given this recent development, many OPC studies did not stratify tumors according to the HPV or p16 status, a sensitive and specific surrogate marker for HPV.24 Therefore, OPC studies that did specify the HPV or p16 status were analyzed separately. Studies of other anogenital sites did not regularly stratify tumors according to the HPV status. Antibody studies examining immunoglobulin G antibodies were categorized according to site, and the 2 antibodies with the strongest correlation with cancer are presented in the tables. For antibody studies in which various optical densities were tested, the optical density that showed the greatest odds ratio was chosen for analysis. Exceptions to this methodology were as follows: some studies evaluated only 1 antibody; if studies did not present odds ratio data and presented only sensitivity and specificity, then all the antibodies were presented; and if studies described the same odds ratios for multiple antibodies, then all those antibodies were listed.

Risk of Bias Analysis

We applied the Tool to Assess Risk of Bias in Case-Control Studies to assess the risk of bias in the included studies.25,26 Studies were characterized as having a low, moderate (higher), or high risk of bias according to 5 domains (Supporting Tables 1 and 2). An overall risk of bias score was assigned to each study according to the highest risk of bias designation in any domain. All studies identified are reported in Supporting Table 3.

RESULTS

Overview

We identified 137 eligible studies. Among all evaluated blood-based biomarker candidates for HPV-associated cancers, HPV-16 E antibodies and circulating HPV DNA exhibited the strongest biomarker performance characteristics.27–40 (Figs. 2–5). Although select microRNA expression profiles exhibited modest associations with cervical cancers in comparison with controls, significant findings were not conserved across studies41–43 (Table 1). Finally, among the multitudinous invalid blood-based biomarker candidates identified in this review (Supporting Table 4), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), serum folate, insulin-like growth factor binding protein 3 (IGFBP-3), and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) are highlighted in Figure 6 because pooled studies trended toward exhibiting a correlation with HPV-associated cancers compared to controls.44−55

HPV-16 E Antibody Associations With Oropharyngeal and Anogenital Cancer

HPV antibodies were the most common blood-based biomarkers for HPV-associated cancers assessed in this review. Many studies evaluated multiple antibodies for 1 or more HPV genotypes. To crudely determine which blood-based antibody biomarkers to select for presentation in the review, the 2 type-specific antibodies with the strongest association with cancer cases were identified in each study, ranked and then tallied across all studies. HPV-16 E6 antibodies exhibited the most significant association with cancer cases in 19 studies, followed by HPV-16 E7 antibodies, which exhibited the strongest association with cancer cases in 13 studies (Supporting Table 5).

Analyses in Figure 2 demonstrate that HPV-16 E6 seropositivity is significantly associated with oropharyngeal and cervical cancers at the time of diagnosis in comparison with controls, regardless of tumor HPV or p16 status. Among pooled populations of known p16+ or HPV+ cancer cases and controls, HPV-16 E6 seropositivity exhibited very strong associations with OPC (crude odds ratio [cOR], 133.10; 95% confidence interval [CI], 59.40–298.21; sensitivity, 56.3%; 95% CI, 44.4%−68.1%; specificity, 99.0%; 95% CI, 98.2%−99.7%)27,32,56 and cervical cancer (cOR, 81.61; 95% CI, 29.24–227.78; sensitivity, 50.3%; 95% CI, 43.1%−57.4%; specificity, 98.8%; 95% CI, 97.6%−100.0%).57

The findings in Figure 3 corroborate Figure 2 and establish that HPV-16 E6–seropositive individuals are significantly more likely to develop an HPV-associated cancer than seronegative individuals. In pooled analyses of nested case-control studies within cancer epidemiology cohorts, HPV-16 E6 seropositivity identified before a cancer diagnosis predicted the development of all HPV-associated cancer types.58−60

Analyses in Figures 2 and 3 also highlight the variability in the strength of HPV-16 E6 antibody associations with cancer across anatomic sites. Notably, in pooled analyses of both HPV- or p16-unspecified and specified case-control studies (Fig. 2), HPV-16 E6 seropositivity exhibited nonsignificantly stronger associations with OPC (cOR in unspecified OPC, 25.41; 95% CI, 8.71–74.06)27–33 in comparison with cervical cancer (cOR, 12.05; 95% CI, 3.23–44.97).57,61–64 According to pooled analyses in Figure 3, HPV-16 E6 antibodies exhibited particularly strong associations with anal cancers and OPCs and relatively modest associations with cervical, penile, and vulvovaginal cancers. Data in Supporting Figure 2 further confirm these findings: HPV-16 E7 seropositivity modestly predicts for development of OPC and anal cancer but not penile or cervical cancer.58–60 Finally, HPV E antibody studies evaluating associations with noncervical anogenital case-control studies were sparse, and significant findings were not conserved across studies (Supporting Fig. 6).65-67

Pooled analyses of OPC and anogenital cancer case-control studies in Figures 2 and 3 demonstrate that blood-based HPV-16 E6 antibody biomarkers generally exhibit nonsignificantly superior performance characteristics for HPV-associated cancers in comparison with HPV-16 E7 antibodies. Overall, across all anatomic sites, prediagnostic serum HPV-16 E7 antibodies had weaker associations with cancer in comparison with HPV-16 E6 antibodies (Fig. 3 and Supporting Figs. 1 and 3). As described in Figure 4, HPV-16 E6 seropositivity (cOR, 25.41; 95% CI, 8.71–74.06) exhibited nonsignificantly stronger associations with OPC in comparison with E7 seropositivity (cOR, 16.49; 95% CI, 5.25–51.75).27–33 However, in p16+ or HPV+ OPCs (Supporting Fig. 2), HPV-16 E7 seropositivity (cOR, 165.12; 95% CI, 70.26–388.04) was nonsignificantly stronger compared to HPV-16 E6 seropositivity (cOR, 133.10; 95% CI, 59.40–298.21).27–33 Supporting Figure 4 demonstrates that HPV-16 E6 antibodies exhibited modest but stronger associations with cervical cancer (cOR, 12.05; 95% CI, 3.23–44.97)57,61–64 in comparison with HPV-16 E7 antibodies (cOR, 4.975; 95% CI, 2.02–12.28).57,61–64,68

Concurrent testing of different HPV-16 E antibodies nonsignificantly augmented the performance characteristics of singular HPV-16 E antibodies (Fig. 4). Pooled data from 3 studies demonstrated that a combination of HPV-16 E6 and/or E7 antibodies exhibited the strongest associations with unspecified OPC (cOR, 452.8; 95% CI, 40.93–5010.29; sensitivity, 77.0%; 95% CI, 64.1%−89.9%; specificity, 99.0%; 95% CI, 97.8%−100%).27,28,32 In a single-institution study conducted by Anderson et al in 2015,32 a combination of NE2, CE2, E6, and/or E7 showed stronger associations with p16+ or HPV+ OPC (cOR, 685.65; 95% CI, 155.41–3024.98; Supporting Fig. 3) than p16- or HPV-unspecified OPC (cOR, 132.96; 95% CI, 59.15–298.87).

Supporting Figure 5 describes studies evaluating antibodies to more than 1 HPV type (ie, HPV-16 and HPV-18) in patients with cervical cancer and controls. In contrast to OPCs, antibody cotesting in cervical cancer exhibited weak performance characteristics.62,63,68

Circulating HPV DNA in Cervical Cancer

A pooled analysis of 6 studies (Fig. 5) showed that the association of circulating HPV-16 DNA with cervical cancer was modest but significant (cOR, 15.7; 95% CI, 3.4–72.6).35–40 In this population, the performance characteristics of circulating HPV-18 DNA were weaker than those of HPV-16 DNA.36–40 In the only HPV+ OPC case-control study on the topic (n = 50), circulating HPV-16/18 DNA was found to be 65% sensitive and 100% specific.69

MicroRNA in Cervical Cancer

Table 1 describes the expression profiles of several microRNAs in 3 cervical cancer patient case-control studies.41–43 miR-20a, miR-205, miR-1246, miR-2392, miR-3147, and miR-3162–5p were all significantly overexpressed, and miRNA-218 was significantly underexpressed in patients with cancer in comparison with controls. However, significant microRNA expression profiles were not conserved across any of the 3 studies.

Other Candidate Blood-Based Biomarkers in Oropharyngeal and Anogenital Cancer

Figure 6 highlights the overall weak performance characteristics of other candidate blood-based biomarkers for HPV-associated cancers. Eight studies demonstrated no significant difference in the blood-based levels of IGF-1, IGFBP-3, folate, or IFN-γ in patients with cervical cancer in comparison with controls.44–47,52–55

Supporting Table 4 describes all other studies with potential biomarker candidates identified in this review. These other biomarker candidates were categorized as vitamins, immunologic markers, apoptotic markers, tumor markers, tumor suppressors and oncogenes, sex hormones, growth factors, cell-signaling molecules, matrix metalloproteinases and inhibitors, products of oxidative stress, enzymes, macro- and micromolecules, molecular markers and chromosomal aberrations, or miscellaneous markers.

DISCUSSION

This meta-analysis demonstrates that among all identified blood-based biomarkers to date, HPV-16 E antibodies and circulating HPV DNA exhibited the strongest associations with HPV-associated cancers. Blood-based HPV-16 E antibody biomarkers exhibited robust associations with oropharyngeal and anal cancers and weaker associations with cervical, vulvovaginal, and penile cancers. Notably, the strength of the associations was significant across all 3 OPC study populations: prediagnostic serum specimens from cancer epidemiology cohorts, and p16+/HPV+ or p16/HPV-unspecified cases from serum collected at the time of diagnosis. HPV-16 E antibody cotesting (eg E6 and/or E7 antibodies) exhibited nonsignificantly improved validity in comparison with independently assessed E antibodies across p16- or HPV-specific OPC and unspecified OPC tumor types. Circulating HPV-16 and HPV-18 DNA also exhibited significant associations with OPC and cervical cancers. Blood-based microRNA expression profiles exhibited associations with cervical cancers, but significant findings were not conserved across studies. Pooled analyses of other candidate biomarkers did not reveal significant correlations with HPV-associated cancers; notably, however, blood-based genetic biomarkers (ie, HLA or single-nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs]) were not evaluated in this review.

Both HPV-16 E antibodies and circulating HPV-DNA exhibited comparably strong associations with HPV-associated cancers. Therefore, validation studies assessing the performance characteristics of these 2 biomarker types, in isolation and via concurrent testing, are warranted. Because of variation in type–specific, high-risk HPV DNA prevalence according to anatomic site and world region, both classes of biomarkers should be adapted to target multiple high-risk HPV genotypes. Finally, we argue that these biomarkers should serve as references in the assessment of novel blood-based biomarkers.

Prediagnostic HPV-16 E6 antibodies showed significant associations with cancer across all anatomic subsites. A recent nested OPC case-control analysis of pooled cancer epidemiology cohorts (n = 743 cases, n = 5814 controls) provides the most up-to-date analysis of the kinetics of seroconversion among patients with incident OPC.70 The prevalence of HPV-16 E6 seropositivity was inversely associated with the lead time. Serum HPV-16 E6 antibodies were present in: 38.9% of individuals less than 10 years prior to their OPC diagnosis; 23.7% of individuals 10 to 20 years before to their diagnosis, 13.5% of individuals 20 to 30 years prior to their diagnosis, and 0% of individuals more than 30 years before their OPC diagnosis. The identification of a detectable HPV-16 E antibody immune response decades before the development of OPC indicates that a subclinical disease state exists. Collectively, these findings highlight the promise of HPV-16 E6 antibodies in screening for HPV-associated cancers and for HPV+ OPC in particular.

Although HPV-16 E6 or E7 antibodies proved to be valid blood-based biomarkers for all HPV-associated cancers, the strength of the association varied significantly according to the anatomic site. HPV-16 E antibody biomarker validity is superior at oropharyngeal and anal sites in comparison with cervical, vulvovaginal, and penile sites; however, we identified only 5 noncervical anogenital cancer studies, highlighting the overall dearth of data on blood-based biomarkers for these cancers. Therefore, in light of available evidence and the lower incidence of anal cancer, HPV-16 E antibodies may serve as a better biomarker for OPC, the most common HPV-associated cancer, in comparison with other HPV-associated cancers.

The heterogeneity in biomarker validity across anatomic sites is multifactorial. First, some investigators have argued that infection or cancer arising in the oropharynx may be more immunogenic than infection or cancer arising at other anatomic sites, such as the cervix. The oropharynx is largely lined by the palatine and lingual tonsils (lymphoid organs contributing to humoral and cell-mediated immunity), whereas cervical immunity is mediated by the mucosa.71–73 However, this explanation does not account for the superior validity of the biomarker for cancers arising at the mucosally-lined anus. Ultimately, an improved understanding of differences in immune response in at-risk mucosal anatomic sites may advance secondary prevention of HPV-associated cancers.

Second, differences in biomarker validity across anatomic sites may be attributed to differences in etiology. For instance, approximately 75% of head and neck cancers overall are caused by alcohol and tobacco, with the other 25% attributable to HPV.3,74,75 In contrast, alcohol and tobacco are less significant risk factors for anogenital cancers in comparison with HPV.76,77

Third, the prevalence of HPV type–specific, site-specific cancers also varies according to geographic location.17 Although high-risk HPV DNA is present in 82% of invasive cervical cancers and 79% of anal cancers worldwide,17–19 in the United States, relatively higher proportions of these cancers are attributable to high-risk HPV: 91% of cervical cancers and 91% of anal cancers.75 The worldwide prevalence of HPV-16–associated OPC varies even more greatly and is: 59.3% in the United States (95% CI, 53.1%−65.5%), 31.1% in Europe (95% CI, 22.7%−39.5%), and 4.1% in Brazil (95% CI, 1.1%−7.1%).78 In our meta-analysis, we found that the majority of OPC studies included populations from the United States and Europe, and the majority of cervical cancer studies included populations from Europe or Asia (particularly India). These data highlight the need to develop biomarkers accounting for the heterogeneity of etiologies. Within individual cancer subsites, HPV-16 E6 antibodies generally trended toward stronger correlations with HPV-associated cancers in comparison with E7 antibodies. Notably, our pooled analyses demonstrated no significant differences in validity between the 2 antibody types. Nonetheless, these findings generally corroborate reports that HPV-16 E7 antibodies are present in a higher proportion of cancer-free control patients (2%−11%) in comparison with HPV-16 E6 antibodies (0.4%−0.7%).29,31,59,70,79,80

Importantly, antibody cotesting (eg, E6 and/or E7) exhibited relatively improved validity (E6 and/or E7) or significantly improved validity (a predefined E antibody panel) in comparison with independently assessed E antibodies (eg, E6) in pooled OPC case-control studies. In their recent pooled HPV+ OPC case-control study (n = 306), D’Souza et al81 reported mixed results from cotesting with multiple HPV-16 antibodies. HPV-16 E6 and E7 antibody testing improved specificity (100%) but decreased sensitivity (69%); HPV-16 E6 and/or E7 testing improved sensitivity (94%) and decreased specificity (93%); and HPV-16 E1, E2, E6, and/or E7 testing maximized sensitivity (96%) but further compromised specificity (91%). In contrast, antibody testing of multiple HPV strains and antibodies in cervical cancers was not shown to have as significant validity. In the absence of expert consensus, additional validation studies comparing HPV-16 E antibody combinations will be needed to identify the strongest antibody combination for screening.16,32,70,81

In this meta-analysis, circulating HPV DNA exhibited validity in cervical cancer case-control studies comparable to that of HPV-16 E antibodies. In a review of HPV+ OPC case-only or case-case studies (HPV+ OPCs vs HPV− OPCs), pretreatment circulating HPV DNA detected by conventional real-time and/or quantitative techniques also exhibited high sensitivities ranging from 61% to 95%.82 Advances in technology such as digital droplet polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) may further augment the validity of circulating HPV DNA. Using ddPCR targeting select high-risk DNA strains, Chera et al83 and Wang et al84 reported 88% and 86% sensitivity among patients with known p16+ or HPV+ head and neck cancer, respectively. Circulating HPV-16 DNA detected by ddPCR is also valid at other subsites: among patients with advanced anal carcinoma, the biomarker was 91% sensitive.85

Circulating HPV DNA has shown potential efficacy in posttreatment cancer surveillance. The detection rate of HPV DNA in cervical cancers is greater in later stage cancers in comparison with carcinoma in situ, emphasizing that circulating HPV DNA appears to dynamically reflect the real-time tumor status of newly diagnosed patients with HPV-associated carcinomas.69,86 There is limited evidence that HPV-16 E6 seropositivity is useful in the surveillance of patients with HPV-associated cancer. In one study, patients with HPV+ OPC recurrence had higher E6 and E7 antibody levels than those who did not experience recurrence (P = .02 and P = .002, respectively).87 These elevated antibody levels after treatment might reflect evidence of response to the active tumor.87 Further studies are needed to evaluate the role of circulating HPV DNA and potentially HPV E antibodies in HPV-associated cancer surveillance.

Pooled cervical cancer case-control study data suggest that the continued search for a blood-based microRNA expression signature for HPV-associated cancers may be warranted. MicroRNAs are a class of noncoding short-nucleotide RNAs that regulate posttranscriptional gene expression. Among the 2500 microRNA sequences identified to date, some microRNA signature associations with other cancers and cancer-specific, prognostic, microRNA-based risk classifiers have been established.88–101 Further, investigators evaluating HPV+ tumor tissue have reported discovery of unique microRNA expression signatures.102–105 Our meta-analysis features 8 unique blood-based microRNAs which were correlated with cervical cancer, although, none were conserved across the 3 studies. However, because of the vast number of microRNAs and lack of specificity for HPV+ cancers, identifying a valid expression profile for HPV-associated cancers may prove challenging. Limited stability also constrain their promise: microRNA testing requires more intensive and rapid specimen collection, processing, and storage protocols.41,106,107 More highly powered case-control studies evaluating not only microRNA expression profiles specific to HPV-associated cancers but also effective and efficient sample analysis techniques are needed to strengthen its utility as a biomarker.

Although several studies evaluated blood-based folate, IGF-1, IGFBP-3, and IFN-γ levels as candidate biomarkers for HPV-associated cancers, none were valid according to our meta-analysis. There are multiple pathophysiologic bases for the consideration and evaluation of these potential biomarkers. Folate is a cofactor in several metabolic reactions, including mitosis and DNA synthesis, and prior studies have shown conflicting results with respect to folate levels and cancer risk.108–111 IGF-1 influences cell cycle progression and apoptosis, and IGFBP-3 binds to IGF-1 and thus prevents its degradation and increases its activity. Although lower levels of IGF-1 have been associated with cervical cancer, higher levels of IGF have been associated with cancers at other sites, such as lung and colorectal cancers.44 IFN-γ is a cytokine contributing to host antiviral responses and antitumor activity by augmenting natural killer cell activity and tumor cell antigenicity, but it has also been shown to paradoxically promote tumor growth and prevent tumor immunogenicity.112 The overall poor validity of these biomarker candidates is likely mostly due to the lack of HPV-associated cancer specificity (unlike HPV E antibodies and circulating HPV DNA). For example, although folate levels in erythrocytes are more stable, serum folate levels fluctuate with diet and assay type.108 Similarly, IGF-1 and IGFBP-3 levels vary by age, sex, and nutritional status, and cytokine levels fluctuate with the immune state.112,113

Because of the known complex associations with HPV-associated cancers and the already broad scope of this review, we determined a priori to exclude blood-based genetic markers from the analysis. However, because of the number of studies evaluating this topic, a cursory discussion of the subject is warranted. To our knowledge, the most commonly evaluated genetic markers for HPV-associated cancers are HLA-B*08, p53 SNPs, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) SNPs. HLA-B*08 is a class II antigen with a high rate of occurrence in European populations and with a potential role in autoimmunity.114 Although 1 study reported that Indian patients with the HLA-B*08 antigen exhibited 10 times greater odds of cervical cancer (P = .002) in comparison with their respective counterparts, 3 other studies did not report a significant association.115−117SNPs are the substitution of a single base pair for another and play a key role in the evaluation of disease susceptibility, drug efficacy, and adverse drug reactions.118 Some investigators have analyzed the TNF-α G>A rs1800629 SNP.119–121 According to Duarte et al,119 the TNF-α GA genotype showed a modest but statistically significant difference in genotype distribution (16.4% in controls vs 25.6% in patients with cervical cancer; n = 439; P = .012). Other investigators have analyzed the p53 G>C rs1042522 SNP, and a few have found that the Arg/Arg genotype had a statistically higher frequency in cases versus controls; this could sbe due to the fact that the arginine residue at amino acid 72 may be more susceptible to degradation by HPV E6 protein in comparison with the proline residue.122–124 Finally, genetic studies have been inherently limited by heterogeneous assessment (ie, allele vs genotype vs phenotype frequency), inconsistent use of nomenclature across HLA studies (World Health Organization classification vs International Cell Exchange classification), and a lack of a standardized reference group when odds ratios are being calculated (ie, some p53 studies have used the Arg/Arg genotype as the references, and others have used Pro/Pro). In addition, certain genotypes are more prevalent in certain racial/ethnic groups, and this also decreases the generalizability of genetic biomarkers.114–117

Although robust screening strategies are established for cervical cancer, other anogenital cancers and OPCs lack effective preventative screening programs. According to the analytic framework for evaluating a potential clinical preventive screening service described by the US Preventive Service Task Force, an effective screening modality must 1) reduce morbidity and mortality, 2) accurately detect the intended condition, and 3) do no harm. In addition, diseases that are being screened must 1) have a target, high-risk population; 2) have treatments for early or intermediate outcomes identified through screening; 3) have treatments that improve overall disease outcomes if the disease is identified early through screening; 4) have strong associations between intermediate outcomes and reduced morbidity and mortality; and 5) have treatments that do not cause significantly more harm than the disease itself.125

A blood-based biomarker is an attractive option for a screening modality because of the widespread use of blood-based laboratory tests in medicine and overall low risk of harm. In our meta-analysis, we have highlighted the potential utility of HPV-16 E antibodies and circulating HPV DNA for the screening and surveillance of HPV-associated cancers. However, the potential use of these biomarkers in secondary prevention presents significant challenges. Although these biomarkers have strong associations with HPV-associated cancers, a highly specific biomarker is needed to limit false-positive results and reduce harm. Although the incidence of HPV+ OPC is rising, HPV-associated cancers have a relatively low population-level incidence overall. Therefore, the identification of a target higher risk population and improved biomarker specificity will be needed before the implementation of a screening program is possible. Furthermore, these blood-based biomarkers lack utility in that they fail to localize disease: subsequent clinical and/or radiologic examination of all oropharyngeal and anogenital sites in biomarker-positive individuals will be necessary. Although we have identified promising screening biomarker candidates, substantial progress will be needed to ensure compliance with all US Preventive Service Task Force screening intervention criteria.

To our knowledge, this is the most robust evaluation of blood-based biomarkers for HPV-associated cancers in the literature. As highlighted previously, we appropriately accounted for differences in biomarker validity across anatomic sites by stratifying our analysis accordingly. However, we were unable to further substratify data according to geographic location because of the limited number of eligible studies. In addition, because of the low incidence of anal, penile, vaginal, and vulvar cancers, fewer studies evaluated blood-based biomarkers at those sites, and this limited our analysis to the evaluation of primarily OPC and cervical cancer biomarker candidates. Because few studies stratified biomarker associations with cancer according to tumor stage, we were unable to evaluate this potential correlation. We used rigorous inclusion criteria: hence, 60 otherwise eligible studies were excluded because of inadequate data (ie, the data were not presented numerically, the data were insufficiently described [eg, the study included controls in the experimental design but did not present control data in the results], or the study did not analyze a single blood-based biomarker alone [eg, both HPV and Chlamydia trachomatis seropositivity]). Finally, because of the dynamic nature of the field, several important and even landmark studies were published after the date of our literature search and were, therefore, not included in the data analyses.70,81,126

In conclusion, among the biomarkers assessed in this review, HPV E antibodies and circulating HPV DNA are the most well-established and valid blood-based biomarkers for HPV-associated cancers. The lower validity of HPV-16 E antibodies in HPV-unspecified cervical, vulvovaginal, and penile cancers calls attention to the need for biomarkers that account for the diversity of oncogenic HPV types. Multiple HPV E antibody combinations showed strong associations for OPC; however, comparative validation studies are needed to identify the precise combination with the greatest association with cancer. Studies to further characterize a blood-based microRNA expression signature for HPV-associated cancers are warranted. In the absence of novel findings, other previously assessed blood-based biomarker candidates exhibit little promise in screening for or surveilling HPV-associated cancers.
