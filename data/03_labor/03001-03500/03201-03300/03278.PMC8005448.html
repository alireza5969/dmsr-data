Systematic review of plasma to packed red blood cell ratio on survival in ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysms

Amanda R. Phillips[1]

Lillian Tran[1]

Jill E. Foust[2]

Nathan L. Liang[1]

[1] University of Pittsburgh Medical Center,

[2] University of Pittsburgh

Author notes:

Correspondence to: Corresponding author: Amanda R Phillips, M.D. 100 South 11th Street, Pittsburgh, PA 15203

Abstract

Background:

The ideal perioperative fluid resuscitation for ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysms (rAAA) is unknown. It has been shown in the trauma literature that a higher ratio of plasma and platelets to packed red blood cells confers a mortality benefit. There remains controversy whether this is true also in the ruptured aneurysm population. The objective of this study is to investigate the benefit of higher ratio of plasma to packed red blood cells in patients with ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysms.

Methods:

A health sciences librarian searched four electronic databases including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and ClinicalTrials.gov using concepts for the terms fluid resuscitation, survival, and ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm. Two reviewers independently screened the studies that were identified through the search strategy and read in full any study that was potentially relevant. Papers were included if they compared mortality of patients with rAAA who received a higher ratio of plasma to other component therapy to patients who received a lower ratio. Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I validated tool and evidence quality was rated using the GRADE profile. No data synthesis or meta-analysis was planned or performed given the anticipated paucity of research on this topic and the high degree of heterogeneity of available studies.

Results:

Our search identified seven observational studies to be included in this review. Of these seven studies, three found an associated decrease in mortality with a higher ratio of plasma to packed red blood cells and the remaining four found no significant difference. The overall risk of bias was serious and the evidence quality was very low.

Conclusions:

Overall, the available studies would suggest that for patients that have undergone open surgery for a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm, mortality rates tend to decrease when the amount of plasma transfused perioperatively is similar to the amount of packed red blood cells. However, this is very low-quality evidence based solely off of highly heterogenous observational studies and further research is warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Despite many medical advances that we have experienced in the last several decades, ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (rAAA) continues to be one of the most devastating and morbid vascular surgery emergencies with an overall mortality rate of nearly 90%.1,2 It has been reported to be the 15th leading cause of death overall and the 10th leading cause of death in men over the age of 55, and was the primary cause of death for nearly 10,000 people in 2014.3, 4 Studies based on autopsy findings have shown that a large proportion of patients suffering a rAAA die before reaching the hospital or prior to undergoing intervention.5 For patients fortunate enough to make it to the hospital, current estimates of postoperative mortality remain 20–40%.6

A significant predictor of postoperative mortality following open or endovascular repair for a rAAA is the onset of multiple system organ failure (MSOF) due to ischemic changes, inflammation, and coagulopathy.7–10 Postoperative resuscitation strategies have been shown to serve as a primary means of preventing coagulopathy leading to MSOF and death.11,12 There is no clear guideline as to the ideal resuscitation strategy for rAAA. Studies dating back to the 1980s have indicated that preoperative transfusion of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) is associated with a lower mortality.13 Since that time, there have been several studies that have looked at the relation of FFP transfusion on survival using multivariate modeling, however there remains a controversy as to its effect.9,14–16 In the trauma literature, the ratio of FFP to packed red blood cells (PRBC) has been of interest, and it has been shown that a more balanced resuscitation paradigm with a higher ratio of FFP and platelets to PRBC confers a mortality benefit.17 It is unclear whether this is true in the ruptured aneurysm population. The goal of this review is to explore the extent of the literature to investigate whether a higher ratio of FFP:PRBC improves survival in patients presenting with rAAA.

METHODS

This systematic review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.18 The primary query was formulated by a group comprised of vascular surgeons and trainees and the search strategy was discussed with a librarian who performed the extensive database search.

Studies

Our search included retrospective and prospective human studies published in peer-reviewed journals that are available in the English language during any time period. We also queried for unpublished clinical trials that are pending or underway, and read through any available expert reviews, systematic reviews and meta-analyses that had been done on this topic to identify any original data not captured in our database query. We excluded data where a final publication was not available and the abstract did not provide sufficient information for inclusion.

Participants

Studies were included if they were conducted on adults diagnosed with a rAAA.

Interventions

The intervention of interest to this review was resuscitation of patients with rAAA using a high ratio of FFP:PRBC, as defined in each study. The control was a lower ratio of FFP:PRBC in relation to the intervention. We excluded those studies that did not sufficiently or clearly group patients in this way. Variable study characteristics prevented a specific ratio of interest to be predetermined.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was mortality which was most often reported as 30-day mortality. Given the paucity of research in this area and heterogeneity of available studies, no secondary outcomes were analyzed.

Search methods

A health sciences librarian searched four electronic databases including PubMed (1946-September 2019), EMBASE.com (1947-September 2019), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (January 1999-September 2019), and ClinicalTrials.gov (2000-September 2019). A PubMed query (Appendix A) was developed using concepts for the terms fluid resuscitation, survival, and ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm. These terms were then adapted for use in the EMBASE.com, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov databases. English language and human filters were applied to the PubMed and EMBASE.com search results. Retrieved citations for PubMed, EMBASE.com, and Cochrane CENTRAL were delivered as an EndNote library. Retrieval for ClinicalTrials.gov was provided as an Excel spreadsheet. DistillerSR was used to screen and extract data.

Study selection methods

Two reviewers (AP, LT) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of publications that were identified through the search strategy. If an article was identified as potentially relevant with any mention of transfusion of blood products in patients with rAAA, it was read in full. Whether a study should be included in the analysis was discussed and a third reviewer (NL) was available in the event of a disagreement. Data were also collected independently by two review authors (AP, LT).

Risk of bias assessment and GRADE profile

The risk of bias for each of the seven observational studies was assessed by two individuals (AP, LT) using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool.19 This tool requires a clearly articulated review question which was predetermined and submitted to the PROSPERO systematic review registry prior to ROBINS-I use. For each study, the risk of bias in seven domains was evaluated in relation to a defined target trial. A study-level risk of bias judgement was used to inform the risk of bias across the body of evidence and integrated into the GRADE certainty of evidence assessment.20

RESULTS

The electronic search identified 2,134 records and one additional record was found through reference review. After title and abstract screening, 288 records were read in full. Of the studies reviewed in full, several were not included based on their study grouping despite potentially being relevant to the subject matter. Seven met all inclusion criteria and were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Study Characteristics

Clinical characteristics, interventions, and outcomes for each included study is listed in Table I. All included studies were observational with three being pre-post design with a prospective audit of outcomes after implementing a change in practice patterns. All are single center with sample sizes ranging from 12 to 174. All patients underwent open aortic repair for a ruptured aneurysm. The transfusion ratio in the intervention groups varied between studies as did measure of mortality (in-hospital versus 30-day mortality).

Risk of Bias

The ROBINS-I tool for non-randomized studies on interventional effects was used to assess the risk of bias (RoB) for each included study (Table II). The overall risk of bias judgement for each study with regard to mortality is shown in the final column and ranges from moderate to critical. A significant amount of bias in the overall judgement was due to confounding. Confounding in observational studies is difficult to account for and often times is inherent in study design. Over the entire body of evidence, the risk of bias is serious.

Main Findings

This review focused on how the ratio of FFP:PRBC impacts mortality for patients with ruptured AAA, and we included studies that grouped patients into receiving either a high or low ratio. After an extensive search of the literature, seven studies met criteria and were included in this review.

Studies performed by Mell and Kauver et al are similar in their design, intervention, and outcome measure. Each performed a retrospective study on over 100 patients over a consecutive 20-year span at a single center who underwent open repair for rAAA. Patients were included if they received >10 units of blood or blood products prior to the end of their operation. Mell et al reported a lower mortality rate in patients who received high FFP compared to low FFP (15% vs 39% p<0.03). Kauvar et al did not find a significant difference in mortality between the two groups. These analyses are limited in that the study period was long, and general healthcare improvements may be confounding. In addition, the authors do not provide rational behind their ratio groupings. It is not clear whether there is a significant difference between the ratio in the high FFP group versus the low FFP group. These papers are also subject to significant information bias as the medical record keeping and the handling of patient information has likely changed dramatically over twenty years. For example, the accuracy in number of units of blood products transfused in the era of digital medical records is likely much better than it was prior to digital implementation. Given these concerns, these studies were judged as having a serious risk of bias.

Hall et al compared mortality for patients undergoing open repair for rAAA from the years 2005–08 to 2010. Between these time periods, the single center introduced the use of a shock pack (4:4:1 PRBC:FFP:platelets) for resuscitation in patients with major hemorrhage. The amount of PRBC per patient after adoption of the shock pack did not change, however the amount of FFP and platelets were higher post intervention (4.1 and 1.4 compared to 2.9 and 0.89, respectively). The authors found no significant difference in mortality after the change in practice patterns, however they did observe a decrease in postoperative thrombocytopenia that may support the use of a more balanced resuscitation profile. Overall the risk of bias is critical given this is a published abstract without accompanying publication, thus the amount of missing data and details on the study execution could not be assessed.

Two papers were included in this review by Johansson et al. Although both utilized the same control group for analysis, the intervention groups highlighted here represented two separate cohorts. They implemented a change in transfusion therapy and compared outcomes to the 24 months leading up to the change in practice. In total, 124 patients who underwent open repair for rAAA between 2004 to 2008 were included and compared to 82 controls. The intervention included two packs of platelets given immediately upon arrival to the emergency department and again 30 minutes prior to unclamping of the aorta and resuscitation utilizing a 1:1 ratio of PRBC:FFP perioperatively. The ratio of PRBC:FFP was higher in the control arm. Overall, they found a significantly reduced mortality in the intervention compared to the control (29% vs 56%, p<0.05). This study is limited by possible performance bias as the control subjects were patients that had surgery prior to implementation of a proactive intervention that may have prompted better medical care. In addition, this study focused not only on FFP but also on early platelet administration. Overall these studies were judged to have a moderate risk of bias.

Tadlock et al compared mortality in patients who received damage control operative (DCO) strategies alone to those receiving damage control resuscitation (DCR) in addition to DCO. The DCR measures included a more balanced resuscitation regimen with significantly more FFP and less crystalloid compared to the DCO alone group. The mortality in the DCO and DCR compared to the DCO alone groups was found to be 25 and 75 percent, respectively. However, this was not significantly different between groups (p=0.22) likely due to their small sample size (n=12). Overall, this study was judged to have critical risk of bias due to confounding factors, small sample size, and unclear selection criteria.

Henriksson et al performed a retrospective review of 174 patients in the Swedish Vascular Registry who underwent open repair for rAAA between 1992–2008. They compared transfusion practices between the early and late time periods (1992–1999 and 2000–2008). In general, the patients were similar between the two periods with the transfusion profile being the only reported difference. The ratio in the early time period of FFP:PRBC was 0.8 compared to 0.9 in the late group (p=0.001). The overall mortality was found to be lower in the later time period (20%) compared to the early time period (31%), however this was not found to be significant (p=0.111). The authors do offer that “the cause of the decline in mortality in patients with rAAA [can be] interpreted as partly due to a modern blood component therapy.” Overall, this study was judged to have a critical risk of bias given the lack of reported confounding variables and the likely potential of performance bias.

DISCUSSION

Although there is a great number of publications that have studied management of ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysms, this review reveals how few have analyzed the potential benefit of a higher transfusion ratio of plasma to packed red blood cells in the perioperative setting. Overall, based off of what is available in the literature to date, a balanced perioperative transfusion regimen with a ratio of FFP:PRBC close to one is likely beneficial for patients with a rAAA who undergo open surgical repair.

Although it is difficult to draw firm conclusions based off of observation studies alone, the striking mortality benefit seen from Johansson et al is particularly intriguing. The patient characteristics including amount of PRBC transfused are similar between the pre- and post-intervention groups with ratio of blood products being the only significant difference observed. There also seemed to be reasonable efforts made to standardize the remainder of care with the surgeons, and intensivists blinded to the change in practice. It is important to note, that in addition to a higher ratio of FFP:PRBC, the intervention group also received more platelets than the control group. This may point to the notion that a more balanced resuscitation fluid that includes platelets and is “whole-blood-like” is optimal for these patients. This is in agreement with what is known from the trauma literature, where the research is more extensive.28,29 In fact, many groups are studying whole blood as an optimal transfusion strategy for patients with massive hemorrhage from trauma.30,31 Although trauma patients are significantly younger with fewer medical comorbidities compared to patients with rAAA, this is an intriguing concept and may prove to be an important area of future research for patients with rAAA.

The question remains largely unexamined whether a more balanced transfusion ratio of FFP:PRBC is beneficial for patients who undergo endovascular repair for rAAA. Current SVS guidelines recommend an EVAR-first approach for patients with rAAA and appropriate anatomy, yet none of the studies identified in the literature clearly include patients who underwent EVAR.32 Blood product transfusion is certainly not unique to open surgical repair as it has been reported that over 50% of patients undergoing EVAR for rAAA require massive transfusion.33 At this time, blood product transfusion ratios and possible benefits have not been well studied in this cohort.

Overall, while this review supports that a balanced transfusion ratio is likely beneficial for patients with rAAA who undergo open repair, it also serves to highlight the lack of relevant research on this vulnerable population of vascular surgery patients. With the limited data, the authors of this review are unable to provide an evidence-based recommendation as to what the appropriate transfusion ratio is for patients with rAAA undergoing surgery, and this is especially true for patients undergoing EVAR. However, at our institution, we attempt to provide balanced component transfusion based on extrapolation from trauma protocols. It is evident that future studies must include prospectively studying both patients that undergo open surgical repair and EVAR.

Limitations

The studies available on this topic consist of non-randomized studies on interventional effects (NRSI) and thus confounding, survival bias, and publication bias are unavoidable. The heterogeneity in patient groupings with different transfusion ratios limits the pooling of data and therefore no meta-analysis or meaningful data synthesis was possible. In addition, patients who were treated with modern endovascular techniques for repair of rAAA were not clearly included in any of the studies, and thus this review has limited generalizability. The overall quality of the evidence is very low mostly due to the aforementioned risk of bias and heterogeneity. Lastly, this review is subject to language bias in that the reviewers only speak and read English.

CONCLUSIONS

From the data presented in this review, a higher ratio of FFP:PRBC confers a survival benefit in patients undergoing open surgical repair for rAAA. However, there is a severe risk of bias in the studies presented and the quality of evidence is very low. Overall, further research is warranted.

ARTICLE HIGHLIGHTS

Type of Research:

Systematic review

Key Findings:

Seven observational studies were included in this review. Three found an associated decrease in mortality with a high ratio of FFP:PRBC, and the remaining studies found no association. No meta-analysis was performed given significant heterogeneity. We found a serious overall risk of bias and very low quality of evidence.

Take home Message:

A balanced transfusion ratio of blood products that is whole-blood-like may have a survival benefit in patients with a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm that undergo open surgical repair. However, further research is warranted.
