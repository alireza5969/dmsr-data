Psychosocial Outcomes in Pediatric Patients with Ventricular Assist Devices and Their Families: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Background:

Ventricular assist device (VAD) therapy has provided pediatric patients with severe heart failure new therapeutic options. However, pediatric patients and families receiving VAD therapy also experience psychosocial challenges. No synthesis of the literature on psychosocial outcomes within the pediatric VAD population has been conducted; thus, the current review sought to systematically investigate the impact of pediatric VAD on patient, parent, and family psychosocial outcomes and assess variables associated with poorer outcomes.

Methods:

Literature searches were conducted in PsycInfo, PubMed, and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature databases and full texts were assessed according to pre-established inclusion criteria. Main findings and study quality were reviewed.

Results:

Sixteen studies were included in the present review. Findings highlighted the likelihood for psychological sequelae among pediatric patients and families receiving VAD therapy, including decreased psychological and emotional well-being, elevated stress, and difficulty coping with changes to family dynamics and responsibilities. Health-related quality of life was reported comparable to that of healthy peers and children with other cardiac conditions.

Conclusions:

Patients and families on VAD therapy experience significant difficulties in psychological well-being and challenges related to adjustment. The current review underscores the importance of ongoing support for families and continued assessment of psychosocial functioning across VAD support. Three critical periods for increased distress were identified: 1) immediately following VAD implantation, 2) discharge home and the weeks following discharge, and 3) long-term VAD therapy.

In the past 30 years, ventricular assist device (VAD) therapy has provided pediatric patients with severe heart failure new therapeutic options while awaiting treatment decision, recovery, or transplantation.1 Since the first reported use of pediatric VADs in the 1990s,2 pediatric VAD therapy has continued to increase. Recent estimates suggest that as many as one-third of pediatric patients who undergo heart transplantation are supported by VADs.3 The emerging literature suggests that pediatric patients supported by VADs have similar survival after transplant when compared to patients without VADs, despite the VAD population having greater illness severity prior to implant.4 Today, VAD therapy allows some pediatric patients to survive to transplant who may have previously suffered mortality from severe heart failure.4

Despite increases in survival, VAD therapy is associated with other inherent medical risks, such as infection and stroke.5 VAD therapy is also associated with increased psychosocial challenges for pediatric patients and their caregivers. Although children often discharge from the hospital while on VAD therapy, the demands of VAD care are significant, including frequent dressing changes, daily medications, and regular medical follow-up. Additionally, wait times for transplantation can be long,6 inducing tremendous stress and uncertainty for children and their families.7 Psychosocial distress and challenges associated with heart transplant have been shown to continue into the post-transplant years and across patients and family members.8–11

In adult populations, VAD implantation often contributes to improvements in patient quality of life12,13; however, anxiety and depressive symptoms remain prevalent.14 Caregivers of adults with VADs are also at risk for increased psychological distress, including diminished quality of life following their loved one’s VAD implant.15 Prior literature suggests that pediatric patients and families who have received or are receiving VAD therapy experience psychosocial challenges, consistent with findings in the adult literature. This is also largely consistent with previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses across other pediatric chronic illness groups which demonstrate that pediatric patients and their families are at risk for poorer psychosocial outcomes relative to healthy peers.16–18 However, no such synthesis of the literature on psychosocial outcomes has occurred within the pediatric VAD population.

Building upon the current adult VAD literature, as well as child chronic illness and transplant findings, this study aimed to review and summarize the existing literature on psychosocial outcomes in pediatric VAD patients and their families as guided by the following questions: (i) What is the impact of pediatric VAD on patient psychosocial outcomes?, (ii) What is the impact of pediatric VAD on parent and family psychosocial outcomes?, and (iii) What variables are associated with poorer patient and parent/family psychosocial outcomes in the pediatric VAD population? This systematic review will provide targets for intervention and underscore future research directions in this rapidly growing field.

Methods

Search Strategy

Literature searches were conducted in PsycInfo, PubMed, and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) databases. Authors searched databases using the following terms: 1) “child$ $,” “youth,” “adolescen$ $,” “teen$ $,” “infant,” “pediatric,” “paediatric,” 2) “Ventricular assist device,” “VAD,” “mechanical circulatory support,” “MCS” 3) “patient,” “parent,” “mother,” “father,” “caregiver,” “family” and 4) “depression,” “anxiety,” “mood,” “trauma,” “stress,” “distress,” “marital,” “functioning,” “coping,” “adjustment,” “adaptation.” The ancestry method was also utilized, in which authors examined reference sections of studies meeting the review’s predefined inclusion criteria for additional studies examining psychosocial functioning among pediatric patients who have received a VAD.

Inclusion Criteria

To account for recent medical advances in the field and to reflect the most relevant literature, the following predefined inclusion criteria were established prior to conducting the search: 1) published within the last decade (i.e., January 2010 – September 2020), 2) included pediatric patients (i.e., the majority of the sample under age 18) or caregivers of pediatric patients who have received a VAD, 3) contained either a quantitative or qualitative report of patient, caregiver, or family psychosocial functioning, 4) published in a peer-reviewed journal, and 5) published in the English language.

Data Extraction and Study Coding

Titles and abstracts of all search results were reviewed independently by two authors (KR and MC). The first author (KR) extracted data from each study, including sample characteristics (sample size, patient age), VAD characteristics (bridge to transplant and/or bridge to recovery, VAD duration, time since VAD), use of a comparison group, measures, and results. Study quality was evaluated by two authors (KR and AM). The National Institute of Health National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies19 was adapted based on applicable criteria for the current literature. Assessment items included reference to an established research objective or study aim, description of study population including time period and geographical location of recruitment, participation rate of > 50%, established selection criteria and procedures, consideration of sample size, statistical power, or effect sizes in interpretation, defined and validated outcome measures, and consideration of potential confounding variables in analyses. Each study was assessed for all items and received a “yes” when quality indicators were met and a “no” when quality indicators were not met. Additional responses included “not applicable (NA),” “not reported (NR),” and “cannot determine (CD).” Based on these indicators, studies received a rating of “good,” “fair,” or “poor” quality. Discrepancies in ratings were discussed between the two rating authors and an agreement was made. The senior author (MC) reviewed 20% of study data extracted to assess accuracy and reliability. As there was significant variability in sample size and methodology, a meta-analysis was not conducted. Therefore, results represent a narrative synthesis of literature.

Results

Study Characteristics

Following PRISMA guidelines, our search yielded 613 studies after the removal of duplicate results (n=101). See Figure 1 for the PRISMA flow diagram. Of the studies excluded, the majority did not include a pediatric VAD sample (n=473) or did not assess patient, caregiver, or family psychosocial functioning (n=82). The remaining 27 articles were full text reviewed to assess eligibility, for which 16 met criteria. For a full summary of study results, see Table 1. Within these 16 studies, approximately half (n=9) were published within the last five years. The majority of studies (n=11) included patients who were bridged to transplantation with a VAD, though three studies included patients who were bridged to transplantation or recovery. Two studies did not specify whether patients were bridged to transplantation or recovery. The included studies were conducted internationally, including the United States (n=6), Canada (n=3), Turkey (n=3), Germany (n=2), the United Kingdom (n=1), and Australia (n=1).

Study Quality Assessment

A composite of included studies’ quality is presented in Figure 2. Across included studies, all (N=16; 100%) explicitly defined the research objective or study aim. The majority of studies established inclusion criteria and consistent selection procedures (n=12; 75%),20–31 defined the study population, including time period and geographical location of recruitment (n=11; 69%),20,21,23–31 and utilized defined outcomes with validated measures (n=11; 69%).20,21,23,24,26,27,29,31–34 Ten studies (63%) reported a participation rate of greater than 50%,20–27,29,31 though the remaining six studies did not report participation or attrition results. Notably, few studies justified sample size or utilized effect sizes (n=4; 25%),21,29,30,35 and few statistically considered potential confounding variables in analyses (n=4; 25%),21,23,26,29 though six studies utilized qualitative data and thematic analyses, and thus statistical assessment of confounding variables was rated NA.22,25,28,30,33,35 Based on the results of this quality assessment, 50% of included studies were rated as “good” quality (n=8)20,21,23,24,26,27,29,31 and 50% of included studies were rated as “fair” quality (n=8).22,25,28,30,32–35 No studies were excluded from the current literature reviewed based on the quality assessment.

What is the Impact of Pediatric VAD on Patient Psychosocial Outcomes?

Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQOL)

Four studies examined HRQOL among pediatric patients with VAD. Three studies reported HRQOL comparable to that of healthy peers.20,23,24 Among 21 pediatric patients who were bridged to transplant with VAD support, no significant differences in HRQOL outcomes were found when compared to pediatric patients who received a heart transplant without prior VAD support or pediatric patients with other cardiovascular disease.23 Only one study reported poorer HRQOL when comparing pediatric patients supported by VADs to healthy peers, pediatric patients who experienced a heart transplant without VAD support, and pediatric patients with heart disease.27 Overall, available literature suggests that pediatric patients who undergo VAD therapy experience comparable HRQOL to healthy peers and pediatric patients with other cardiac conditions.

Psychological Functioning

Four studies examined aspects of psychological functioning among pediatric patients supported with VADs. In a recent national database review, patients bridged to transplant with VAD support were significantly more likely to receive a psychiatric diagnosis and to begin antidepressant medication treatment when compared to pediatric patients who went straight to transplant.21 Forty percent of pediatric patients bridged to transplant with a VAD received psychiatric diagnoses, the most common of which were adjustment disorders and mood disorders, including anxiety and depression.21 Among pediatric patients who had a VAD and began antidepressant medication, 84% began this treatment at the time of VAD implantation or shortly after.21 In a qualitative investigation of four children’s experience with VAD, children reported mixed feelings about their VAD support, including both fear and relief.25

Inconsistencies in the literature were found as it relates to the long-term effect of VAD implantation on children’s psychological functioning. One study reported that the majority of pediatric patients (62.5%, n=5) experienced psychiatric symptoms at both one-month post-VAD implantation and six-months post-VAD implantation.32 However, a case study of three pediatric patients on long-term VAD support reported improvements in psychological functioning following VAD implantation and return to school.33 Overall, the literature to date suggests that pediatric patients with VAD therapy are at risk for increased psychiatric conditions, which may persist over time.

Cognitive Functioning

Four studies in the current review examined aspects of cognitive functioning among pediatric patients who underwent VAD implantation. Per review of a national database, approximately one-quarter of pediatric patients who received VAD support had a probable or definite intellectual disability diagnosis, with those receiving VAD support prior to transplantation having a significantly higher rate of probable intellectual disability diagnosis than patients who went straight to transplant.26 This is inconsistent with findings among nine pediatric patients which found that patients bridged to transplant with VAD support scored on average higher than patients straight to transplant across several domains, including general intellectual functioning, working memory, and verbal comprehension.29 Similarly, the majority of participants in a sample of pediatric patients supported by VAD therapy scored in the “good” to “average” range across measures of executive functioning and motor control.20 Among 16 patients who received VAD support, the presence of abnormal neurological findings was associated with significantly lower intellectual functioning, visual-motor integration, and adaptive functioning.31 Thus, available literature is mixed as to whether VAD support is associated with poorer cognitive functioning among this population.

VAD-Specific Concerns

Three qualitative studies examined patient’s beliefs about and experiences managing their VAD. While patients reported some relief and value for the normalcy and improved health that VAD support provided,25,36 several concerns were noted. Patients reported hesitation when faced with discharge home from the hospital while on VAD support, and expressed concerns regarding whether discharge home would impact their position on the organ transplant waiting list.35 Patients felt unprepared for the physical experience of having a VAD, including the actual size of the device and the placement on their bodies.25 Patients also discussed the challenges and risks associated with VAD support, including the importance of maintaining a charge and having additional batteries, and the experience of VAD as a constant presence, both internally (e.g., humming and vibrations from the device) and externally (e.g., impact on appearance).36

What is the Impact of Pediatric VAD on Parent and Family Psychosocial Outcomes?

Parent Outcomes

Five studies in the current review examined parent psychosocial outcomes within the pediatric VAD population. All studies consistently demonstrated that VAD implantation was associated with feelings of fear and an increased sense of responsibility among caregivers, as assessed via qualitative interviews and questionnaires.22,25,28,35 While it is the goal of many VAD centers to discharge patients to their home, parents qualitatively reported that discharge from the hospital was associated with increased care demands on the parent, heightened fear, sleep deprivation and increased concern over their ability to adequately care for other children within the family.35 Notably, significant parental concern surrounding discharge remained present even among caregivers who appropriately demonstrated their ability to independently care for their child on VAD support while in the hospital.35

Three studies highlighted the experience of mothers specifically as primary caregivers for their children after VAD implantation.22,34,35 Among a sample of six mothers, all mothers qualitatively reported reduced independence for themselves and increased responsibility for providing around-the-clock supervision for their child with a VAD.22 Mothers also reported adjusting their career and household roles, with some mothers resigning from employment to be their child’s primary caregiver.22 Increased anxiety and depressive symptoms were also common among eight mothers who were designated as the primary caregiver for children with VADs, particularly once home from the hospital.34 However, with increased time elapsed since VAD implantation and following bridging to transplant, some improvement was noted in maternal anxiety and depressive symptoms.34 Notably, the one mother in this study whose child was still awaiting transplant 6 months after VAD implantation reported increases in anxiety and depressive symptoms.34 Having other trained caregivers present to help with their children’s care provided mothers with the opportunities to engage in self-care without having to worry about the child with a VAD.22

In one study, despite the majority of parents feeling they received inadequate information regarding the anticipated course of VAD support and that no acceptable alternative existed, the majority of parents also felt that they made the right decision to consent to VAD.25 Parents who had the opportunity to talk to another patient or parent about VAD implantation reported this as an overall positive experience.25 Parents and families continued to have hope for heart transplantation in the future while trying to maintain a normal life on VAD support.25

Family Outcomes

Four studies examined family outcomes in the present review. The transition to home after discharge presented many challenges to families. Family coping was reportedly worsened by discharge,35 especially in the first few weeks post-discharge while families adjusted to the new medical regimen surrounding VAD care.28 Physical modifications to the home were often necessary to ensure safety and to fully accommodate having a child with a VAD, including grounding electrical circuits, arranging furniture to support VAD equipment, and purchasing additional supplies needed to care for the VAD.22 Frequently, families reported financial burdens associated with providing care at home that were not covered by health insurance.28 It was also reported that as their child receiving VAD therapy felt better and activity levels increased, family negotiations surrounding necessary activity restrictions emerged as significant areas of conflict.28

Despite a major reported benefit of discharge being families’ ability to resume normal life; the majority of families did not feel this benefit outweighed the risks of potentially life-threatening complications while at home.35 Daily life at home was often interrupted by clinic visits, tests and medication administration.28 Families reported heavy reliance on the healthcare team as a source of support.22,25,28,35 Proximity to the VAD center and the potential need for family relocation to accommodate their child’s VAD medical care was an important consideration among family members.28

What Variables are Associated with Poorer Patient and Family Psychosocial Outcomes in the Pediatric VAD Population?

Few studies have examined variables associated with psychosocial outcomes among the pediatric VAD population. The presence of neurological comorbidities or complications developing during VAD support, such as presence of seizure activity, was associated with poorer physical HRQOL among pediatric patients bridged to transplant with VAD support,23 as well as lower cognitive and adaptive functioning.31 Among those bridged to transplant with a VAD, pediatric patients who were older and had greater time elapsed since transplant reported greater independence in communication about their cardiac condition as assessed via cardiac-specific HRQOL.23 Improvements in mothers’ anxiety and depressive symptoms were found as greater time elapsed since VAD implantation.34 Only one study examined other variables associated with outcomes and found no significant relationships between patient HRQOL and VAD duration, age at transplantation, pulsatile vs. continuous support, acquired vs. congenital disease, race, or socioeconomic status.23

Discussion

Results of the current review present the first known synthesis of the literature regarding psychosocial functioning in the pediatric VAD population across patient, parent, and family outcomes. Overall, results underscore the likelihood for psychological sequelae, including decreased psychological and emotional well-being, elevated stress, and difficulty coping with changes to family dynamics and responsibilities. Qualitative reports consistently demonstrated the added burden of VAD implantation on patient and family outcomes, particularly upon discharge home. These findings are largely consistent with previous reviews across other chronic illness groups, including elevated rates of parenting stress and poorer family functioning among pediatric organ transplant recipients11 and deficits in psychological functioning among youth with severe congenital heart disease.37

Much of the extant literature suggests that while pediatric patients on VAD support experience HRQOL comparable to that of healthy peers and other chronic illness populations,20,23,24 VAD implantation was associated with elevated rates of psychiatric diagnosis and treatment21,32 and reported negative feelings about their VAD.25,35,36 In non-VAD pediatric cardiology populations, HRQOL was found to be more comparable to that of healthy peers than those with other chronic conditions, such as spina bifida.16,38 This suggests differential effects of pediatric medical conditions on HRQOL, particularly across conditions with varying levels of physical and functional impairment.16,38 However, as varying levels of impairment may also be present within the pediatric VAD population, ongoing assessment of HRQOL across the VAD implantation course is warranted. Additionally, it is not clear from the limited research whether VAD implantation is related to deficits in cognitive functioning,26,29,31 which may further impact psychosocial functioning and has been associated with disease severity among pediatric patients with congenital heart disease.37 Pediatric patients on VAD support appear to demonstrate deficits in psychological functioning, though the effects of long-term VAD implantation and long-term post-VAD functioning are not well understood. This is largely consistent with reported increases in psychological distress among other pediatric cardiac populations, including children with pacemakers and implantable cardioverter defibrillators39,40 and those undergoing open-heart surgery for congenital heart disease (CHD).41 While some psychiatric diagnoses may be present prior to initiation of VAD support, research suggests that VAD implantation is associated with increased rates of diagnoses and onset of mental health treatment21, thus, assessment of patient psychological functioning pre-VAD implantation and throughout the VAD course is warranted.

Parents and families of pediatric VAD patients consistently report increased fear and caregiving demands, particularly with regard to discharge home.22,25,28,35 Families reported difficulty adjusting in the first few weeks following discharge, with significant physical and lifestyle modifications needed.22,28 This is consistent with reported elevations in general parenting stress and altered family functioning among pediatric organ transplant recipients,11 and qualitative reports of increased stress and difficulties surrounding discharge home among families of children with a variety of medical conditions, including organ transplantation42 and acute lymphoblastic leukemia.43 Families on VAD support reported recognizing the value of discharge home as a return to some semblance of familial normalcy, though this benefit did not outweigh the perceived risks.35 Several experiences were noted to mitigate poorer psychosocial functioning among parents and families within the VAD population, including the presence of social support to assist with caregiving and opportunities to talk with other families who have experienced VAD implantation and the healthcare team for support.22,25 Results of the current review suggest that parents experience elevated distress in response to discharge home with their child on VAD support, and thus, increased attention to discharge planning and following up with parents soon after discharge is warranted to assist parents during the challenging transition home.

Few studies examined variables associated with improved or worsened psychosocial outcomes among the pediatric VAD population. The presence of neurological complications or abnormalities was associated with poorer patient functioning across two studies, including physical HRQOL and cognitive functioning.23,31 Neurological complications or abnormalities may result in greater patient difficulties in managing VAD care, including limitations of functional independence or difficulties organizing and planning ahead for necessary care, such as having extra battery packs for their VAD. These difficulties may further relate to other aspects of functioning, such as patient psychological and emotional well-being, as well as increased parental stress and treatment responsibility. Although VAD-related neurological events, such as stroke, are declining with increased use of direct thrombin inhibitors44 and quality improvement efforts through learning collaboratives,45 regular neuropsychological monitoring and intervention should occur for pediatric VAD patients.

Among patients and caregivers, greater time elapsed since initiation of VAD support and resulting bridge to transplant was associated with improved outcomes, including decreased maternal anxiety and depression and greater patient HRQOL.23,34 However, among those remaining on long-term VAD support without a transplant, caregiver anxiety and depressive symptoms persisted.34 Another potential contributor to this finding could be the considerable stress of waiting for transplant for those on longer-term VAD support, particularly the worry of a complication or death occurring prior to transplantation. Findings highlight three critical periods for caregiver support and intervention: 1) immediately following VAD implantation, 2) upon discharge home and the weeks following discharge, and 3) long-term VAD therapy.

Several limitations to the current body of literature must be noted, which inform future research directions for the field. Very few studies have examined factors associated with improved or worsened psychosocial outcomes, and thus limited information was available for the current review. Future research should examine the duration of implantation as a potential moderator of psychosocial outcomes among patients and families, as literature among pediatric patients with implantable cardioverter defibrillators suggests that longer duration of implantation is associated with poorer functioning and HRQOL.39 Additionally, greater disease severity has been associated with poor cognitive functioning among youth with CHD, as well as higher rates of anxiety among children with pacemakers and implantable cardioverter defibrillators.37,40 Future research should examine disease severity and chronicity in relation to psychosocial outcomes among children on VAD support, including whether VAD support is used for bridging to transplant or bridging to recovery. Given limited research in the pediatric VAD population, both qualitative and quantitative studies were included to reflect all available literature regarding psychosocial functioning in this population. Some of the constructs of interest, including variables thought to moderate outcomes and inform potential interventions, were only reflected in single qualitative studies, and thus statistical relationships cannot be determined. Qualitative reports in the current review suggest a protective role of social support and added burden of financial strain and distance from hospital on parent functioning. Future research should seek to quantitatively examine these variables in relation to parent and child psychosocial outcomes to further understand potential associations. Studies in the current review were also limited by small samples, with 14 of the 16 included studies having sample sizes of 25 participants or less. While this is reflective of the relatively rare occurrence of VAD implantation at single centers, future research would be bolstered by multi-site collaborations to increase sample sizes and power to detect relationships. Lastly, little is known about the long-term psychosocial impacts of pediatric VAD implantation. Longitudinal investigations are warranted to determine how long-term VAD support impacts patient and family psychosocial functioning, as well as longitudinal post-VAD outcomes.

This review highlights several important practice considerations. Increased preparation for families in anticipation of discharge home while on VAD support is critically important. This may include further psychoeducation on what to expect upon discharge, particularly from other families or support groups with greater understanding regarding caring for a loved one on VAD support. As the current literature suggests that families who demonstrate technical ability in managing the medical tasks in an inpatient setting still experience fear of discharge,35 competence in completing medical tasks should not be the only indicator used to assess for discharge readiness. Use of telemedicine and remote monitoring may also mitigate caregiver stress and burden once home. Additionally, results of the current review underscore the importance of regular psychosocial and mental health screening of both patients and families throughout their hospitalizations and outpatient care to identify those at risk for poorer outcomes and provide necessary interventions or resources. As the review highlights, assessment of HRQOL alone is unlikely to fully capture psychosocial functioning among VAD patients.

The results of the current review must be interpreted in light of several limitations. Given the limited available research on psychosocial functioning in the pediatric VAD population and the benefits of findings from both quantitative and qualitative data, studies of both methodologies were included. This is a strength of the current review as it synthesizes all available literature on the topic but also a limitation, as comparisons of results between studies was often not feasible. Additionally, the limited number of quantitative studies and inconsistencies in methodology precluded a meta-analytic investigation of psychosocial functioning in the pediatric VAD population. While every effort was made to include all published literature on this topic, including the use of broad search terms to capture a variety of studies, it remains possible that studies were missed, as the language regarding psychosocial outcomes is variable and inconsistent between studies. Based on study quality assessment, 50% of included studies were considered “fair” quality. Results of studies with lower methodologically quality should be interpreted with caution, and future research should seek to address these methodological concerns, including use of validated measures, reporting of effect sizes, and transparency of recruiting and attrition.

To our knowledge, the current systematic literature review is the first to synthesize literature on psychosocial outcomes among patients, parents and families within the pediatric VAD population. Results indicated notable difficulties in patient and family psychological well-being and challenges related to adjustment following discharge home on VAD support. Notably, HRQOL was comparable to that of healthy peers in the majority of studies. Taken together, findings present several directions for future research, including longitudinal investigations of psychosocial functioning during and post-VAD support, as well as greater understanding of which factors may improve or worsen outcomes. Clinically, results support the need for pre-implantation psychological assessment, ongoing mental health assessment and support throughout the VAD course for both patients and families, and specific intervention regarding discharge planning.
