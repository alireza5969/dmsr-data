Abstract

Databases were searched, for the past 10 years, to identify studies that addressed the utility of patient-reported outcomes (PROs) in patients receiving chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy in patients with hematological malignancies. Among 280 records, 3 articles covering 206 patients were eligible. The data were prospectively collected at multiple time points. The compliance rates were 70-94%. There was an inverse relationship between fatigue and social function among adults. The quality of life (QoL) improvement and ability to complete PROs were linked to disease status. About 40% of adults reported at least some cognitive difficulties, with a detrimental impact on mental and physical health status. In adults, the most commonly reported cognitive impairment was memory difficulties. Depression was associated with cognitive difficulties. Younger adults were at higher risk of long-term poor mental health, anxiety, and depression. Among pediatrics/adolescents, emotional dysfunction improves over-time. QoL status improved over-time, yet severe cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity caused delayed improvement. Information regarding whether the PROs were integrated into medical records and clinical guidelines are lacking. Utilizing PROs in patients on CAR T-cell therapy seems feasible and informative. Larger sample sizes and using validated PRO tools at different time points, remain unmet needs.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy is a therapeutic breakthrough treatment for hematological malignancies, with proven durable responses and improved survival rates 1-5. Although most of the adverse events associated with CAR T-cell therapy are generally manageable, the unique cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurological toxicities can be dramatic and potentially lethal. The incidence rate of CAR T-cell-induced toxicities ranged from 57% to 93% across different studies, with up to 50% of patients experience grade 3 or higher toxicities 67, 89. Potentially life-threatening toxicities after CAR T-cell therapy may result in multi-organ dysfunction 10, 117, 8 or require intensive care support 7. These acute toxicities usually develop and peak within 1 to 2 weeks after CAR T-cell infusion 12, with a duration of 5 to 6 days 13-156. Further, some CAR T-cell patients develop long-term chronic toxicities 16. CAR T-cell therapy is increasingly being used as a standard care option to treat hematological malignancies, and hence there is an unmet need to improve the management guidelines of patients who are treated outside of clinical trials setting.

Incorporation of patient-reported outcomes (PROs) into the management of cancer patients has been shown to improve patient satisfaction and treatment outcomes in various cancer disease settings 17-1918, 20, 21, including survival and quality of life (QoL). Our team published results from clinical trials where PROs were monitored and triaged when reach pre-defined critical thresholds and subsequently alert emails were sent to providers to take clinical actions. Such intervention has proven to improve the functional outcomes after surgery for lung cancers 17, 18. Further, PROs are considered a better indicator for toxicity compared to clinician-reported outcomes 6. Over the past few years, significant achievements have been made in grading toxicities of CAR T-cell therapy and several management plans have been published. However, PROs have not been incorporated into the CAR T-cell toxicity grading and/or treatment guidelines. CAR T-cell therapy is a novel treatment with a unique manufacturing process and toxicity profile. This leads to several challenges for selecting the best PRO assessment tool, identifying the optimal timing and frequency of PROs administration, and for incorporation of PROs in the routine care for these high-risk patients. The current systematic review aims to characterize the utility of PROs in patients with hematological malignancies receiving CAR T-cell therapy.

Methods

Our systematic review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement 22.

Search strategy:

An extensive search of the literature was performed by a qualified medical librarian (RSH). Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), Scopus, CIINAHL (Ebsco), and Web of Science (Clarivate Analytics) were queried using both controlled vocabulary and natural language terms for chimeric antigen receptor therapy, immunotherapy, and patient-reported outcomes. Results were limited to English-only citations published within the past 10 years (01/01/2010-04/21/2020). After deduplication, 280 unique records were identified. The complete Medline search strategy is included in Supplementary Table 1. Reference lists of the selected articles for inclusion were manually searched for additional eligible articles.

Study Selection and Inclusion Criteria:

Database search results were screened by 2 medically qualified preliminary reviewers (M.K and J.J). Eligible studies were defined as those that addressed PROs and included patients of any age and gender who had received CAR T-cell therapy for hematological malignancies, including both clinical trials and observational studies. For the initial screening step, we included original studies (i.e., case reports, case series, and observational studies). We also included cases that were published in an abstract format. In the following screening step, the full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed for further eligibility by the same 2 review team members. Any disagreement between the 2 review members over a study’s eligibility was resolved through consensus; conflicts were adjudicated by a third independent expert reviewer (S.S) who also confirmed the adequacy of studies on the basis of previously defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies published only in abstract format were excluded. The PRISMA flow diagram outlining the study selection process is available in Figure 1.

Outcomes:

In this systematic review, we identified studies with full published reports that include enough data describing how they used PRO assessment tools in patients receiving CAR T-cell therapy. Additionally, we collected and discussed PRO data reports that addressed the challenges of using PROs to assess CAR T-cell-induced toxicities and the clinical implementation of PROs in the management of patients with hematological malignancies on CAR T-cell therapy.

Data Extraction, Quality Assessment, and Statistical Analysis:

Inter-observer agreement between the 2 reviewers was calculated using the Cohen kappa test 23. A data extraction form was created in an Excel document to collect data for each eligible study and was handled by two team members (U.G & M.K). Extracted variables included: study characteristics (name, publication year, country, design, primary and secondary outcomes, number of patients, the start and end year of each study [duration] and major limitations), treatment and disease characteristics (type of treatment, intent of treatment, previous treatments, and disease type), and study population characteristics (age, and gender).

To characterize PRO assessment tools, we collected information about the timing and frequency of administration and their relation to CAR T-cell infusion, who provided the PRO questionnaires (nurse, caregiver, or family member), where and how PROs were collected (in person, during the clinic visit, or remotely at home, and via paper, email or online). To address the clinical utilization and implementation of PROs, we determined whether PROs were added to the medical records if there were guidelines for toxicity management based on PROs, if there was any treatment interruption /hospitalization /Emergency room admission /treatment re-schedule based on PRO scores, and whether there were concerns from patients’ or providers’ perspectives about utilizing PROs. We also collected data on which PROs were recorded, the percentage of each PRO reported, and the highest PRO scores reported.

Data were summarized using descriptive statistics, with frequencies and percentages for dichotomous variables. The results were presented narratively and tabulated. The final report follows the PRISMA statement, (Figure 1). A critical appraisal of eligible studies was performed using The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool to assess risk of bias in randomized trials 24 and Newcastle-Ottawa Scale Scores of Observational Studies, (Supplementary Table 2) 25.

Results

Study characteristics

Among 280 searched records, 3 full-text articles met our criteria 26-28, the remaining 277 papers were excluded primarily because of an irrelevant topic, duplicate records, or review articles, (Table 1). The 3 studies were published from 2019 to 2020, and included a total of 206 patients. Two studies covered adult populations, the first for patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) chronic lymphocytic leukemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) 26, and the second for patients with R/R diffuse large B cell lymphoma 27. The third study included only pediatrics/adolescents with a diagnosis of R/R B-cell ALL 28.

PROs characteristics

Tools, response rate, timing, frequency, and methods of administration:

PROs were assessed prospectively in the 3 studies (Table 2). The median follow-up duration in these 3 studies ranged from 9.9 months to 5 years after CAR T-cell infusion. Ruark et al. 26 assessed patient-reported neuropsychiatric status at one time point (1 to 5 years after CAR T-cell infusion). The other 2 studies focused on the first year after treatment and assessed patient-reported QoL at multiple time points during the first 12 to 18 months (baseline, months 3,6,12, and 18 in Maziarz et al. and months 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 in Laetsch et al., respectively). 27, 28 PROs were self-administered, via papers, in all 3 studies. Ruark et al 26provided the option of electronic administration via emails.

Ruark et al. 26 utilized the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) 29, 30 with an overall total response rate of 76.9%. They used the PROMIS Scale v1.2 Global Health module, which includes 10 items assessing the overall physical and mental health, as well as the PROMIS-29 Profile v2.1, which contains 7 domains that assess symptoms within the past 7 days each contain 4-item short forms. In addition, Ruark et al. used 30 additional questions, addressing patients’ demographic data, cancer disease status, additional diagnoses and symptoms that patients may have developed since CAR T cell therapy, current medications, and evaluation of cognitive functioning status.

Maziarz et al. 27 utilized the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy- Lymphoma (FACT-Lym) 31 and the Short Form-36 (SF-36) 32 Health Survey, with overall completion rates of 94%, 76% , 81%, 86%, and 65% at the baseline and at months 3, 6, 12, and 18, respectively. The FACT-Lym includes the FACT-General (FACT-G) and the lymphoma subscale (Lym S). The FACT-G assesses QoL via 27 general questions in 4 primary domains, with a total score range of 0-108. Lym S is a 15-question survey that assesses patient response to lymphoma-associated treatment and symptoms, with a total score range of 0-60. The SF-36 Health Survey 32 includes 36 items with 8 subscales, with a total score range of 0-100.

Laetsch et al. 28 used the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) 33 and European Quality of Life-5 Dimensions Questionnaire (EQ-5D) 34. At day 28, QoL data were submitted by 77% of 48 responders for each measure of PedsQL. PedsQL includes emotional, social, and school functioning subscale scores (five items each); physical (eight items), and psychosocial health summary scores and a total score. The EQ-5D questionnaire has five dimensions and a visual analogue scale (VAS) that records the self-rated overall health state. PedsQL and VAS scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better QoL.

Detailed descriptions of the abovementioned PRO assessment tools are included in Supplementary Table 3.

PRO assessments results:

In a prospective observational study by Ruark et al., 26 37% of patients reported at least some cognitive difficulties: 35% reported memory difficulties, 30% reported difficulty finding words, and 20% reported clinically meaningful depression or anxiety. There was also a very strong inverse correlation between fatigue and social function 26. Higher scores of pain interference, sleep disturbance, fatigue, depression, anxiety, physical function, and social function were associated with more cognitive difficulties. The presence of depression before CAR T-cell infusion was associated with a significantly higher incidence of cognitive difficulties after therapy 26. Post-CAR T-cell cognitive difficulties were associated with poorer global mental health and global physical health 26. It was also noted in this study that younger patients had worse long-term global mental health, anxiety, and depression 26. Further, global mental health scores were at least 1 standard deviation lower than that of the general population. However, the patient-reported similar scores of global physical health compared with the general population.

Maziarz et al. 27 found that both the PRO completion compliance rate and sustained improvement in QoL were linked to disease status at each time point. The largest mean change in improved scores from the baseline to month 12 were included in the emotional domain.

In a study by Laetsch et al., 28 of only pediatrics and adolescents, the largest mean change in improved scores was noted for emotional function at 28 days after CAR T-cell infusion, and the least mean change was for school functioning. There was an observed improvement in all QoL measures at 3 and 12 months compared to the baseline score; the greatest improvement was observed for physical functioning, and the least was for social functioning. Further, the greatest improvement in the EQ-5D-VAS score was at month 12 compared to the baseline score. This change was above the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). Approximately 46%, 81%, and 72% of the patients achieved MCID for the total score of PedsQl at 28 days, 3 months, and 6 months, respectively. Severe CRS was associated with a lower mean change in improved QoL scores at day 28.

None of the studies reported whether the PROs were added to the medical records, if there were guidelines for toxicity management based on PROs, or whether PROs were used to guide treatment interruptions, hospitalizations, or emergency room visits.

Discussion

The use of PROs for CAR T-cell therapy patients remains investigational. Critical challenges still exist in the implementation of PROs in the routine clinical care of patients receiving CAR T-cell therapy. In this comprehensive systematic review, only 3 published full articles were identified. These studies addressed the use of PROs in CAR T-cell patients with B-cell lymphoid malignancies. Cognitive impairment, emotional well-being, and QoL were the main domains that have been addressed in these studies, in addition to symptoms burden. Thirty-seven percent of patients in Ruark et al. reported cognitive impairment 26, Maziarz et al. observed that the disease status and response to CAR T-cell therapy were associated with improvement in QoL.27 and Laetsch et al. 28 reported a temporal pattern between the QoL scores and the severity of CRS and neurotoxicity 28. We didn’t identify any studies that explore and validate standardized PRO tools in CAR T-cell therapy patients or addressed the role of PROs at multiple time points in assessing the trajectory of PROs overtime. Furthermore, it is unknown how PROs can be best implemented and incorporated in clinical management guidelines for CAR T-cell patients.

Most CAR T-cell clinical trials list PROs as exploratory or secondary endpoints which leads to substantial weaknesses in the quality of the analyzed outcomes from the patients who have completed PROs in these studies 2827. Further, some PRO models compare the resultant scores with normative scores of the general population, rather than those for a specific age group, or particularly from cancer populations, which in turn limits data interpretation and generalizability 3536, 37. Maziarz et al. 27 utilized both anchor- and distribution-based methods to define the MCID 38, 39 as an alternative approach for better interpretation.

Including multiple critical time points for PROs collection is important to assess the impact of CAR T-cell therapy on the trajectory of patients-reported symptoms in the acute and late phases after CAR T-cell infusion. The majority of acute toxicities occur in the first 2-4 weeks after CAR T-cell infusion, of which the unique CRS and ICANS are the most frequent and potentially serious adverse events. Hence, the unmet need to explore unique PRO tools at different time points. For instance, more frequent assessments using PRO tools with short recall memory questions are needed during the first month when acute toxicities are expected. However, less frequent assessments using PRO tools with longer recall time can be suitable after 1 month of CAR T-cell therapy. None of the published studies addressed the utility of PROs at different critical time points. Maziarz et al. 27 rightly claimed that the lack of QoL assessment during the first month of infusion was a study limitation. On the other hand, Laetsch et al. 28 assessed PROs on day 28 but not during the first month, while Ruark et al. was designed to assess the long-term assessment after at least one year of completion of CAR T-cell therapy. Patrick et al. published preliminary data showing that the QoL status drops and then improves in keeping with disease status during the first 3 months after infusion, especially with regards to scores of pain, fatigue, and general health status 40. In the acute phase, utilizing PRO models with a short recall time will help to capture the dramatic patterns of CAR T-cell therapy-related symptoms.

The long-term PRO data from patients on CAR T-cell therapy may not represent the actual patient-reported experience considering that only responders, patients with less severe illness, patients with less disease and toxicity burden, and patients who are alive are more likely to complete the PRO questionnaires, especially later in the treatment course. Some non-responders stopped reporting PROs at as early as 28 days because of treatment discontinuation, loss of follow-up, or death 28. In Maziarz et al. and Laetsch at al. 27, 28, patient-reported QoL data were collected up to 12 and 18 months after CAR T-cell therapy. However, Maziarz et al. 27, collected PROs only from treated patients who had achieved a complete response or partial response. The study by Ruark et al. 26 reported the longest follow-up up to 5 years after, but they have only included patients who survived at least 1 year after CAR T-cell therapy 26.

The nature of underlying cancer, tumor burden, and previous therapies may confound the accurate determination of the impact of CAR T-cell therapy on PROs, and it remains unclear whether using generalizable PRO modules (which are no disease- or treatment-specific) are more applicable in these settings. Maziarz et al. and Laetsch et al. gave detailed information about previous treatments 2627. Patients in the Ruark et al. study 26 received a median of 5 lines of therapy (including SCT in 27%) and 30% underwent allogenic SCT before CAR T-cell infusion. However, no subgroup analysis was reported accounting for previous treatments received. Some experts argue that using PRO models that are not disease nor treatment-specific are more applicable and representative in the setting of previously treated patients. We could see that Maziarz et al. 27 used a disease-specific PRO module (FACTLym) and reported associated improved QoL scores with the disease response. Although the selected cohort in Maziarz et al. was heavily pretreated and had failed SCT or ineligible for the procedure, the response rate was reasonable (86%, and 65% at months 12, and 18, respectively>) 27.

Compliance is a major challenge in the continued collection of PROs after CAR T-cell therapy. In Laetsch et al.’s study, overall compliance dropped from 86% to 24% between the baseline and month 12 of follow-up 28. A higher compliance rate (77%) at 1 year was reported by Ruark et al., which may be attributed to the option of electronic questionnaires to collect PROs26. While in Maziarz et al., 80% of the evaluable patients at each time point completed QOL assessment before 12 months, and 86% and 65% completed the PRO assessment at 12 and 18 months, respectively 27. Furthermore, pediatric patients represent a true challenge regarding PRO data collection and interpretation, an area which requires further exploration 41-43. Parents/caregivers may feel overwhelmed by all information about the disease response and expected outcomes. So, we should consider that the PROs assessment questionnaires need to be easy to understand and not require a long time to complete. Listening to parents/caregivers and assessing their needs and concerns may improve the PRO implementation in these special populations43. More issues need to be considered in studies of pediatric patients on CAR T-cell therapy: reporting the age at the time of completing PRO questionnaire rather than the age at the time of diagnosis and using appropriate tools that have been validated in the same age group 28

There is growing clinical and research interest in utilizing PROs during and after CAR T-cell therapy, but published data remains scarce, and prospective clinical trials are needed to improve the use and implementation of PROs in CAR T-cell therapy management. Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) panel expresses confidence in four existing tools (Patient-Reported Outcomes-Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, MD Anderson Symptom Inventory, the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire (EORTC-QLQ-C30), and the PROMIS. Yet, there is no concusses among CMS panel regarding the timing of administration of PROs 44. According to CMS, PRO measurement tools need to be patient-centered, scientifically acceptable, feasible, useable, psychometrically-acceptable, able to reflect collaboration and shared decision-making with patients, meaningful, amenable to change, and implementable45. Clinicians are looking for an ideal PRO tool which should be fit for purpose and user-friendly to detect early changes; a short recall timeframe is required with minimal burden on patients and caregivers. In addition, close monitoring of PROs during the early phase after therapy is needed to capture data on acute toxicity, which would allow early interventions to minimize symptoms burden, and perhaps prevent development or progression to higher CRS and ICAN toxicity grades. PROs at later time points are more important to assess long-term QoL and neurocognitive sequelae. Considering that patients on CAR T-cell therapy are usually heavily pre-treated, underlying cancer diagnosis and prior therapies received need to be considered while we are interpreting the PRO and toxicity scores. Likewise, baseline psychiatric and neurocognitive disorders and concomitant therapies should be accounted for. Furthermore, these novel treatments are associated with high expenses that would add to the financial burden of these heavily pre-treated patients. It would be of interest to explore this commonly encountered financial “toxicity” from patient’s perspectives through a useful PRO tool. Further, we speculate that implementing electronic PROs into patient management would improve compliance and facilitate rapid and personalized toxicity treatment interventions. There are many advancements in using the technology to monitor the PROs, such as smart applications on mobile devices as well as patient-friendly platforms that are linked to the medical records are now available. Also, voice recognition technology has been introduced to make the assessment of PRO much easier. All these challenges require a strong collaborative initiative from large study groups to harmonize research efforts, not only to better understand the role of PROs in CAR T-cell therapy but also to identify the best usable PRO tools in different patient populations and to incorporate PROs in the CAR T-cell therapy management guidelines.

Highlights:

  • Using PROs in patients on CAR T-cell therapy seems feasible and informative.

  • PROs need to be integrated into medical records and clinical guidelines.

  • Validated PRO tools still needed in patients on CAR T-cell therapy.

  • Disease status, CRS and neurotoxicity impact QoL in patients on CAR T-cell therapy.

  • Emotional and cognitive status post-CAR T-cell therapy need to be timely monitored.
