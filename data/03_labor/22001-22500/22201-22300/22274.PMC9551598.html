Abstract

BACKGROUND:

Although guidelines have been published on BIA-ALCL treatment, there has been no comprehensive analysis of BIA-ALCL treatment variation based on the available literature. The authors sought to assess current treatment strategies of breast implant associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) relative to current guidelines.

METHODS:

Database searches were conducted in June 2020. Included articles were case reports and case series with patient-level data. Collected variables included clinicopathological features, implant characteristics, diagnostic tests, ALCL characteristics, treatment, and details of follow-up and outcome. Treatment data before and after 2017 was compared with National Cancer Center Network guidelines.

RESULTS:

89 publications were included, and a total of 178 cases of BIA-ALCL were identified. Most patients presented with seroma (N=114, 70.4%), followed by a mass (N=14, 8.6%), or both (N=23, 14.2%). Treatment included en-bloc capsulectomy of the affected implant in 122 out of 126 cases with treatment details provided (96.8%). Radiation therapy was given in 38 cases (30.2%), and chemotherapy was given in 71 cases (56.3%). Practitioners used less chemotherapy for local disease after treatment guideline publication in 2017 (p<0.001), while treatment for advanced disease remained unchanged (p=0.3). There were 10 recurrences and 8 fatalities due to BIA-ALCL, which were associated with advanced presentation (29% vs. 2.1%, OR = 19.4 [3.9 – 96.3], p<0.001).

CONCLUSION:

BIA-ALCL remains a morbid but treatable condition. Current guidelines focused treatment for local disease and reduced non-surgical interventions with radiation or chemotherapy. Patients presenting with advanced BIA-ALCL experience higher rates of recurrence and mortality.

INTRODUCTION

Since the first reported case in 19971, breast implant associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) has been a difficult entity to characterize due to its low incidence and understated clinical course, but studies have begun to shed light on its pathogenesis, diagnosis, and management. It is now understood that BIA-ALCL is a rare form of non-Hodgkin lymphoma which has been associated with textured breast implants. Textured surfaces are thought to induce a chronic inflammatory state that may induce malignant transformation of T-cells in susceptible patients2. Most cases present as a local peri-prosthetic effusion, and local disease is virtually curable with early treatment3. BIA-ALCL is thought to be an indolent disease, but it is crucial to recognize it early, as deaths have been reported in very advanced cases3,4. While once thought to be very rare, recent studies have suggested the true incidence may be higher than early epidemiologic estimates5–7.

Historically, treatment of BIA-ALCL has been highly variable, utilizing both surgical and nonsurgical interventions4,8. Lack of consensus led to individualized treatment regimens based on expert opinions. In 2017, the National Cancer Center Network (NCCN) released landmark guidelines that provided an algorithmic approach to diagnosis and treatment of BIA-ALCL9. Based on NCCN guidelines, surgical intervention with implant removal and en-bloc capsulectomy ensures optimal outcomes in all BIA-ALCL patients, while systemic therapy is appropriate for more advanced cases.

National and international efforts to create BIA-ALCL registries have resulted in more robust database studies10, but many cases are missed by these registries and are instead published as case reports. Given the improved understanding of BIA-ALCL diagnosis and management, the authors sought to provide an updated systematic review of the case literature on BIA-ALCL to help characterize treatment variation in relation to NCCN guideline publication.

METHODS

Search Strategy

The present systematic review was registered on Prospero (ID: CRD42020197585) and conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for a Systematic Review and Meta- analysis (PRISMA) checklist11 and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) group guidelines12. Database searches were conducted in June 2020 to identify material written on breast implants and ALCL. In consultation with all authors, a medical librarian (FC) developed individual search strategies (See Figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which shows PRISMA Flow Diagram) and retrieved citations from PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Cochrane Library. A combination of the following text words and controlled vocabulary terms were used: “Breast Implants”, “Mammaplasty”, “Breast Reconstruction”, “Anaplastic Large-Cell Lymphoma”, “ALCL”, “Lymphoma”, and “Seroma”. In addition, a search of “grey literature” (the unpublished, noncommercial, hard-to-find information that organizations such as professional associations, research institutes, think tanks, and government departments produce) was conducted using Google Scholar. All search results were limited to material published since 1999. After the final exclusion, bibliographies of included articles were collected and screened. No authors of searched articles were contacted throughout the course of this study.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion criteria encompassed the diagnosis of ALCL in patients ≥ 18 years of age with at least one breast implant. Included articles were case reports and case series with patient-level data. Excluded articles were retrospective and prospective cohort studies, review articles, editorials, commentaries, and abstracts. Using Covidence systematic review management software, two authors (JM, HN) independently reviewed the abstract and, if necessary, the full text of all search results for inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies between authors were resolved through discussion with the senior author (JF).

Data Extraction, Outcomes of Interest, and Synthesis of Evidence

Predefined variables were independently extracted by study authors (MM, AC, JM, HN), and any discrepancies were reconciled by the study team. Collected variables included clinicopathological features (age at presentation, initial presenting symptom, indication for implant, breast cancer history, time from implant to presentation), implant characteristics (surface, type, manufacturer), diagnostic tests (radiologic and pathologic), ALCL characteristics (laterality, staging), treatment (implant removal, capsulectomy, axillary node excision, radiation, chemotherapy), and details of follow up and outcome (duration of follow-up, recurrence, fatality). Staging data and treatment data were compared with 2017 National Cancer Center Network (NCCN) Consensus Guidelines9,13 using a date of publication before or after 2017 to denote pre- or post-guideline publication. Staging was defined using tumor, lymph node, metastasis (TNM) classification when available in the included manuscripts. Local disease was defined as TNM IA-IC and advanced disease was defined as TNM II-IV based on NCCN guidelines. Cases without staging data were excluded from analysis. Data were pooled and represented as collective data points using descriptive statistics. For each variable of interest, the denominator utilized was defined as the number of cases which reported the variable, not the total number of cases. Fischer’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables, as appropriate. Univariate statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical calculations and analyses were performed using STATA version 12 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

Clinicopathologic, Implant, and Radiologic Characteristics

89 case reports/series and 178 patients were included (See Document, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which shows the list of case reports used for data collection). Descriptive statistics are found in Table 1. Median age at presentation was 53 years (Interquartile Range [IQR] 45–61). 114 patients (70.4%) presented with seroma, 14 patients presented with a mass (8.6%), 23 patients presented with both (14.2%), and 11 patients presented with various other symptoms or physical exam findings (6.8%), including capsular contracture, B-symptoms, and lymphadenopathy, among others. No data on initial presentation was available for 14 cases. 90 (56.6%) patients had implants placed for cosmetic augmentation while 69 patients (43.4%) underwent implant-based reconstruction, and 19 cases had no information regarding reason for implant placement. 52 (37.7%) patients had a reported breast cancer history prior to initial implant placement. Median time from implant to presentation was 9 years (IQR 4.75–13.75). 88 implants (69.8%) were silicone-filled while 38 were saline-filled (30.2%); 52 cases did not report type of implant. Implant surface was reported in 93 cases, all of which had textured surfaces. The most common implant manufacturers reported are summarized in Table 2. Most implants were manufactured by Allergan (Allergan plc, Dublin, Ireland) (N=51, 79.7%), followed by GC Aesthetics (GC Aesthetics, Dublin, Ireland) (N=8, 12.5%). The implant manufacturer was not mentioned in 114 cases.

The most common diagnostic radiographic tests performed were ultrasound (N=90, 71.1%) and Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography (PET CT) (N=81, 76.5%). Pathologic tests included cytology in 110 cases and immunohistochemistry in 125 cases. The majority of BIA-ALCL cases were unilateral (N=110, 96.4%), and very few were bilateral (N=4, 3.5%), with 64 cases which did not report BIA-ALCL laterality. 127 (100%) of reported cases were confirmed to be CD30+/ALK-, while others did not mention immunohistochemistry status. Staging was stratified by local disease (TNM IA-IC; N=50, 73.5%) or advanced disease (TNM II-IV; N=18, 26.5%), but most cases did not have staging data.

Treatment modalities and comparisons to standard of care

Treatment included implant removal and en-bloc capsulectomy in 122 out of 126 cases (96.8%). Removal of contralateral implant occurred in 69 out of 89 reported cases with bilateral implants (77.5%). Axillary node excision occurred in 13 cases (10.3%). Radiation therapy was administered in 38 cases (31.4%), and chemotherapy was given in 71 cases (53.8%). The most common chemotherapy regimens were CHOP [cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone] (N=44, 75.9%), R-CHOP [rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone] (N=4, 6.9%), EPOCH [etoposide, vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and prednisone] (N=4, 6.9%), and brentuximab veditoin (N=4, 6.9%). Other regimens included V-EPOCH [bortezomib, etoposide, vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and prednisone] (N=1, 1.7%), ABVD [adriamycin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine] (N=1, 1.7%), DHAOX [dexamethasone, cytarabine, oxaliplatin] (N=1, 1.7%), and hyper-CVAD [hyperfractionated cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, and prednisolone] (N=1, 1.7%). Other adjuvants were rarely reported but included ICE [ifosfamide, carboplatin, etoposide, mesna]in 4 cases and methotrexate in 1, among others. 7 patients received stem cell transplants.

Sub-analysis of treatment by BIA-ALCL stage revealed nuances in treatment strategies. For the 50 patients with reported local disease (TNM IA-IC), 35 patients (71.4%) were treated with implant removal and en-bloc capsulectomy only; 7 (14.3%) with implant removal, en-bloc capsulectomy, and chemotherapy; 3 (6.1%) with implant removal, en-bloc capsulectomy, and radiotherapy; and 5 (10.2%) with implant removal, en-bloc capsulectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. For the 18 patients with reported advanced disease (TNM II-IV), 3 (16.7%) were treated with implant removal and en-bloc capsulectomy only; 9 (50%) with implant removal, en- bloc capsulectomy, and chemotherapy; 0 with implant removal, en-bloc capsulectomy, and radiotherapy; 4 (22%) with implant removal, en-bloc capsulectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy; and 2 (11%) with other regimens, which were chemotherapy and radiotherapy without en-bloc capsulectomy (Figure 1).

Analysis of treatment modalities before and after 2017 (which correlates with NCCN guideline publication) revealed significant differences. For local disease, treatment used in cases prior to 2017 tended to be more aggressive; 17 cases (58.6%) were treated with implant removal and en-bloc capsulectomy only, while 12 cases (41.4%) were treated with implant removal, en- bloc capsulectomy, and chemotherapy. After 2017, 21 cases (100%) were treated with implant removal and en-bloc capsulectomy only, and no cases were treated with implant removal, en- bloc capsulectomy, and chemotherapy (Table 3). Prior to 2017, 3 cases of advanced disease were treated with implant removal and en-bloc capsulectomy only, and 12 cases were treated with implant removal, en-bloc capsulectomy, and chemotherapy prior to 2017. After 2017, all 15 cases of advanced disease were treated with implant removal, en-bloc capsulectomy, and chemotherapy (Table 4). Use of systemic chemotherapy for treatment of local BIA-ALCL was significantly associated with presentation prior to 2017 (p<0.001), while there was no association between guideline publication and treatment variation for advanced BIA-ALCL (p=0.31). In regard to adjuvant chemotherapy, 32 cases used CHOP or brentuximab veditoin prior to 2017, while 16 cases used them after 2017. Other chemotherapy regimens were used in 6 cases before 2017 and in 2 cases after 2017. There was no association between guideline publication and type of chemotherapy regimen used (p=0.72) (Table 5).

Clinical follow-up was reported in 128 cases with a mean follow-up duration of 22.5 +/− 20.9 months. Recurrence occurred in 10 cases (7.8%), and 8 patients (6.3%) died from complications of BIA-ALCL. Details of recurrence site were only reported in 4 cases, with 1 case of recurrence at the previous site of the breast capsule and 3 cases of distant recurrence outside of the capsule. Of the patients with recurrent disease, 2 patients initially presented with local disease (TNM IA-IC), 7 patients initially presented with a mass (TNM IIA), and 1 patient initially presented with advanced disease (TNM IIB-IV). Treatment details for each recurrent case, along with other details about recurrent cases are available in table 6. Adjusting for the one outlier of 144 months, mean time to recurrence after treatment was 14.6 months+/− 14.4 months. Recurrence/fatality was associated with mass at time of initial presentation (29% vs. 2.1%, OR = 19.4 [3.9 – 96.3], p<0.001).

DISCUSSION

The presented study sought to summarize patient-level data on BIA-ALCL, to provide an updated analysis of the association between presentation and recurrence/mortality, and to analyze temporal trends in treatment strategies. To our knowledge, this is the largest systematic review of patient-level data to date. Descriptive aspects of the case literature were found to be consistent with prior studies with regards to disease presentation, implant surface type, time to diagnosis and recurrence. However, the sub-analysis of temporal trends in BIA-ALCL treatment strategies was particularly revealing. After NCCN guideline publication in 2017, treatment patterns for local BIA-ALCL shifted significantly, favoring surgical therapy only (implant removal and capsulectomy) as opposed to both local therapy with systemic chemotherapy or locoregional radiation therapy. This signifies a decrease in the provision of unnecessary chemotherapy, decreasing the systemic side-effects and overall morbidity of treatment for patients with local disease. Conversely, treatment for advanced disease was not significantly associated with any change over time. This result is likely due to the fact that practitioners were already treating advanced BIA-ALCL aggressively with local therapy and chemotherapy prior to guideline publication. Altogether, our results represent a shift in BIA-ALCL treatment for local disease.

With hundreds of thousands of breast implants placed every year14, BIA-ALCL continues to warrant intense investigation and scrutiny as its overall rarity has caused debate about how practitioners should alter their breast implant-based practices. Many studies have attempted to estimate risk by reporting the incidence of BIA-ALCL7,15–17, but an accurate estimate has been difficult to attain due to inconsistent global reporting of cases and inaccurate estimates of the total number of patients with breast implants. A study by Magnusson et al. revealed dramatically different incidences of BIA-ALCL stratified by textured surface type18. Additionally, they found that the incidence of BIA-ALCL in their population was increasing, which they attributed to improved awareness and detection. Nonetheless, textured implants continue to be used in various contexts worldwide. A 2015 report suggested that plastic surgeons have been incorporating more textured implants into their practices due to their advantages over smooth implants, including better stability within the implant pocket, lower rates of capsular contracture, and associated improved cosmetic outcomes19. However, since 2015 more recent articles have argued for the elimination of textured implants from practice20,21, and so, many surgeons are asking themselves whether textured implant reconstruction is worth the risk of BIA-ALCL. In 2019, the Food and Drug Administration released a series of initiatives to garner better evidence about BIA-ALCL, but importantly, they did not ban macrotextured implants, citing insufficient evidence22.

One trend becomes obvious upon appraising the literature – early diagnosis and treatment is essential to optimize outcomes in BIA-ALCL23. A study in 2016 by Clemens et al. asserted the importance of complete surgical excision to ensure event-free survival in BIA-ALCL patients. In comparison to partial capsulectomy, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy, complete surgical excision with implant removal was significantly associated with improved survival24. They also found that extracapsular involvement was associated with worse survival. In this study, we found that presentation with a mass as opposed to a seroma was significantly associated with recurrence or mortality, with an odds ratio of 19.4 [3.9 – 96.3]. We also found that recurrences/fatalities were often appropriately treated with local and systemic therapies. This further adds to the evidence that advanced disease is associated with significantly worse outcomes, reemphasizing the importance of early detection. Nonetheless, surgical excision remains an effective treatment option regardless of cancer stage, as a recent retrospective study showed25. More recently, a 2020 prospective study by Tevis et al. found that 52 patients treated for BIA-ALCL achieved complete remission. This study is significant as it as it is one of the only prospective studies that evaluates BIA-ALCL outcomes, and it indicates that timely comprehensive care can effectively cure BIA- ALCL26.

Early implant removal and en-bloc capsulectomy have essentially become standard of care for BIA-ALCL due to the aforementioned studies, but other treatments, such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, have yet to be intensively studied. The NCCN guidelines suggest adjuvant chemotherapy with either CHOP or brentuximab veditoin for advanced disease. CHOP is an anthracycline-based systemic therapy traditionally used for peripheral T-cell lymphomas27. Brentuximab-veditoin is an antibody-drug conjugate that binds to CD30, a marker that is highly expressed on malignant T-cells in ALCL28. These agents have yet to be thoroughly studied in BIA-ALCL, so their efficacy is based largely on case reports29–31. In this study, we find that even before NCCN guideline publication, the majority of practitioners had been using CHOP to treat BIA-ALCL. While practitioners are using less chemotherapy overall in the treatment of local BIA-ALCL, their chemotherapy of choice has not changed over time.

Despite the strength of the aforementioned studies, there are no rigorous studies or trials to date that have comprehensively assessed BIA-ALCL treatment options. Most of the available literature that has guided BIA-ALCL treatment is based on either case reports or retrospective cohort studies. As such, prospective studies and reviews of the literature are critically needed to provide evidence for the most effective management of BIA-ALCL.

We consider our reliance on case reports to be a strength of our study, as the case literature catches cases not recorded in patient registries. However, the use of case reports leads to information gaps and, thus, inconsistencies in the data collected. As such, this is also the largest weakness of our study. There were limited data on multiple parameters, including breast cancer history, BIA-ALCL staging, implant characteristics, recurrence, and fatality, among others. This limits the generalizability of our findings. Using case reports as opposed to cohort studies for data collection also underestimates the overall case numbers of BIA-ALCL. Larger studies that incorporate cohort studies may allow for more comprehensive analyses but would necessarily lack the granularity of specific patient data. In addition, using case reports likely overestimates the incidence of advanced disease and recurrence/fatality, as they are more likely to be reported than mild disease. Although we analyze BIA-ALCL management in relation to NCCN guideline publication, practitioner management patterns had likely already started shifting due to mounting evidence about BIA-ALCL management. NCCN guideline publication served as a matter of consensus that may have further shifted management patterns, although causality has yet to be established. In addition, we used 2017 as a timepoint for analysis; we acknowledge that there can be a lag time from when guidelines are published to when they are put into practice and then presented in downstream publications. Of note, we did not see this, suggesting that a change had already started to occur in management strategies prior to the guideline presentation.

While our results are encouraging, more studies that investigate the effects of treatment variation on long-term outcomes are needed. We were not able to provide an analysis of the association of treatment type with outcome due to low case numbers. Studies that prospectively follow BIA-ALCL, such as the aforementioned study by Tevis et al.26, will more comprehensively define the implications of treatment variation in patient care. Furthermore, vigilant participation in PROFILE, the BIA-ALCL registry that is maintained by the American Society of Plastic Surgeons and the Plastic Surgery Foundation, is of critical importance to plastic surgeons as it provides a robust dataset to further BIA-ALCL investigations and ensure optimal outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

BIA-ALCL continues to be primarily associated with textured implants and most often presents with local disease. We demonstrate that treatment for local disease was significantly less aggressive after treatment guideline publication in 2017. Patients who presented with advanced disease had higher rates of recurrence and mortality.
