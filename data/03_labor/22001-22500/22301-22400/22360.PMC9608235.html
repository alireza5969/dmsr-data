Contributions of Zebrafish Studies to the Behavioural Consequences of Early Alcohol Exposure: A Systematic Review

Flavia Gheller Schaidhauer[1][2]

Abstract

Background: The consequences of mild to severe exposure to alcohol during brain development is still a matter of debate and scientific investigation. The long-term behavioural effects of ethanol exposure have been related to impaired social skills and cognition. Zebrafish have become a suitable animal model to investigate the effects of early ethanol exposure because it is very feasible to promote drug delivery during early development.

Objective: The goal of the current report is to review existing behavioural studies addressing the impact of early alcohol exposure using zebrafish to determine whether these models resemble the behavioural effects of early alcohol exposure in humans.

Methods: A comprehensive search of biomedical databases was performed using the operation order: “ZEBRAFISH AND BEHAV* AND (ETHANOL OR ALCOHOL)”. The eligibility of studies was determined using the PICOS strategy, contemplating the population as zebrafish, intervention as exposure to ethanol, comparison with a non-exposed control animal, and outcomes as behavioural parameters.

Results: The systematic search revealed 29 scientific articles as eligible. The zebrafish is presented as a versatile animal model that is useful to study FASD short and long-term behaviour impairments, such as anxiety, impaired sociability, aggressiveness, learning problems, memory impairment, seizure susceptibility, sleep disorders, motivational problems, and addiction.

Conclusion: This systematic review further promotes the use of zebrafish as a model system to study the pathophysiological and behavioural consequences of early alcohol exposure (PROSPERO CRD42020215072).

INTRODUCTION

1

The adverse effects of alcohol consumption during pregnancy affect the early development of the foetus. The spectrum of structural abnormalities and behavioural and neurocognitive deficiencies is called foetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD), whose most severe condition is Foetal Alcohol Syndrome (FAS) [1, 2]. The global prevalence of FASD among children and young people in the general population is estimated at 7.7 per 1000 inhabitants, with the highest rate (111.1 cases per 1000 inhabitants) in South Africa and the lowest rate in Middle Eastern countries (0.1 cases per 1000 inhabitants) [3, 4]. Important factors that may influence the impact of prenatal alcohol exposure include the dose and the period of development in which exposure occurs. The amount of alcohol to which the foetus is exposed influences the extent of the damage and the severity of the impact on normal development [5, 6]. The multitude of exposure factors aligned with different individual susceptibility account for the differences in sequelae seen in humans exposed during early development.

Many individuals with a history of prenatal alcohol exposure have no morphological changes and, consequently, may not be diagnosed or diagnosed incorrectly, despite cognitive and behavioral impairment [7]. It has been reported that in utero exposure to alcohol has a negative impact on a multitude of cognitive domains (Fig. 1), including general intelligence [8], motor function [9], sleep [10], level of attention and activity [11], language development [12], executive function [13], perception and visual construction, learning and memory [14], and adaptive functioning [15]. The adaptive functioning impairment induced by early alcohol exposure has been linked to inappropriate sexual behaviour [16], little fear [17], high impulsivity [18], problems with the law [16], self-destructive behaviour, and addiction to drugs [19]. In addition, prenatal alcohol exposure leads to a high rate of comorbidities with respect to other learning and behavioural disorders, as well as seizures or epilepsy [18, 20, 21].

An important contributor to the difficulty in diagnosis and, therefore, in the treatment is insufficient information regarding the pathogenesis and the link between early cellular ethanol targets and behavioural outcomes. Animal models have played a critical role in FASD research, including studies that confirm that alcohol is a teratogenic substance and the mechanisms by which alcohol exerts its teratogenic effect from cellular dysfunction to functional and behavioural deficits [22]. Mammals, including mice, rats, and primates, were the most common specimens used for scientific study until the early 2000s, when ethical issues and specific factors, such as difficulty in handling and greater cost, did not fully embrace the principles of 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction and Refinement) [23]. In this context, zebrafish (Danio rerio) has emerged as an excellent alternative for FASD research [24, 25]. The general attributes that make zebrafish a good model for FASD are easy access to all stages of development and the optical clarity of embryos and larvae that allows real-time visualisation of development [26]. While no animal model is without its limitations as a model of human diseases, a good animal model should have the face/apparent, construct, and predictive validities. This is a challenge when behavioural issues are a core component, as in the mild and slight forms of FASD [27]. The vast behavioural repertoire of zebrafish [28] allows behavioural assessments regarding memory, social behaviour, aggression, and motor skills, among other behavioural presentations affected by FASD [20]. Larval and adult zebrafish are widely used for the evaluation of long-term behavioural effects caused by embryonic ethanol exposure [29], with a variety of behavioural parameters and models of exposures to assess FASD impacts on behaviour. In this way, this systematic review provides a comprehensive overview of the current contribution of zebrafish as a model system to study the behavioural consequences of early exposure to ethanol, as well as its limitations. Here, the existing behavioural studies with zebrafish model for early ethanol exposure were emphasized, addressing the main protocols, parameters, and results reached thus far to contribute to a trustworthy picture of this animal model to study the long-term behavioural effects of early alcohol exposure.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

2

The study concept for this systematic review was first registered and approved in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the protocol number CRD42020215072, and the review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) statement.

Information Sources

2.1

Between August 19th and 20th, 2020, a search was conducted in the databases PubMed/MEDLINE (https://PubMed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), SciELO (https://SciELO.org), Scopus (https://Scopus.com), Web of Science (https://apps.webof-knowledge.com), and EMBASE (https://embase.com). The DeCS/ MeSH terms were BEHAV* (BEHAVIO$R, BEHAVIOR, BEHAVIOUR, BEHAVIOURAL), ETHANOL or ALCOHOL and ZEBRAFISH. The order of operation was ZEBRAFISH AND BEHAV* AND (ETHANOL OR ALCOHOL). On December 28th, 2020, the search was repeated. The search strategy in PubMed/MEDLINE can be accessed at https://PubMed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=%28%28BEHAVIOUR+OR+BEHAVIOR%29+AND+%28ETHANOL+OR+ALCOHOL%29+AND+ZEBRAFISH%29+.

Study Selection and PICO Strategy

2.2

After gathering the retrievals from all databases, the study selection was performed following the sequence (1) exclusion of repetitions; (2) analysis of titles; (3) analysis of abstracts; and (4) analysis of full-length manuscripts (Fig. 2). These steps were performed by three independent reviewers (FGS, HAC, and GPS), followed by a final revision by the senior researcher (RSS).

The PICOS strategy involved population: wild type zebrafish; intervention: in vivo exposure to ethanol during early development; comparison: no alcohol or any other substance added to embryos or media, same manipulation as the experimental group; outcomes: behavioural parameters; and study designs: primary, randomised studies. Considering this strategy, the eligible articles had at least one group as a control/naïve, one group exposed only to ethanol, and at least one type of behaviour evaluated after a period of ethanol washout that could serve as a model of FASD. Articles were considered ineligible if they were not a full research paper, not a study with zebrafish, had no exposure to ethanol during the embryonic/early period, or contained no behavioural analyses. Only primary studies written in English were considered.

Data Extraction and Risk of Bias Check

2.3

The data extraction of eligible studies was performed by two independent reviewers (FGS and RSS). The extracted data included the specific animal strain and sex, period of ethanol exposure, ethanol concentration, duration of ethanol exposure, period of behavioural evaluation, type of behavioural evaluation, and parameters assessed. Additionally, the type of control, number of replicates, country of origin, year of publication, and main results were also documented. To evaluate the risk of bias of individual studies, the SYRCLE`s tool was applied [30]. All data were gathered and presented through a narrative description accompanied by the presentation of data through graphics of the percentage/frequency of data. To determine the percentage of ethanol concentration and developmental stages, the total number of experiments presented from all studies was considered as 100%. For ethanol concentration, 56 different experiments were extracted from the 29 studies, while for the developmental stage, each hour of development was counted by each experiment performed in each selected study. Zebrafish embryos usually hatch from the eggs at 48-72 hours post-fertilisation (hpf). The larval stage extends from hatching to 10 days post-fertilisation (dpf), the juvenile stage extends to 3 months and the adult stage extends beyond 3 months. If the main data was lacking, the authors of the selected studies were contacted by e-mail.

RESULTS

3

The electronic search from scientific databases retrieved 1181 scientific articles. After application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 29 scientific manuscripts were eligible (Fig. 2). All eligible studies (Table 1) were primary experimental studies with control animals, and at least one group of animals was exposed solely to ethanol during early development. The risk of bias varied between 1 to 10 in the SYRCLE`s tool of analysis (Supplementary material 1). The AB (59%) strain was the most frequently used as an FASD model, followed by TU and TU background (17%), wild-type not assigned (14%), and others (10%).

The time at which zebrafish were exposed to alcohol in the eligible studies ranged from 2 hours post-fertilization (hpf) to 9 dpf; 43% of the zebrafish were exposed to alcohol between 2-24 hpf, 22% between 24-48 hpf and 35% were exposed to alcohol after 48 hpf (Fig. 3A and Table 1). These periods for ethanol exposure include the early Cleavage stage (0.75 to 2.25 hpf), which corresponds to the first few specialized divisions, until the Segmentation stage (10.33 to 24 hpf) described as the regionalization process that divides the organism into a series of segments along the longitudinal axis; the Pharyngula stage (24 to 48 hpf), when the body axis begins to straighten, and the head straightens out and lifts dorsally, and when the notochord is developed and the nervous system is hollow and expanding anteriorly; and the Hatching (48 to 72 hpf) to larvae (> 72hpf) period, when the primary organs are developed [31].

A 1% (alcohol/water – volume/volume) ethanol concentration was the most frequent choice for ethanol exposure since 57% of the manuscripts used this concentration, especially when only one dose was used to induce FASD models in zebrafish. As several studies analysed concentration dependence (curve of doses), when we consider all experiments to model FASD in the 29 manuscripts, 60% applied concentrations below 1%, 29% applied 1%, and 11% applied concentrations higher than 1% (Fig. 3B).

Behavioural evaluations occurred in the short- and long-term. In summary, 31% of the experiments from selected studies performed behavioural evaluations at the larval stage (< 10 dpf), 20% during the juvenile stage (< 3 months), 43% after the sexually mature stage (> 3 months), and 6% in fish greater than 1-year-old (Table 1). From the selected studies, only two [32, 33] did not provide the specific age of animals. Since there was a statement in these studies presenting the animals as “adults”, for the purpose of the current review, we added this data as the adult group (over 3 months).

Behavioural Outcomes Assessed in Zebrafish Model of FASD

3.1

Table 1 summarizes the age at which zebrafish were exposed to ethanol, the concentration of ethanol that was used and the age at which the effects of this exposure on major behavioural variables was documented. Table 2 and Fig. (4) show each approach used to model common human conditions related to FASD with details about the apparatus used and parameters assessed during larval, juvenile, and adult stages of zebrafish development.

Main Behavioural Outcomes in the Larval Stage

3.1.1

Locomotor Activity, Anxiety and Sleep Behaviour

3.1.1.1

The larval stage is often used to evaluate the motor activity and anxious behaviour. The open field, which allows free swimming, was used in most studies [34-37], with an apparatus as simple as Petri dishes [34, 36] and 96-[35] or 6-well plates [37]. Motor activity was also assessed under light challenge, based on the fact that zebrafish larvae increase locomotor activity under light, allowing fast changes in motor activity in a light/dark challenge test [38], which can be performed in specific automatised chambers using 6 to 96-well plates to allocate several larvae simultaneously [39-42]. Decreased locomotion [36, 40, 41, 43] was the main outcome detected at the larval stage, but some studies did not find any effect on motor activity or even an increase in motor activity after early ethanol exposure [34, 37, 44]. This variability was attributed to the developmental time point and regimen of ethanol exposure, as well as genotype differences and visual impairment [34, 36, 42].

Anxious behaviour in zebrafish has been related to thigmotaxis, the preference exhibited by fish to be close to the walls of the apparatus. This behaviour has been examined in Petri dishes or well plates of various sizes [34, 36, 40, 43-45]. The most frequently observed parameter was time spent near the wall after a specific free-swimming period [34, 40, 43-45] or after a stimulus, such as virtual images [36]. The adaptation of the novel tank test used for adult zebrafish was also applied for larvae assessment using mini-tanks [44] or Petri dishes [43, 44], with movement and immobility time as the main parameter considered [43]. The effect of early ethanol exposure also induced variable effects in anxiety models using zebrafish larvae. Cadena et al. [40] and Baiamonte et al. [45] observed reduced thigmotaxis and decreased anxiety-related behaviour in larvae with different regimens of early ethanol exposure, while Sterling et al. [44] did not detect changes in such behaviour. Pinheiro-da-Silva et al. [43] related the increased immobility time to anxiety-related behaviour. Ramlan et al. [36] showed decreased thigmotaxis when animals were exposed to withdrawal ethanol exposure, while increased thigmotaxis was observed when animals were exposed to continuous ethanol treatment.

Sleep behaviour testing, also assessed at larval stage, uses inactivity as a parameter, and early ethanol exposure increased activity at night and the number of arousal periods, which authors related to sleep disorders [40].

Learning

3.1.1.2

Learning abilities were investigated in zebrafish larvae using avoidance tasks based on the movement of virtual images [37] or black and white compartments in a Petri dish [43]. Fernandes et al. [37] and Pinheiro-da-Silva et al. [43] did not observe any effects of early ethanol exposure on learning using aversive-based models.

Sociability

3.1.1.3

While social preference has not been considered at the larval stage, Fernandes et al. [37] evaluated, using FASD model, the average distance from a given larval fish (5 dpf) to all others and the nearest neighbour distance as a social behaviour indicator. However, this study did not identify impaired social skills as an effect of early ethanol exposure [37].

Seizures

3.1.1.4

Although seizures are not a cognitive dysfunction, the seizure incidence is higher in children with FASD [21]. Similarly, Wang et al. [35] observed increased sensitivity to chemical-induced seizures through individual exposure of zebrafish larvae to Pentylenetetrazol after embryonic ethanol exposure.

Main Behavioural Outcomes in Juvenile Stage

3.1.2

Locomotor Activity and Anxiety

3.1.2.1

As in larvae, juvenile zebrafish were mainly used to study motor function and anxiety. The novel tank approach [46-48], especially using a trapezoid-shaped tank, and thigmotaxis in 6-well plates [45] were used to identify anxiety. Sensorimotor, vigour, and energy were evaluated by Bailey et al. [48] and Weeks et al. [49] using tap startle or laminar flow as stimuli.

Early ethanol exposure had a variable effect on the dive response, a measure of anxiety, between studies using juvenile zebrafish. Burton et al. [46] identified reduced and more transient dive responses using mild to high ethanol concentrations (1 to 3%), which was also associated with risk-taking behaviour, while Boa-Amposem [47] did not identify any effects on this parameter using 0.5% ethanol. Using the thigmotaxis evaluation in Petri dishes, Baiamonte et al. [45] observed decreased time spent in the periphery, an indicator of an anxiolytic effect, in 23 day-old larva exposed to ethanol during early development.

Hyperactivity was observed in most studies using juvenile zebrafish to assess early ethanol exposure effects [39, 44, 48]. However, Weeks et al. [49] identified a reduction in active motor level with early ethanol exposure, which was more related to reduced vigour and energy using a different methodological approach, a swimming response to laminar water flow.

Learning and Sociability

3.1.2.2

Impaired learning and sociability can be assessed in juvenile zebrafish in a more complex way, using spatial discriminative [48], shoaling [50], and schooling [51] tests. Shoaling and schooling are group behaviours, the shoal refers to any group of fish that remain together for “social reasons”, and not by the pressure of external stimulus such as a predator [48], whereas schools are shoals of fish that are polarized and highly coordinated, which confers anti-predatory advantage [49]. Different approaches of early ethanol exposure were not able to affect spatial discrimination [48], interindividual response [51], or social behaviour [50] when zebrafish were evaluated at the juvenile stage.

Main Behavioural Outcomes in Adult Stage

3.1.3

The adult stage offered a more diverse toolbox to evaluate behaviours affected by ethanol, which included sociability, motor function, fear, anxiety, vigour, stress reactivity, associative learning, working memory, motivation, and aggression. Only two studies from the selected ones were performed using 1 year-old or older animals to study locomotor, anxiety, aggressiveness, and social behaviour [52, 53].

Locomotor Activity and Anxiety

3.1.3.1

Anxiety behaviour was assessed in open [52] and novel tanks [54], where outcomes of time spent in the bottom zone, thigmotaxis, and scototaxis (the preference for a dark area of an apparatus) could be explored and related to stress reactivity [45, 54]. The use of a natural predator image revealed anxiety- and fear-related behaviour [32, 55]. Early exposure to ethanol promoted anxiolytic behaviour in adult zebrafish (3 month-old animals) as revealed by decreased time in the bottom zone or periphery of the aquarium and increased time on the bright side of the tank, an effect that did not persist in older fish (12 month-old) [45, 52]. Additionally, adults subjected to the predator image test were not different from control animals [32, 55], except by overall swimming activity [32].

Sociability and Aggressiveness

3.1.3.2

The strong sociability of adult zebrafish was explored by the individual behaviour of fish facing a living [52, 56, 57] or a virtual [53, 55, 58] conspecific fish group. Additionally, the cohesion of group fish was assessed by the analysis of cluster scores [54]. All approaches of sociability in adults revealed deficits in this cognitive domain as a long-lasting effect of early ethanol exposure [52-58], including effects as persistent as one or two years after exposure [52, 53].

Aggressive behaviour was assessed based by the natural territoriality of zebrafish [28]. In FASD models using adult zebrafish to detect long-lasting effects related to aggressive behaviour, the mirror test was applied [44, 52]. That test considers that when fish approach the mirror, its specular image is not recognised as itself but as an opponent, which is reinforced by an aggressive posture, such as erect dorsal, caudal, pectoral, or anal fins [28]. Aggressive behaviour was persistently increased as an effect of early continuous 1% ethanol exposure, specifically at the pharyngula stage [52], while acute and mild ethanol exposure had no effect on aggressive behaviour [44].

Learning and Memory

3.1.3.3

Learning behaviour and memory were assessed by several adapted protocols designed for rodents, such as the Y-maze and T-maze, using visual clues [33] and positive [33, 54, 59, 60] and negative [59, 61] conditioning. Moreover, the analysis of this associative behaviour was linked to motivational aspects [33, 59, 60] that are strongly affected in FASD. Cleal and Parker [59] showed that early moderated ethanol exposure at 48 hpf was able to affect the spatial working memory, without effects on appetitive and aversive learning, while Lutte et al. [61] showed that aversive learning was affected by ethanol exposure during gastrula/segmentation and pharyngula stages of development [59, 61]. Impaired learning in adult zebrafish treated with ethanol early was detected using visual clues and food rewarding tests under a continuous ethanol regimen [33], while no effect was observed using a similar approach with a more restricted ethanol regimen [60]. This effect was not related to appetite, food amount consumption [59, 60], or locomotor effects [60]. Early ethanol exposure promoted enhanced habituation in adult zebrafish tested in the Conditioning Preference Place (CPP), which was related to the susceptibility to addiction to alcohol [33].

DISCUSSION

4

This is the first systematic review dedicated to evaluating the contribution of experimental work using zebrafish as an animal model for behavioural effects in FASD studies. The FASD model using zebrafish is a well-recognised platform to assess morphological and behavioural impacts of these disorders, contributing also to molecular, cellular, and physiological data [62, 63], which was recently very well reviewed by Bailey et al. [64].

The concentration and timing of alcohol exposure was a determinant of the outcomes observed. To preserve swimming activity, the basis of general behaviour in zebrafish, the studies intended to evaluate behavioural effects on FASD models using mild or low [1% (v/v) or less] ethanol exposure in most zebrafish strains (Fig. 3 and Table 1). These doses did not show gross morphological outcomes but rather neurochemical impacts providing a neurobiological basis for behavioural effects. Mild or low doses promote a robust reduction of dopamine, DOPAC, and serotonin levels in the AB strain [65] and an increased number of apoptotic cells and decreased glutamate transporters in wild-type shortfin phenotype embryos treated with an ethanol concentration around 1% [36, 66]. While a 1% ethanol concentration (171 mM) could be considered high in comparison to human levels, which often reach 66 mM [67], using zebrafish eggs, the barrier imposed by the chorion must be considered, since the ethanol concentration inside the egg is 2.7–6.2-fold lower than ethanol media levels [68-70]. A balance between dosing and timing of ethanol exposure must be made to avoid severe morphological outcomes in zebrafish to promote specific behavioural evaluations. In this way, 65% of the selected studies were performed up to pharyngula stage completion (Fig. 3). While it is difficult to do an overall parallel between zebrafish and human development, there has been an effort to relate to the second and third trimesters of human development, as this period includes an intense period of cell migration and differentiation to the beginning of brain growth, pruning, and refinement [71, 72], which occurs in zebrafish up to 120 hpf [73].

Motor activity is an easy and informative task that can be evaluated in all developmental stages of zebrafish. This type of analysis can be used to discharge the impacts of subtle anatomical defects but can also be a way to evaluate hyperactivity, a common outcome in FASD. In fact, early ethanol exposure increases the incidence of deficit attention and hyperactivity disorder 10-fold [67]. From the selected studies, no alteration in basal motor activity was seen in the adult stage. The larval period of evaluation presented more variable motor activity effects, while the juvenile stage was presented as a more robust stage to identify hyperactivity as a response to early ethanol exposure [39, 44, 48].

One of the most sensitive cognitive aspects that is strongly impaired in zebrafish exposed early to ethanol is sociability. The social preference of zebrafish is a feature developed in juveniles and young adults, which is limiting in some aspects; however, all studies using adult zebrafish evaluated in this review presented persistent impaired social skills and less fear from early ethanol exposures as low as 0.25-0.5% and as specific as 2 hours of exposure (Table 1). Human FASD is associated in adolescence and adulthood with impulsivity [18], little fear [17], problems with the law [16], increased risk taking, and maladaptive behaviour [15]. Therefore, the use of social parameters of zebrafish, especially during the adult period, is of great importance regarding the strength of this outcome since social cohesion and sociability are core behaviours of zebrafish biology.

The sociability can be also affected by adaptative functioning and be related to reduced anxiety and increased risk taking. Anxiety-related behaviours are the most frequently assessed aspect in all developmental stages of zebrafish. Early ethanol exposure promoted an anxiolytic effect in all developmental stages especially when 1% or higher ethanol concentrations [40, 46, 52] were used or during prolonged ethanol exposure with low doses [45]. This decrease in anxiety-related behaviour can be related to risk taking and stress reactivity. However, anxiogenic effects were also detected in larvae [43] and juveniles [47] treated early with ethanol. These effects were observed using low ethanol concentrations (less than 1%) with acute periods of exposure [43, 47]. In humans, anxiety disorders are a mental illness associating FASD with high comorbidity to depression, which is the main predictor of suicide [74]. Aggressiveness is also a component of maladaptive behaviour and an increase was observed in adult zebrafish exposed to ethanol during the pharyngula stage [52].

Poor academic performance and engagement and difficulty in daily living skills are common outcomes of FASD in adolescents and adults [16, 75, 76] with an increase of incidence 23 times higher than healthy people [67]. Learning, memory, and motivational disabilities can be related to protocols using zebrafish performance in associative conditioning approaches even during larval and juvenile stages, which could be of great importance in the impairment faced by children with FASD during the schooling period. However, studies selected during this systematic review were unable to identify learning impairment during larval and juvenile periods, while the adult period evaluation revealed impaired associative learning using visual clues and food rewarding and impaired memory retention in an aversive protocol [33, 61] with variable ethanol concentrations and prolonged periods of exposure. The adult stage was used to evaluate addiction behaviour, another key problem in the long-lasting effects of early ethanol exposure in humans [16].

Notably, in this systematic review, the substantial heterogeneity of protocols and approaches prevented a metanalysis but yielded a rich set of strong reliable protocols to provide guidance regarding the use of zebrafish as a model of long-term behavioural effects of early ethanol exposure. There is a need to increase guidelines, such as ARRIVE, as a way to guarantee transparency, reliability, and comparability in animal-based studies.

CONCLUSION

Over 1700 new cases of FASD are identified globally per day, revealing a burden to public health, global economy, and individual and familial suffering [3, 77]. While theoretically, there is no tolerance for alcohol consumption during pregnancy, in the real life, this is almost impossible, since most women are unaware of being pregnant at the beginning of pregnancy. To face the inherent problems of early alcohol exposure, animal models, such as zebrafish, are indispensable. Here, we systematically reviewed the current literature to determine the main contributions of zebrafish to understand and provide a platform of common behavioural effects of FASD. We identified that adult zebrafish offer a varied and reliable way of studying behaviour related to sociability and learning, which are cognitive domains strongly affected by early exposure to ethanol. Studies using larvae and juvenile zebrafish are more restrictive in methodological approaches and variable in their outcomes, although juvenile zebrafish studies robustly reproduce hyperactivity in FASD models. Methodological approaches to explore the cognitive domains affected by ethanol need to be used as soon as possible, since early diagnosis in humans offers a better chance of management. For this reason, the efforts of researchers to develop methodological approaches to explore sociability and innate and early learning in juvenile and young adult zebrafish, in line with studies based on neurobiology aspects, are of great importance for future research aimed at improving zebrafish as FASD models.

Main protocols to evaluate behaviour in FASD model using zebrafish. (a). Thigmotaxis protocol: evaluation is based on the preference to be near the walls through a delimited area in Petri dishes; (b). Light/dark protocol: evaluation is based on the changes in motor activity during the switch of light/dark periods; (c). Trapezoid-novel tank protocol: evaluation is based on the preference to explore first the bottom of an aquarium in a novel environment; (d). Predator protocol: evaluation is based on the behaviour facing a natural predator; (e). Mirror protocol: evaluation is based on the behaviour of a non-recognized specular image of the fish as itself; (f). Conspecific group image protocol: evaluation is based on the preference to be near a conspecific group; (g). Group cohesion protocol: evaluation is based on the preference to be in group formation; (h). Living shoal protocol: evaluation is based on the preference to be near a conspecific group; (i). Y-maze protocol: evaluation is based on the exploratory behaviour following spatial clues; (j). T-maze protocol: evaluation is based on the exploratory behaviour with a positive stimulus (e.g., food or conspecific fish); and (k). Inhibitory-avoidance protocol: evaluation is based on the preference for a dark environment confronted by a negative stimulus (e.g., mild electric shock).
