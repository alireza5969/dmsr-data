A Systematic Review of Campus Characteristics Associated with Sexual Violence and Other Forms of Victimization

Abstract

Violence researchers have highlighted a need to understand connections between campus characteristics and violent victimization among students. Responding to those calls, we systematically reviewed research examining the characteristics of secondary and post-secondary educational settings associated with sexual violence and related victimization experiences, including dating/intimate partner violence, stalking, bullying, hate crimes, and crime more broadly. We screened 1,124 quantitative and qualitative records, 43 of which met the inclusion criteria for this systematic review. Evidence emerged for institution demographics, institution type, institution climate, institution financial characteristics, and educational characteristics being related to various forms of victimization; institution setting (urban vs. rural) was not associated with victimization. Additionally, evidence was observed for institution location and size/density. Some factors, including institution type and campus demographics, operated differently for different forms of victimization. We highlight limitations of existing data, including variability in the measurement of victimization outcomes, lack of power to detect differences at the campus level, and challenges of creating a database on victimization that contains campus identifiers. We also reinforce calls for more intersectional research, both in terms of the types of victimization experienced by students as well as in the disproportionate impact victimization may have on students with marginalized identities.

Introduction

Violence in educational institutions is a significant public health concern. For example, a US Department of Education (ED) publication recently noted that reports of sexual violence at K-12 public schools rose from about 9,600 in the 2015–2016 school year to nearly 15,000 in the 2017–2018 school year (2020). The problem also extends to higher education, with climate survey estimates showing that 21% of women and 7% of men report experiencing sexual violence while in college (Krebs et al., 2016). Physical and intimate partner violence (IPV) as well as stalking, discrimination, and bullying also are concerns in secondary and post-secondary institutions. For example, meta-analyses suggest that 35% of youth age 12–18 have perpetrated or experienced traditional bullying (e.g., name-calling, physical violence, etc) while 15% have experienced cyberbullying (e.g., technology-facilitated harassment; Modecki et al., 2014). Although certain forms of victimization, such as bullying at the secondary level or sexual violence at the post-secondary level, have received substantial attention, few, if any, studies have examined campus factors associated with multiple victimizations across academic contexts.

Recent work, mainly focused on post-secondary institutions in US contexts, has highlighted the importance of attending to ecological risk factors for sexual violence victimization in academic institutions (Moylan & Javorka, 2020). This is a notable departure from the preponderance of literature addressing individual and interpersonal factors that are associated with risk for victimization such as alcohol use in the case of sexual assault victimization (e.g., Abbey, 2011). The socioecological model is a variation of Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model that posits that behavior is determined by factors including: 1) the individual’s knowledge, attitudes and beliefs, 2) their interpersonal interactions and social support systems, 3) institutional and organizational characteristics, 4) community factors including relationships among institutions and cultures within defined boundaries, and 5) public policy, which focuses on the influence of local and national laws and policies (McLeroy, 1988). In a recent review of research on campus sexual assault, Moylan and colleagues (2020) took an important step towards explicating ecological factors by examining institutional-level variation in sexual violence victimization. They highlighted the importance of campus services and resources for survivors, alcohol and party culture on campus, student activities such as athletics and fraternities, and the impact of state and federal policies. However, they also called for additional research focusing on ecological factors associated with sexual assault victimization, which is consistent with other work highlighting a pressing need to examine how the “upper” levels of the socioecological model affect victimization rates (DeGue et al., 2014). This research could inform more effective multi-tiered prevention programming that addresses all levels of the socioecological model to be effective and comprehensive (Dills et al., 2016).

The current review builds on Moylan and colleagues’ (2020) review in three critical ways. First, this is a systematic review, designed to identify and include relevant studies (see methods), including those from non-US contexts. Second, the review encompasses research on victimization in secondary schools in addition to post-secondary institutions. The expansion to secondary schools reflects both considerable public attention to bullying as a social problem in K-12 settings, as well as World Health Organization surveillance data showing that college is hardly the only or even the first educational institution in which young people experience violence. Third, this review examines institutional factors associated with a type of campus victimization including but extending beyond sexual violence, encompassing work on bullying, hate crimes and discrimination, stalking, intimate partner violence, and overall crime rates.

The inclusion of multiple forms of victimization reflects the growing understanding that the gender-based power inequalities and oppressions that contribute to rates of sexual violence take place in contexts characterized by other power-based inequalities and other forms of violent victimization. Researchers must move beyond attention to singular forms of gender-based violence, as well as beyond a focus only on gender as an axis of social inequality, to examine how gender and sexuality as socially organized inequalities intertwine with racism, heterosexism, classism, and ableism to produce multiple forms of violence that are racial, sexual, gender, class, and ability-based (Buchanan & Wiklund, 2021). Intersectionality, a term rooted in Black Feminism and coined by Kimberle Crenshaw (1989), refers to a perspective that considers the meaning and consequences of multiple, overlapping categories of identity and disadvantage. We assert that risk for multiple forms of campus victimization can be understood through an intersectional lens that situates victimization as a product of societal inequalities and power differentials reflected across the microcosms of academic institutions.

This review paper takes a step in that direction by summarizing research on the effect of institutional- and environmental- level factors, including campus demographics and campus resources and financial characteristics, on institutional-level rates of several different forms of violent victimization. Specifically, it examines studies that compare rates and frequencies across crime as an overall category, which is legally defined and can include both interpersonal (e.g., rape) and non-interpersonal (e.g., theft) harms, as well as sexual and relationship violence, including partner violence, stalking, and bullying. These latter experiences are socially defined and may or may not be considered a crime (e.g., emotional abuse). Including both types of harm was important to understand institutional characteristics associated with victimization as many established data sources that are used for institution-level analyses are available from law enforcement and other criminal legal resources.

Methods

Search Strategy

A research librarian identified potential studies across three electronic databases: Web of Science Core Collection (1985-September 2020) via Clarivate Analytics, PubMed via National Library of Medicine, and PsycINFO via American Psychological Association for any entries from inception until September 2020. Search results were limited to English language studies. A combination of Medical Subject Heading [MeSH] terms (or equivalent) and free text were searched in the title and abstract fields and included: crime victims [MeSH], intimate partner violence [MeSH], violence [MeSH], workplace violence [MeSH], gender Based violence [MeSH], domestic violence [MeSH], spouse abuse[MeSH], bullying[MeSH], sexual Behavior [MeSH], sex offenses [MeSH], aggression[MeSH], rape [MeSH], physical abuse”[MeSH] AND college campus*, multicollege*, multiple campus*, multiple universities, academic institution*, educational institution*, multiple college*, multiple campus*, multiple university*. A topic search was conducted in Web of Science. Journal and Dissertation filters were applied in PsycINFO. All studies were imported into Zotero software for duplicate removal, screening and data extraction. A complete list of search strategies in each database is available in Appendix A.

Inclusion Criteria

Articles, book chapters, reports, and dissertations indexed by September 2020 were included in the current review. To be included in this review, manuscripts needed to be published or translated into English, empirical in nature, focus on some form of victimization (sexual violence, physical violence, dating violence, bullying, identity-based violence/ discrimination or hate crime), include data from multiple campuses at either the secondary or post-secondary level, and report on campus characteristics including fixed physical characteristics (e.g., campus size, type of school, location), cultural (e.g., school climate and perceptions of support), and policy-level (e.g., practices and procedures for preventing and responding to victimization) factors. Qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods research was eligible for inclusion.

Exclusion Criteria

Non-empirical papers, including commentaries, editorials, and reviews were excluded, as were papers that collected data from a single campus, papers that did not include data on campus characteristics, and papers published in other languages without translation to English.

Identifying Other Eligible Papers

Consistent with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta- Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, the reference lists of eligible papers were examined for additional relevant articles that may not have been identified by our search. We identified 39 additional papers for review and possible inclusion (Figure 1).

Study Selection

The first, second, or last author performed initial and double-screening of the titles and abstracts of all 1124 records to determine whether the full text of the study should be reviewed; 43 studies met the inclusion criteria described above (Figure 1).

Data Extraction and Review Procedures

For each article deemed eligible for inclusion, the first, second or last author extracted year of publication, type of publication (journal article, dissertation, etc.), type of analysis (quantitative vs. qualitative vs. mixed methods), sampling method (representative, census, convenience), sample size, number of institutions examined, response rates (if applicable), dataset(s) used, forms of victimization examined, campus characteristics, and study findings. Each article was then reviewed a second time by the first author when creating a table of characteristics for which there is evidence of an association with victimization (see Table 1).

Results

Campus Characteristics Associated with Crime

Eleven studies published since 1978 examined the association between campus characteristics and criminal victimization. The preponderance of research examining campus-level characteristics associated with crimes reflects 1980s- and early 1990s- era concerns about crime as a social problem. Bromley (1994), for example, opens by citing stories in the popular press about campus safety, such as a 1990 USA Today story titled “Students ‘easy prey’ on Campus” (Ordovensky, 1990, cited in Bromley, 1994). A more recent and much smaller set of articles examines campus-level factors associated with hate crimes (Stotzer 2010, 2012) (although, as we discuss in the conclusion, our search strategy may have failed to identify the full range of research on hate crimes). One major theme of the research on crime and campus characteristics – which reflects a more general theme in this literature review – is how strongly the articulation of specific research questions both reflects and shapes the nature of public debates (Gusfield, 1981). In many of these articles, there is explicit reference to how public concern spurs research that examines campus crime, but that research also seems to have solidified (or at least failed to challenge) understandings about the problem itself.

Study Descriptives

The majority of research on institutional characteristics and campus crime examined post-secondary institutions, with the exception of studies focusing on bullying and criminal victimization (Department of Education, 2016). The number of institutions studied in the research on campus crime varied widely, with a few in the double (Fisher et al., 1998; Bromley, 1994; McPheters, 1978) or even single digits (Fisher & Wilkes, 2003) and the remainder in the hundreds. The Fisher studies, which collected data directly from students, had response rates around 70%. None of the studies that used only institutional-level data had a representative sample of educational institutions, although the 2016 Education Department (ED) study drew on a nationally-representative sample of the US population.

The earliest set of articles looking at campus crime (Bromley, 1994; McPheters, 1978; Sloan, 1992; Sloan 1994) used campus-level data, either from the Uniform Crime Report (UCR) or from individual campus security or police departments. Crimes included murder, non-negligent manslaughter, forcible rape, burglary, aggravated sexual assault, larceny, motor vehicle theft, and arson. Some (e.g., Bromley, 1994) used a simple count of crimes from the UCR as an outcome variable, while others (McPheters, 1978; Fox & Hellman, 1985) used the same data presented as a rate. Sloan used a dataset with information about 494 campuses collected by a USA Today reporter doing a story on campus crime nationally (Sloan, 1992, 1994, citing Ordovensky, 1990), with data from 481 of the campuses collected directly from each school’s campus police or security, and from 13 additional schools from UCR reports; Sloan’s first analysis (1992) uses a count of crimes, while the second one (1994) looks at campus rates.

This first set of articles used data for crimes that were officially reported and thus relatively rare, as indicated by reporting as rates/1,000; in no cases do they exceed two digits. Three explicitly used the phrase “Ivory Tower” to examine campus crime (Fernandez & Lizotte, 1995; Fisher et al., 1998; Fisher & Wilkes, 2003). Fisher and colleagues (1998) examined the nature, extent, and causes of victimization of students in higher education, pointing to “lifestyle factors,” including partying, that are associated with victimization. Fisher and Wilkes (2003) expanded this work by examining lifestyle factors associated with higher rates of violent victimization on UK campuses compared to US campuses.

A second wave of research included some methodological innovation, collecting data directly either by phone (Fisher et al., 1998; Fisher & Wilkes, 2003) or through mail-in surveys (Fisher & Wilkes, 2003) from those victimized. These studies found substantially higher rates of victimization compared to research relying on official reports. Whereas Fox and Hellman’s (1985) total average campus crime rate was 32/1,000, Fisher et al.’s (1998) overall rate was 601/1,000. This is also the earliest research that compared on-campus and off-campus victimization and that framed the problem as requiring multi-level analysis of individual and contextual factors (Fernandez & Lizotte, 1995).

A third more recent set of articles, measuring criminal victimization both by self-report (Department of Education, 2016) and FBI hate crime reports (Stotzer, 2010; Stotzer & Hossellman, 2012; Van Dyke & Tester, 2014), reflect a fundamentally different concern in terms of student victimization: institutional and contextual factors related to identity-based peer persecution. The secondary-school level (Department of Education, 2016) analysis reflects recent concern about school bullying combined with the more longstanding attention to criminal victimization; that report examines the association of campus characteristics with criminal victimization, but also looks at the association between criminal victimization as a school characteristic and percentages of students experiencing bullying or cyberbullying (table S3). The other three studies examine campus characteristics in relation to the experiences of minoritized students, with the first looking at rates of reported sexual-orientation-based hate crimes (Stotzer, 2010) and the second two looking at racially-based hate crimes (Stotzer & Hosselman, 2012; Van Dyke & Tester, 2014). In comparison with the earlier articles that use official crime reports without considering reporting bias, this newer work addresses the question of underreporting; Stotzer (2010), for example, notes that hate crimes in particular “have been documented to be seriously under-reported to law enforcement” (p. 147) and “can be interpreted as a system’s willingness to report, rather than as an actual count of incidence” (p. 148). Unlike Bromley’s (1994) or Sloan’s (1992, 1994) earlier work (which uses single-year incident counts), this later work (Stotzer, 2010, Stotzer & Hosselman, 2012) accounts for the instability of single-year estimates by using an average of ten years of data.

Campus characteristic data come from various sources. Some researchers extracted information from guides designed for students applying to colleges such as Barron’s (Bromley, 1994), Peterson’s Guide to Four Year Colleges and Universities (Fernandez & Lizotte, 1995; Sloan 1992, 1994), or The Princeton Review Guide (Stotzer, 2012). Others used public data such as the National Center for Education Statistics (Stotzer, 2012, 2016) or IPEDs (Van Dyke & Tester, 2014) and one set of papers (Sloan, 1992, 1994) drew on information collected by a journalist. Many combined data from multiple sources. Fernandez and Lizotte (1995), for example, used data from the Consortium for Higher Education Campus Crime Research combined with information from Peterson’s Guide, the US Census, and “other authoritative sources” (p. 85), and Fisher et al. (1998) combined aggregate data from students’ individual responses with census tract-level data on SES and age structure.

Institutional Demographics.

Demographic characteristics examined in relation to crime rates on campuses included racial composition, gender composition, proportion involved in Greek life, and proportion living on campus. Results regarding percentages of students living on campus were mixed. Some studies showed that schools with higher percentages of students living on campus have higher campus crime rates (Fernandez & Lizotte, 1995; Fox & Hellman, 1985, McPheters, 1978); however, one found that schools with more students living on campus had a lower rate of theft (Fisher et al., 1998).

Institution Location, Setting & Community Characteristics.

Many studies have examined the school setting (urban, rural, suburban) as a factor influencing crime rate on campus. Notably, only one – Sloan’s (1992, 1994) research – found a significant relationship between setting and crime rates. Others examined geographic locations. Fisher and Wilkes (2003) compared crime rates on campuses in the US vs. England and found that crime rates on English campuses were significantly higher than US ones. The only exception was personal theft without contact, where US students had a significantly higher rate of victimization than English students. Studies have also examined how community characteristics outside the campus affect campus crime rates, with some studies finding little to no effect (Fernandez & Lizotte, 1995, Fisher et al., 1998, Fox & Hellman, 1985) and one (McPheters, 1978) finding that community unemployment rate has a positive influence on campus crime rate.

Financial Characteristics.

Some studies looked at campus crime in relation to institutions’ financial characteristics. Four studies looked at costs (i.e., tuition, room, and board) association with campus crime rates (Fernandez & Lizotte, 1995; Fox & Hellman, 1985; Sloan, 1992; Sloan, 1994). Fernandez and Lizotte (1995) found that campuses with higher room and board costs had higher rates of all forms of crime. Fox and Hellman (1985) also found a strong positive correlation between cost and campus crime, with both studies suggesting that economic inequalities might underlie this relationship. Sloan (1994), however, reported a mixed effect of cost on crime; cost was directly related to thefts and vandalism but inversely related to violence.

Campus Characteristics Associated with Sexual Assault Victimization

Study Descriptives

Twenty papers published between 2004 and 2020 addressed campus characteristics associated with sexual assault victimization. All but one (Oriol et al., 2019) focused on sexual violence at the post-secondary level. The majority (18/20) were published articles and two were dissertations (Damewood, 2019; Seifers, 2020). The number of institutions examined in each paper ranged from two (Graham et al., 2019; 2020) to 11,181 (Seifers, 2020). The definitions of sexual assault victimization also varied with some studies focusing narrowly on the experience of rape (Mohler-Kuo et al., 2004), many focusing on sexual assault (i.e., unwanted or non-consensual experiences ranging from sexual touching through penetration; Moylan et al., 2019), and others broadening to sexual harassment, sexist behavior, unwanted sexual contact, and related behaviors like stalking (Brubaker, 2009). Fourteen studies analyzed data collected directly from students, five analyzed data reported to the ED for Clery purposes, and one combined data from students with institution-level data (Seifers, 2020). For studies using student self-report data, four used random sampling, six used census-based sampling, and ten used convenience sampling. For studies using random or census sampling methods, response rates ranged from 19% (DeLong et al., 2018) to 85% plus (Brubaker, 2009).

Institutional Demographics.

Demographic characteristics of the student body, including gender, racial, and sexual identity composition also have been examined in relation to sexual assault rates. Using data from the National College Health Assessment (NCHA), Moylan and colleagues (2019) found that institutions with a higher proportion of female students had higher rates of sexual assault victimization, while institutions with a higher proportion of older students and those with a higher proportion of heterosexual students had lower rates of sexual assault victimization. However, other studies (Damewood, 2019; Stotzer & MacCartney, 2016) have not found elevated sexual assault rates in institutions with higher proportions of female students.

Institution Location, Setting & Community Characteristics.

One study found that Canadian college students are 54% more likely than American college students to indicate on a survey that they had experienced sexual assault victimization (Daigle et al., 2019), suggesting that national setting is a characteristic that merits further investigation. Findings may reflect higher rates of binge drinking and marijuana use among Canadian college students, a lower age of legal drinking in Canada, and less use of drinking protective behavioral strategies among Canadian students compared to US students. Location within the US also has been associated with risk for college sexual assault. Specifically, institutions in rural settings and those in the Southern and Midwestern (vs. the Western) regions of the United States have been shown to have higher rates of overall sexual assault victimization (Moylan et al., 2019) and intoxicated sexual assault victimization (Mohler-Kuo et al., 2004).

Institution Type.

Many studies examined associations between type of institution (variously defined) and estimates of sexual violence. This includes a wide range of institutional characteristics (military vs. non-military, HBCU vs. Primarily White Institute, religious affiliation, public vs. private). Brubaker found past-year estimates of sexual assault victimization to be similar among women at military service academies (MSAs) and traditional 2- or 4-year institutions (2009). In contrast, Krebs and coauthors found that women at Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) have significantly lower sexual assault victimization rates compared to women at PWIs (Krebs et al., 2011) – a finding that appears to be entirely driven by the lower odds of incapacitated sexual assault at HBCUs compared to primarily White institutions (PWIs). Opposite patterns emerge for forcible sexual assault, where women at HBCUs have higher odds (OR = 1.54) for forcible sexual assault compared to women at PWIs.

Perhaps the strongest pattern emerged for the comparison between public and private institutions: multiple studies reproduced the finding that private institutions have higher rates of sexual assault victimization than public institutions, both when examining reports made to the US ED (Damewood, 2019; Wiersma-Mosley et al., 2020) as well as when using student self-report data from the NCHA (Moylan et al., 2019). However, differences between US public and private institutions in sexual assault victimization were not found across all studies (e.g., Stotzer et al., 2016), and according to American Association of Universities (AAU) data (collected via self-report), repeat exposure to violence, including sexual assault, on campus is more likely to be reported at public vs. private institutions (Kaasa et al., 2016). National context may matter in this case; a study of high school students in Peru showed that “sexual harassment,” defined as uncomfortable sexual touching or forced intercourse, was more prevalent in public (vs. private) institutions.

In an analysis of sexual assaults reported to the US ED, Christian schools had higher rates of rape and fondling compared to non-Christian institutions (Purvis, 2020). Christian schools also lacked sexual health services compared to non-Christian schools, suggesting that greater attention to sexual health may help address problems of sexual violence on Christian campuses.

Institution Size/Density.

Other institutional characteristics are associated with sexual assault victimization rates. For example, using large samples of schools (e.g., 474–524 institutions), studies have found that schools with larger student body size (Moylan et al., 2019; Stotzer & MacCartney, 2016) and campuses that are primarily residential (Stotzer & MacCartney, 2016) have higher rates of sexual assault victimization. However, in an analysis of 44 institutions that included these factors along with personal demographic characteristics and behavioral factors (e.g., personal substance use), neither campus size nor residentiality were associated with sexual assault victimization after accounting for personal characteristics (Rogers & Rogers, 2020). Of note, the latter study (with only 44 campuses) may have been underpowered to detect the effects of institutional factors on sexual assault victimization rates.

Institutional Assault-related Policies.

Findings regarding campus policies and sexual assault victimization have been mixed. For example, DeLong and colleagues (2018) found that across 24 AAU campuses, those with more comprehensive campus sexual assault policies had lower rates of sexual assault victimization, although findings were not statistically significant. A dissertation examining 361 institutions of higher education did not find associations between campus policies and initiatives (i.e., the presence or absence of policies related to sexual assault and disciplinary action and programming for prevention, mental health services, victim legal services, etc.) and sexual assault victimization reported to the US ED (Damewood, 2019).

Institutional Climate.

Emerging evidence suggests that campus climate, operationalized as student perceptions of some aspect of the climate (e.g., tolerance of violence), is important for understanding sexual violence rates, although there are questions about directionality of associations. For example, campuses perceived by students as more inclusive of sexual- and gender-minority (SGM) people have lower rates of sexual assault victimization among SGM students (Coulter & Rankin, 2020). Campuses where students believe other students and campus officials would not be supportive of a student who reports a sexual assault and would not investigate and take action against an offender have higher rates of repeat sexual victimization (Kaasa et al., 2016). These findings may extend beyond perceptions, as at least one study found that campuses with students reporting higher proportions of discrimination experiences have higher rates of sexual assault victimization (Moylan et al., 2019).

In addition to these climate variables, researchers also have measured whether the degree to which campuses provide information about sexual assault to students is associated with rates of sexual violence. For instance, an analysis of Clery data revealed that campuses that partnered with EVERFI, an organization that provides prevention programming, had higher rates of rape reported to the US ED after controlling for campus alcohol and drug crime (Seifers, 2020). Partnering with prevention organizations is unlikely to cause rape on campus; rather, schools that partner with prevention organizations may differ from schools that do not in their public acknowledgement of sexual violence as a problem or messaging around reporting.

Student Participation in Activities.

In addition to demographics, research examined other normative behaviors on campus such as binge drinking or the proportion of students on campus involved in higher-risk activities such as fraternity and sorority life (FSL) and varsity athletics. Using data from 119 schools that participated in the Harvard School of Public Health College Alcohol study, Mohler-Kuo and colleagues (2004) found that women who attended colleges with high rates of heavy episodic drinking (i.e., binge drinking) were more likely to experience incapacitated sexual assault. Similarly, when examining sexual assaults reported to the ED, “party” schools, defined as public institutions with higher tuition, more liquor violations, and greater numbers of fraternity men and athletes were more likely to report rape on their campuses (Wiersma-Mosley et al., 2017). In a separate analysis of rapes reported to the ED, Stotzer and MacCartney (2016) found that residential schools, those with a greater proportion of men in fraternities, and those with more competitive athletics programs, more permissive alcohol policies, or more law enforcement personnel had higher rates of rape reported to the ED. After controlling for other significant factors, only campuses that were more residential, those with NCAA Division I athletics, and those that allowed students 21 and older to possess alcohol had higher rates of sexual assault victimization (Stotzer & MacCartney, 2016). When examining self-report data from students participating in the NCHA, institutions with higher campus rates of binge drinking and a greater proportion of students who had experienced discrimination had higher rates of sexual assault victimization; however, after accounting for these and other institution-level factors, the proportion of students involved in FSL and varsity athletics was not associated with sexual assault victimization (Moylan et al., 2019). Together, these findings point strongly to campus drinking culture, whether operationalized in terms of alcohol policies for students of legal drinking age, liquor violations, or rates of binge drinking, as an institution-level risk factor for sexual violence. Further, athletics and Greek life, activities that center around drinking culture, may pose a risk to students, although findings vary across studies.

Campus Characteristics Associated with Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) and Stalking

Other forms of violent victimization may co-occur with sexual assault victimization. For example, in the AAU study, Kaasa and colleagues (2016) found that 67% of students who experienced forced penetration during the current year reported at least one other kind of victimization, including incapacitated penetration, unwanted sexual touching, stalking or IPV. IPV refers to a partner controlling, using physical harm against, or threatening to physically harm the student, themselves, or someone the student loves (Kaasa et al., 2016). Stalking refers to someone repeatedly making unwanted contact (e.g., phone calls, emails, text messages), unwanted appearances (e.g., waiting for the student), or spying on, watching, or following the student and causing the student to feel afraid for their personal safety (Kassa et al., 2016).

Eight papers focused on IPV, stalking, or both, often in addition to examining sexual assault victimization at the post-secondary level. One paper (Fantasia et al., 2018) found no differences in sexual violence or IPV rates across the five campuses they studied. A study of stalking rates among students in MSAs vs. non-military institutions indicated that 4–8% of MSA students experienced stalking during the last academic year, compared to 13.1% of college students who experienced stalking in the 7 months since starting college (Brubaker, 2009).

Generally, campus predictors of IPV differed from those for sexual assault victimization. For example, using data from 474 US campuses, Moylan and colleagues (2019) found that public (vs. private institutions) and campuses where students were older, reported a greater number of sexual partners, had lower rates of binge drinking, and lower proportions of full-time students had higher IPV rates. Thus, institution type, binge drinking, and student age showed opposite patterns of risk for IPV than for sexual assault victimization. Interestingly, this same pattern also emerged for exposure to repeat and/or multiple types of victimization such that public schools had higher rates compared to private universities (Kaasa et al., 2016). Wiersma-Mosley and colleagues (2020) used latent class analysis to classify 1384 postsecondary institutions according to characteristics and found that “party schools,” defined as public schools with large student bodies, more alcohol use violations, Greek life and prominent athletics did not have higher rates of violence against women, including sexual assault, IPV, and stalking, as evidenced by reports to the ED. However, party schools and private institutions had significantly more Title IX investigations, which may reflect mishandling of reports of sexual assault, compared to small schools, liberal arts institutions, and satellite institutions. Small schools, defined as those with a smaller student body, few alcohol violations, little Greek life or athletics, and fewer White students, had lower rates of violence against women reported to the ED (Wiersma-Mosley et al., 2020). As with the findings for rape reported above, schools that partnered with EVERFI on prevention programming also reported higher rates of stalking to the US ED (Seifers, 2020).

Our search identified only a small number of studies focused specifically on the victimization experiences of students with marginalized identities. One used NCHA data to examine physical violence and assault as well as verbal threats and stalking behavior among Asian American and Asian international students, finding elevated rates of all forms of victimization among Asian Americans compared to Asian international students (Maffini et al., 2018). Additionally, a study of perceived campus readiness to address IPV among LGBTQ+ in the US students found that perceptions of campus climate for LGBTQ+ students and sense of community varied by region of the country, with students at campuses in the Northeastern US perceiving a more positive climate and a stronger sense of community for LGBTQ+ students, compared to students on campuses in other regions of the country (Edwards et al., 2016). Additionally, students at private, non-religiously affiliated schools reported a more positive climate and a stronger sense of community for LGBTQ+ students compared to students at public or private, religiously affiliated institutions. Students who had experienced IPV victimization had stronger perceptions of campus readiness to address IPV compared to students who had not.

Campus Characteristics Associated with Peer-related Bullying

Study Descriptives

Our review also examined research on institutional factors associated with peer-related bullying in educational settings. All studies were published in or after 2010, a year in which Tyler Clementi’s death by suicide (Parker, 2012) and the ensuing “It Gets Better” public awareness campaign (Parker-Pope, 2010) focused public attention on the persecution of gender and sexual minority youth in educational settings, as well as on bullying more generally. Ten papers published between 2010 and 2020 addressed campus characteristics related to peer-related bullying. Four (DePedro et al., 2016, Mucherah et al., 2018, Oriol et al., 2019, Williams, 2015) looked at victimization at the secondary level and included traditional bullying and cyberbullying. Six (Daigle, 2019, Fantasia et al., 2018, Maffini, 2018, Rivas et al., 2020, Stotzer, 2010, Stotzer & Hossellman, 2012) focused on the post-secondary level.

Among research that examined secondary-level victimization, sample type varied widely, with one article comparing US public and private high schools (Williams, 2015), another looking specifically at US military-connected high school students (DePedro et al., 2016), and two looking at international settings (Mucherah et al., 2018, Oriol et al., 2019). Mucherah et al. (2018) examined bullying and victimization among a sample of high school students in Kenya, whereas Oriol et al. (2019) examined bullying victimization among high school students in Peru. Articles that examined US-based samples took a different route, with DePedro et al. (2016) examining peer victimization among a sample of military- and nonmilitary-connected high school students and Williams (2015) exploring the relationship between organizational chaos, racial composition, and bullying incidents among nationally-representative high school students.

When assessing victimization rates, three articles used data collected directly from students (DePedro et al., 2016, Mucherah et al., 2018, Oriol et al., 2019). All three articles used different instruments and approaches to assess different dimensions of victimization. DePedro et al. (2016) used the California Healthy Kids Survey (CHKS) that assessed two domains of victimization, physical and nonphysical. Mucherah et al. (2018) used the Adolescent Peer Relations instrument to assess physical, verbal, and social peer victimization (Parada, 2000). Oriol et al. (2019) used the School Violence Questionnaire (MINEDU) to assess traditional and cyberbullying. Rather than use self-report data, Williams (2015) relied on school administrators’ knowledge of bullying at their schools, as assessed in the Educational Longitudinal Study of 2002 (ELS).

Of the six studies identified that examined institutional factors related to peer-victimization at the post-secondary level, three used students’ self-reported data, two focused on data reported to the FBI’s UCR, and one performed online content analysis. Studies that focused on self-reported data used different datasets. Two articles used data from the NCHA to measure victimization (Daigle et al., 2019, Maffini, 2018). Daigle et al. (2019) used the Spring 2013 NCHA data to find higher violent victimization rates among Canadian vs US college students. Maffini (2018) used the Fall 2014 data to show that Asian Americans were more likely to report verbal threats compared to Asian international students (12.7% vs 7.2%).

One of the articles that used data from the FBI’s UCR looked at racial/ethnic hate crimes (Stotzer & Hossellman, 2012) while the other looked at sexual-orientation based hate crimes (Stotzer, 2010). The final article on college students analyzed anonymous conversations posted online on Yik Yak in four states (California, Florida, New York, and Texas; Rivas et al., 2020).

Institutional Demographics.

Studies looked at associations between demographic diversity (e.g., racial, sexual) and campus victimization rates. Both Stotzer and Hossellman (2012) and Williams (2015) found that schools with higher percentages of Black and Latino students reported lower victimization rates. However, Stotzer and Hossellman (2012) found that the percentage of Asian students on campus was not significantly associated with victimization rates. Similarly, Williams (2015) found that schools with a higher percentage of Asian students reported more bullying until a threshold was reached, and thereafter bullying declined. These findings indicate the importance of considering racial and ethnic minorities as distinct groups, rather than lumping them together as “students of color”, as they may be impacted differently by discrimination in schools. Notably, aspects of racial composition were examined more frequently in research on bullying and peer-related victimization than in research on institutional characteristics associated with sexual violence and IPV victimization.

Institution Location, Setting & Community Characteristics.

Four studies (Daigle et al., 2019, Fantasia et al., 2018, Oriol et al., 2019, Rivas et al., 2020) examined geographical campus characteristics associated with peer-related victimization. Daigle et al. (2019) found that levels of both violent and sexual victimization were higher among Canadian vs. US college students. Notably, being Black predicted violent victimization in the US but not in Canada. Other studies looked at geographical differences within US institutions. For example, Oriol et al. (2019) found that urban schools had higher levels of traditional bullying compared to rural schools. Rivas and colleagues (2020) social media analysis, which examined data from a small number of schools from four states, found differences across states in the frequency of cyber bullying messages. Given the growing role of social media, these findings should be corroborated in future research with larger data.

Institution Type.

Type of school (public vs. private) has been examined in relation to victimization. For example, Oriol et al. (2019) found higher levels of traditional bullying and sexual harassment in public schools, but higher rates of cyberbullying in private schools. Mucherah et al. (2018) also measured school type (i.e., all-girls, all-boys, or co-ed school) and found that attending an all-boys school was associated with lower victimization rates.

State- and School-level Policies.

Articles on state- and school-level policies associated with peer-related victimization all focused on LGBTQ students. However, results were mixed, with one study finding a positive association of inclusive policies on victimization rates (Hong et al., 2016), one finding a negative association (Stotzer, 2010), and one finding no association (Poteat et al., 2013). Hong et al. (2016) found that LGBQ college students who reported that their school’s anti-discrimination policy included sexual orientation were less likely to experience verbal threats. Stozer (2010) found that schools with more LGB inclusive state- and school-level policies report higher rates of hate crime on campus. Finally, Poteat et al. (2013) found no effect of the presence of Gay-Straight Alliance (GSA) on homophobic victimization among LGBTQ middle and high school students.[1]

Institution Climate.

A positive school climate, indicated by having a caring relationship with adults, high expectations from teachers, school connectedness, meaningful participation (DePedro et al., 2016), and high satisfaction with the school (Mucherah et al., 2018) is a protective factor associated with lower levels of peer victimization. Addressing a different aspect of school climate (perception of safety on campus), Maffini (2018) found that Asian American students were more likely to both report feeling unsafe on campus and report being verbally threatened in comparison to their Asian international student counterparts.

Discussion

Progress has been made in identifying institutional characteristics associated with victimization, yet some gaps remain. Generally, institution demographics, institution type, institution climate, institution financial characteristics, and educational characteristics were associated with various forms of victimization while institution setting (urban vs. rural) was not. Institution location and institution size/density were associated with victimization in some studies but not others, and institution type and institution demographics operated differently for some forms of victimization. For example, sexual assault victimization rates were higher at private (vs. public) institutions, whereas IPV rates were higher at public (vs. private) institutions. Bullying experiences also varied by institution type such that higher rates of in-person bullying were observed at public schools while higher rates of cyberbullying were observed at private institutions. Campus demographic (e.g., mean age; % male) and behavioral (e.g., alcohol environment; % of students living on campus) factors were differentially associated with victimization types, underscoring the limitations of one-size-fits-all prevention approaches.

One major take-away from the studies reviewed is that progress in this line of research is substantially hampered by data limitations. To advance research, a multi-campus data set of victimization rates with institutional identifiers could be maintained through a restricted data center to allow for the analysis of highly sensitive data while mitigating concerns about institutional reputation. Researchers could then combine the data with institutional characteristics from other sources and publish analyses that address characteristics associated with vulnerability without revealing which campuses have the highest rates of specific forms of victimization.

A second challenge for research on institutional characteristics concerns reporting bias. For example, research that uses formally reported victimization (e.g., Clery reports, FBI UCR) as outcome data may significantly underestimate the prevalence and scope of victimization compared to studies that aggregate individual-level self-reports of victimization collected from a representative sample via anonymous/confidential survey. Even in survey research, the methodology used to elicit experiences of victimization can vary significantly with some studies (e.g., the AAU) using gold-standard behaviorally-specific measures to assess sexual violence and others asking single questions that require acknowledgement and labeling of experiences as victimization. Meta-analyses suggest that 60% of women who report a behaviorally specific act that meets the definition of rape do not label their experiences as such (Wilson & Miller, 2016); thus, the latter assessment strategy is likely to significantly underestimate victimization. Cross-campus research must scrupulously attend to whether findings reflect differences in experiencing, acknowledging/labeling, or making official reports of victimization.

Third, perceptions of how the campus would handle investigations, or trust in officials, were often not aggregated at the campus level, but rather examined as an individual correlate of victimization (e.g., Kaasa et al., 2016), reflecting a missed opportunity to examine this element of campus climate. Some surveys may have collected individual-level attitudes or beliefs about campus climate (e.g., support for LGBTQ students) across multiple campuses, but have not created or made available campus-level aggregations of those variables that would permit comparison. These malleable factors could serve as critical intervention targets.

Our review leads to additional recommendations for advancing this line of inquiry. First, as illustrated in table 1, campus characteristics research has included both objective (e.g., composition of the student body) and subjective (supportiveness of minoritized racial or ethnic groups) measures of the campus environment. Some of the subjective and objective factors are modifiable through institutional intervention (policies regarding alcohol, rooming, sexual assault prevention and response, DEI) and others (the physical environment, public/private, campus size, location) are not. Both subjective and objective factors may be important and attending to factors that are not modifiable may ensure that prevention and response work fits the environment. Research going forward should consider both the influence of campus characteristics on individual experience (through multilevel modeling), as well as how aggregate experience is influenced by environmental factors. Each analytic strategy addresses a different question: the first would advance understanding of how campus factors might be differentially related to individual vulnerability, while the second could illuminate associations between campus characteristics and overall rates of victimization.

There are limitations to the findings here. First, although we frame this review by discussing why it is critical to adopt an intersectional approach to understanding associations between campus characteristics and victimization, the studies reviewed were largely not intersectional in nature. For example, although we identified some institutional indicators of gender or economic inequality, none of the reviewed studies measured multiple axes of inequality (e.g., examining climate in relation to gender inequality and discrimination experienced by LGBTQ+, racial or ethnic minority, or disabled students). Second, there may be research on institutional characteristics related to cross-campus variation in rates of racial and ethnic discrimination that was not identified by this review. Third, most of the studies identified were primarily about sexual or gender-based misconduct. This may have been an artifact of the search terms, which included crimes (and so identified studies about racially or ethnically targeted hate crimes) but not discrimination. Fourth, most studies examined only one or two forms of victimization, with little attention to the experience of multiple forms of victimization. Given the importance of creating campus climates in which students from all backgrounds can thrive, there is an urgent need for research that examines the association of multiple forms of social disadvantage with cross-campus variation in victimization, including discrimination relating to minoritized identities (e.g., de Heer & Jones, 2017). Research on campus characteristics could be more attentive to diversity by centering the experiences of multiply-marginalized students.

Additionally, research examining secondary institutions focused mainly on peer-related victimization, like bullying. Although sexual assault and other forms of gender-based victimization occur at secondary institutions, the contextual and institutional factors that shape victimization experiences and the policies created to address them may vary and additional research on gender-based violence as well as other forms of power-based violence needs to focus on these settings. Another limitation relates to the small number of institutions examined in some of this research, leaving some analyses substantially underpowered to examine institutional differences. Additionally, some studies identified in the search collected data on campus characteristics but did not publish analyses using those variables (e.g., Lindquist et al., 2013) and thus were excluded; re-analysis of those data could yield fruitful findings. Recent campus climate research has highlighted additional areas for assessment, including felt, structural, and historical climate in addition to behavioral and perceived climate (Moylan et al., 2021), which is primarily what the studies reviewed here addressed. Future cross-campus research could include these elements.

Finally, although findings may have been limited by the inclusion of only English- language research, few studies on campus characteristics have been published outside the United States. Data sets such as the World Health Organization World Mental Health International College Student Initiative (Cuijpers et al., 2019) could be used to broaden the geographical scope in which campus characteristics are examined to understand victimization.

This review points to other key knowledge gaps and next steps in this line of inquiry. The dearth of qualitative and ethnographic research on these questions means that little is known about the pathways and social processes through which institutional characteristics influence rates of sexual violence. Additionally, campuses do not exist in a vacuum; while some factors (region of the country, urban/rural) may capture elements of institutional location or setting, mixed findings about the importance of these variables highlights the need to more precisely operationalize and examine how the community environment (e.g., surrounding alcohol environment) and state- or county-level policies, interacts with the campus environment to predict victimization rates.

Population-level prevention approaches emphasize the identification and modification of environmental characteristics to impact public health (CDC, 2011). The ecological model includes meso- and macro-level factors but also life-course factors, which fall outside of an examination of campus characteristics. Examining modifiable pre-matriculation factors associated with subsequent experiences of victimization represents another line of inquiry that could lead to highly impactful prevention policies. Work on pre-college sex education and campus sexual assault offers just one illustration of this approach (Santelli et al., 2018). Additionally, while acknowledging the many challenges associated with perpetration research, future work also should consider examining institutional characteristics associated with perpetration, rather than victimization, as this might pave the way for highly impactful approaches to primary prevention of sexual violence. Lastly, these ecological approaches have relevance beyond educational settings. For example, workplace prevention of sexual harassment and assault would also benefit from attending to the broader ecology (McDonald et al., 2015), rather than focusing solely on increasing knowledge and changing individual-level attitudes.

Implications for Practice, Policy, and Research

Practice and Policy Research

  • This review points to modifiable institutional and environmental factors (both institutional and environmental) associated with rates of student victimization in academic institutions. For policy to help prevent student victimization in academic institutions, policymakers should rely on evidence-based research to address these factors in the formulation of policy.

  • Policymakers should take a socioecological approach when creating policy that addresses student victimization, while paying close attention to the upper levels of the socioecological model (i.e., macro- and meso- levels).

  • More research is needed to examine the variation of the effect of campus characteristics on victimization rates. For example, studies could examine the effect of campus characteristics on individual experience, as well as their effect on aggregate experience.

  • Researchers studying the effect of institutional characteristics on student victimization should use a more intersectional lens to examine the association of multiple forms of social disadvantage with variation in sexual violence victimization.

  • To advance research examining these questions, it may be necessary to address some of the perceived reputational risks to individual universities that have precluded comparative analyses of datasets with data collected from multiple institutions.
