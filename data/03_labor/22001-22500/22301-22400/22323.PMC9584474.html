Abstract

Aim

To explore the experiences and perspectives of the combined use of continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) on parents of children with TIDM on their daily life.

Design

A systematic review and meta‐synthesis of qualitative studies.

Methods

A systematic literature search of English studies published in seven databases between 2006–2021: CINAHL, MEDLINE, EMBASE, PubMed, PsycINFO, Cochrane Library and Scopus. All included studies underwent the process of thematic interpretive integration by the author team.

Results

Nine studies met the inclusion criteria. Six derived themes were generated which contained interacting with devices, interacting with glycaemic information, improving quality of life for parents of children with T1DM, burden of living with CSII therapy and CGM, impact on the parent–child relationship, requirement and expectation to advanced diabetes technology. Advanced diabetes technologies affect physical, emotional and relationship between the daily life of parents and their children with T1DM.

INTRODUCTION

1

The incidence of Type 1 diabetes (T1DM) has increased significantly in many countries worldwide (Manuwald et al., 2017). Although there was a lower incidence of T1DM in Chinese children, the incidence amongst children under 15 has been rising dramatically over the past two decades (Weng et al., 2018). It is estimated that there are over 4,000 new cases of T1DM in children annually in China (Weng et al., 2018). Without appropriate insulin treatment and optimal glycaemic control, the condition of children with T1DM can exacerbate rapidly within 24 hr, resulting in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), permanent disability or even death (Cavan, 2018). Optimal diabetes management are important for children and adolescents with T1DM.

Taking care of children with T1DM who are receiving diabetes management can be challenging and requires care coordination between parents and children (Shulman & Daneman, 2010). In China, children with T1DM have been identified as a difficult age group to treat (Guo et al., 2013). Parents need to handle a diverse variety of problems related to diabetes management of their children, including adjustment of insulin use according to blood glucose level, nutritional and exercise control, prevention of complications and attention of children' emotional health (ADA, 2018; Lu et al., 2020). The unpredictability of diabetes affects children and requires parents to maintain vigilance, careful management and frequently make parents with psychological stress (Rifshana et al., 2017). Therefore, care‐giving burden of children with T1DM was significantly negatively associated with Chinese parents’ quality of life, and affects the quality of daily life of parents (Luo et al., 2021). It is particularly important to help children with T1DM stabilizing blood glucose through more convenient treatment.

To achieve optimal blood glucose control, blood glucose monitoring and insulin delivery are very important (Giuseppe et al., 2018; Pickup et al., 2015). Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) were successively introduced for people with T1DM, which can achieve continuous injection of exogenous insulin through an infusion pump and 24‐hr real‐time blood glucose monitoring (Forlenza et al., 2016). Such treatment is necessary to avoid complications, such as DKA, diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy (Atkinson et al., 2014; Ziegler et al., 2011). In 2006, the Artificial Pancreas project began (Forlenza et al., 2016). AP (artificial pancreas) therapy, SAP (sensor‐augmented pump) and automated insulin delivery systems also known as the closed‐loop diabetes system, combining CSII therapy with CGM, and adding a control algorithm insulin in a glucose‐responsive manner (Forlenza et al., 2016; Hovorka, 2015). Compared with single CSII therapy or CGM, the closed‐loop system can automatically adjust the amount of insulin that enters the body based on glucose sensor levels to optimize blood glucose on people with T1DM (Bekiari et al., 2018). Its clinical benefits have been evaluated in several studies and the optimistic results have been shown (ADA, 2018; Karageorgiou et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2017).

The closed‐loop diabetes system is now commercially available in some foreign countries (Boughton & Hovorka, 2021; Galderisi et al., 2017). However, some studies have found that the experience and views of using CSII therapy and CGM is mixed which might be related to the accuracy, frequent annoyances from alarms, insertion pain and the interruption to the daily life of users (Brew‐Sam et al., 2021; Messer et al., 2018). Notably, such advanced diabetes technology is only used in clinical research in China (Cai et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2020). Thus, most Chinese studies focus on the effectiveness and safety of the combined use of CSII therapy and CGM in adult population, also including some children and adolescents (Dai et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2020). Few Chinese findings describe the experiences on the combination of CSII therapy and CGM among people with T1DM and their parents or caregivers.

As these new features of CSII therapy and CGM mature, there will be more and more Chinese parents or caregivers need to choose treatment methods according to their children's needs. The major of diabetes technology developments in current and future also needs to concern about the breadth experiences and perspectives of parents or caregivers and their children with T1DM, and how to reduce their living burden. One way to understand these perspectives is to synthesize the existing qualitative literature related to parents’ experiences on advanced diabetes technology. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to explore the experiences and perspectives of the combined use of CSII therapy and CGM on parents of children with TIDM on their daily life, in order to support other new parents who plan to use CSII therapy and CGM to manage T1DM of their children in the future and design appropriate clinical interventions for adherence.

METHODS

2

This systematic review is a meta‐synthesis of the qualitative existing literature, including a literature search, process of quality appraisal, themes synthesis and reciprocal translation (Goins et al.,2015; McSharry et al., 2016), analysing topics according to established standards to solve problems (Moher et al., 2009). This review used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review (PRISMA) flow chart as a guideline for searching and selecting all qualitative studies, in order to establish an acceptable review structure and avoid bias (Moher et al., 2009).

Data sources and search strategy

2.1

The date of the literature search in the database was conducted from 2006–2021. This period can reflect the increased use with the combination of SCII therapy and CGM among children and teenagers with T1DM in the clinical field, and their commercial availability. The following electronic databases were used to search: CINAHL, MEDLINE, PubMed, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Cochrane Library and Scopus. The main search keywords included “parents,” “children with type 1 diabetes,” “insulin pump,” “CGM,” “artificial pancreas,” “closed‐loop control” and “experience." Other search terms included “mothers’ perspective,” “fathers’ views,” “CSII,” “continuous glucose monitoring,” “glycaemic control” and “advanced technology.” Publications only included studies published in the English language. Articles in the reference list were searched manually to identify any other potential studies that are suitable and relevant to the inclusion criteria. The search strategy is presented in Appendix 1. The search process is detailed in Figure 1.

Literature selection

2.2

Studies were selected for inclusion by the first author if they contained original qualitative or mixed‐method research related to parents' experiences with using the combination of SCII and CGM to manage T1DM among their children were considered. Studies that only explore adults, teenagers and healthcare professionals' experiences are not included. Critical articles, editorials and grey literature are excluded from the final yield. All included studies described children with T1DM who used portable subcutaneous insulin pumps and CGM in their daily life (including sensor‐augmented pump, artificial pancreas systems and automated insulin delivery system). Studies only used one of SCII therapy or CGM were excluded. Data unrelated to CGM, CSII, artificial pancreas functionality or data with ambiguous topics were not analysed. Assessment of methodological quality

2.3

CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme) aims to help people develop the necessary skills to make sense of scientific evidence, and has produced appraisal checklists covering validity, results and relevance. Ten questions of the CASP checklist were used by the review author of this systematic review to assess the quality of eight qualitative research and the qualitative elements of one mixed‐method studies in this systematic review (CASP International Network, 2013). An appropriate scoring system provided by Butler et al. (2016) was used to evaluate the quality of included studies related to study design, qualitative methodology, sampling, data collection, data analysis and rigour. The articles were categorized into three categories of high‐, moderate‐ and low‐quality in terms of methods (cut‐off scores: high‐quality study [achieved score≥9 points], moderate‐quality [7 points < achieved score < 8.5 points] and the low‐quality study [achieved score ≤7 points]). Both researchers independently scored each part of the checklist. If the answer to each question in the CASP checklist is "yes," the score is 1. If the answer is "no," the score is 0 and if the answer is “uncertain,” the score is 0.5. Different scores were discussed in one item by two researchers until an agreement was reached. As a result, there are eight high‐quality studies and one moderate‐quality study within the included qualitative studies. The quality evaluation results of one mixed‐method studies are one high‐quality level. All included studies regardless of quality that were included in this review, with the expectation that the moderate‐quality studies may still make valuable contributions to the synthesis (Boland et al., 2017; Campbell et al., 2018). Assessment process is presented in Appendix 2.

Data extraction and synthesis

2.4

In this systematic review, a structured meta‐synthesis approach was used to synthesize original qualitative data of included studies, which is called interpretive thematic integration, including extraction of important information from the research, developing the description of data‐driven themes and then synthesize themes through a process called reciprocal translation (Noblit & Hare, 1988; Thomas & Harden, 2008). First, each included study was analysed by the review author to determine research objectives, study setting, study design, research method, participants and results, and then the thematic contents of those studies were evaluated again to confirm each theme (Korhonen et al., 2013; Sandelowski, 2006). In this process, the entire qualitative content was regarded as data in each qualitative included study. Participant's quotations and authors' explanations will also be included in the qualitative content. The first and second author list topics related to the combined use of CSII therapy and CGM by parents' experiences for managing T1DM children. These articles were read to achieve a consensus on the proposed theme. This process was repeated many times. We carefully selected research topics that included CSII therapy and CGM, and included them only when our clinical judgement indicated that the topic was relevant to both.

The theme in each included study was indexed in the form of a table and create a comprehensive picture of common concept. Individual specific themes in this synthesis was classified and interpreted according to the concepts of those studies. According to the principle of Thomas and Harden (2008) and Noblit and Hare (1988), the process of "translating" synthesis concepts from one study to another is used to synthesize qualitative data across studies. The newly derived themes were aggregated into the final reciprocal translation table and reviewed by the authors' team and agreed upon. The final synthesis can provide new analytical themes and concepts about attitudes and views of parents on the combined use of CSII therapy and CGM to manage T1DM with their children. The data were extracted using the data extraction tool. Based on the data in each included study, the extraction tool was designed by the review author team (Butler et al., 2016). A data extraction form is outlined in Appendix 3.

RESULTS

3

The initial search for “children with type 1 diabetes,” “CSII” and “CGM,” “parents,” “experience” and their synonyms generated a total of 877 articles (Figure 1). With the other additional sources of 68 articles (including head searching, citation chaining), the total initial results of the scoping search were 945 articles. After removing 598 duplicated findings, 347 articles required further review. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 190 articles were not directly relevant to CSII, CGM, AP system and closed‐loop control systems, and other 99 articles are about quantitative methodology, so all needed to be excluded. Fifty‐eight remaining articles were required to obtain the full‐text and re‐read for further review. Sixteen articles only involved CGM, 24 articles only involved CSII therapy and nine systematic reviews were excluded (49 articles in total). A total of eight qualitative articles and one mixed‐method studies were yielded from included studies of this meta‐synthesis.

Theme 1: Interacting with devices

3.1

The theme of interacting with devices significantly impacts on the daily life of parents with children and adolescents with T1DM. The theme of interacting with devices mainly refers to parents' experience and reflection on the devices within the AP system, SAPT and closed‐loop technology (Barnard et al., 2014; Bomba et al., 2018; Gildersleeve et al., 2017; Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016), such as inserting glucose sensors and wearing sensors and transmitters on children, responding to alarms by pressing the button and problems of equipment “fails,” device setting and materials. There are four sub‐themes derived from this theme which include device size/design/settings, alarms, calibration issues and equipment troubleshooting. Parents reported their views about device materials and their size (Barnard et al., 2014; Gildersleeve et al., 2017). Some parents pointed out that insulin entering settings should establish a flexible passcode and add remote unlocking and emergency contact lists to these high‐tech systems (Gildersleeve et al., 2017). CGM, CSII and diabetes are never far from the mind of the user who has a bulky device affixed to the body that must be protected from bumping and dislodging. The extracts below illustrate this:

  • “Just having this little brick on the arm really…Just the size of it” (Barnard et al., 2014: e000025).

  • “It was quite big and I’d quite like to have a slightly little one” (Barnard et al., 2014: e000025).

  • "I’m more worried about them losing it and keeping up with it. The fanny pack is actually an excellent idea for keeping track of it and maybe discouraging sharing it with friends" (Gildersleeve et al., 2017:668).

Alarms are considered as a separate part of an appropriate CGM function, but also have impacts on parents' and children's life. Parents reflected dissatisfaction and frustration of the frequent alarms that could disturb their quality of sleep at night and have bad influences on their children (Barnard et al., 2014; Bomba et al., 2018; Wysocki et al., 2016). There are some parents who advised to limit the number and severity of high alarms in these devices and suggested making alarms simpler and accompanied by pictorial instructions (Gildersleeve et al., 2017). In addition, a variety of participants mentioned the issue of calibration and its difficulty within these devices that have influenced the daily life of participants (Barnard et al., 2014; Bomba et al., 2018; Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016). There are concerns that sensors or transmitters on the body may shift through daily activities, such as exercise or movement (Wysocki et al., 2016).

Parents in one article have particularly demonstrated that they may feel frustrated when equipment failure occurs or devices need to troubleshoot (Barnard et al., 2014).

  • "Sometimes it would stop working for a few minutes…every 5th day” (Barnard et al., 2014: e000025).

  • “It would sometimes turn off during the night, it sometimes wouldn't connect which took quite a long time to sort out…once or twice a week” (Barnard et al., 2014: e000025).

A timely alert will alert parents to the need for attention or the troubleshooting, however, alarm fatigue can cause users to ignore alerts and reduce trust in device usage and troubleshooting CGM and CSII devices can increase frustration and annoyance.

Theme 2: Interacting with glycaemic control

3.2

One of the important purposes of combining the use of CSII therapy and CGM on children and adolescents with T1DM is to optimize glucose levels. The first relevant sub‐theme in this theme is the majority of parents commented that stable glucose levels can be achieved by the combination of CSII therapy and CGM, which can reduce their concerns about unstable glucose levels in their children with T1DM (Barnard et al., 2014; Bomba et al., 2018; Garza et al., 2018; Lawton et al., ,2018, 2019, 2021; Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016). Users respond to real‐time trends in blood sugar, which can help them improve their hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia. Users of sensor‐enhanced pumps say that the insulin pump helps them to feel normal and CGM helps to keep blood sugar levels directly under control (Rashotte et al., 2014). With the help of the insulin pump, parents can perform insulin dose adjustment, food intake and overall pattern assessment (Lawton et al., 2018). Thought helping to control unpredictable blood sugar levels, detecting severe hypoglycaemia, and controlling exercise and dietary choices, CGM can achieve better blood sugar control (Wysocki et al., 2016).

Moreover, trust, reliability and sensor accuracy all influence parents' perception of the use of CSII therapy and CGM in combination. Reducing the incidence of hypoglycaemia and severe hypoglycaemia is also considered a benefit of using CSII therapy and CGM. Parents agreed that diabetes technology can allow preventing nocturnal and exercise‐induced hypoglycaemia in children with T1DM and reminding parents to early warn the sign of hypoglycaemia (Garza et al., 2018; Gildersleeve et al., 2017; Rashotte et al., 2014). This significantly decreased parents' fear of their child's hypoglycaemia and sub‐optimal glycaemic control. Nevertheless, for the use of diabetes technology equipment to control glucose levels in children, parents also questioned the reliability of the information provided by the device and the accuracy of its operation may influence the optimal glycaemic control in children (Barnard et al., 2014; Lawton et al., 2019). The accuracy of the CGM depends on the quality of the calibration and that the CGM does not always display accurate information (Barnard et al., 2014). The extracts below illustrate this:

  • " I’m absolutely not gonna let him out of my sight. I suppose its trust, you know, it's not just a broken leg. It's his life. And it's delivering deadly insulin into my son" (Lawton et al., 2019:122).

  • “It wasn't the most reliable system…Your blood glucose levels did not always what they said it was” (Barnard et al., 2014: e00025).

  • “I didn't really trust it because I think for a whole day I was using it, it didn't give any insulin” (Barnard et al., 2014: e00025).

Theme 3: Improved quality of life for parents of children with T1DM

3.3

Using advanced diabetes techniques can improve the quality of life for parents of children with T1DM. CSII therapy provides an active glycaemic control regimen to children with T1DM, and CGM affords children with a sense of security that would not have been possible without continuous monitoring. This theme includes improved sleep quality, felt safe and less anxious, and benefits of reminders and monitoring. Many parents have mentioned the sleep issue in studies, which was their quality of nocturnal sleep, had improved after using diabetes technology such as a closed‐loop system, and an automated insulin delivery system (Barnard et al., 2014; Garza et al., 2018).

  • "I had better sleep, you don't worry about what you have to do” (Barnard et al., 2014: e000025).

  • “Every night we could sleep” (Barnard et al., 2014: e000025).

  • " Perhaps it can bring back some spontaneity and not having to think ahead and too much planning and organizing and more freedom, I think, and sleep at night" (Garza et al., 2018:225).

Furthermore, advanced diabetes technology provides parents of children with T1DM with a sense of safety and reduced parental "nagging" and interference, and fear of unstable glucose levels in children (Barnard et al., 2014; Bomba et al., 2018; Garza et al., 2018; Lawton et al., 2019). Overall, child safety is an important benefit for parents using these advanced diabetes devices.

Providing real‐time continuous monitoring of glucose in children with T1DM and reminding parents of signs of elevated or decreased glucose are the benefits of advanced diabetes technology mentioned by parents in all included studies (Barnard et al. 2014; Bomba et al., 2018; Garza et al., 2018; Gildersleeve et al., 2017; Lawton et al., 2018, 2019, 2021; Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016). Real‐time monitoring can solve the common problem of parental fear of nocturnal hypoglycaemia in their children. Moreover, parents specifically mentioned that they were interested in remote monitoring and hoped to master the ability to remotely bolus insulin (Gildersleeve et al., 2017).

  • "Remote monitoring could be available by website, phone application, and/or as a health overview on the care providers' mobile phone lock screen" (Gildersleeve et al., 2017:670).

  • “if it sends me an alert saying she has an urgent low soon *low glucose alarm, I look at on the phone and see how quick it's coming down and, often it will be enough just to, you know, her basal will have been knocked out by the [closedloop] for a while, so that might be enough for it to just start coming back up, without needing to do anything. So I can watch and wait.” (Lawton et al., 2021:11).

The combination of CSII and CGM enables parents to feel more comfortable with their child's independence at school or at a friend's home. Children with T1DM are particularly independent and autonomous, and these advanced diabetes devices can enhance their autonomy. Overall, CSII therapy and CGM can help parents feel confident about blood glucose monitoring and insulin regulation.

Theme 4: Burden of living with insulin pumps and CGM

3.4

Although the insulin pump and CGM bring advantages and help to parents in managing T1DM in their daily life, parents still reflected that they did experience some non‐physical burdens associated with using these diabetes technologies. Participants significantly indicated that their psychological burden, stress and worry may be exacerbated while using these advanced diabetes technologies to take care of their children with T1DM for a long time (Bomba et al., 2018; Rashotte et al., 2014). Some parents think that their worries about children's dietary intake cannot be avoided although using the closed‐loop system and even affect sleep (Lawton et al., 2018). One parent described the overwhelming nature of these devices:

  • "Like any technology, it is one more added thing. It is one more thing he has to insert, so now he has two things connected to him. It is one more thing we have to buy. It is one more thing we have to trust to help us manage his diabetes. It is one more thing to talk about. It is one more thing to troubleshoot. It is just one more thing that you have to learn to incorporate into everything else that goes on in a family from the time you wake up in the morning to showers, to catching the bus, little things that people do every day without having to add this to it, so it is added discussion in the family, to the management of his diabetes” (Rashotte et al., 2014:259).

  • "Pizza is a nightmare…pizza and popcorn night, no sleep for us…So there is a rule, pizza is either Friday night or Saturday night or even Sunday lunchtime, but never on weeknights, cause it sends his sugars high" (Lawton et al., 2018:756).

This psychological and emotional burden is a constant reminder for parents and their children with T1DM to be aware of diabetes.

Parents in one study pointed out that unhealthy eating can be developed because of the use of the closed‐loop system (Lawton et al., 2018). The extracts below demonstrate this:

  • "He might have been more complacent on the snacks knowing that the system responds… But now with the confidence in the closed‐loop system, I think he probably just [feels] relaxed in getting snacks wherever he can get them from. So his alertness of what to eat and when might have quite relaxed as well, because of that rebut given by the new system" (Lawton et al., 2018:757)

The cost and insurance also are one of the living burdens to parents with the combination of using insulin pumps and CGM (Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016). Adolescents and children with T1DM may worry about losing expensive equipment, whereas parents worry about the cost of long‐term use of CSII therapy and CGM, as well as their child's suboptimal glycaemic control (Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016). Finally, concerns about the cost affect parents' experiences and perceptions of diabetes technology on multiple levels, which add burden to expensive diabetes care as well.

Theme 5: Impact on the parent–child relationship

3.5

The fifth theme is the impact of advanced diabetes technologies on the relationship between parents and their children with T1DM. Parents' relationship with their children with T1DM may change due to the use of CSII therapy and CGM. These impacts may benefit to the relationship between parents and their children in their life to manage T1DM, such as reducing parents' stress to take care of their children with T1DM (Garza et al., 2018; Lawton et al., 2019; Rashotte et al., 2014), giving the guiding effects and establishing partnership relationship between parents and children.

A parent of an automated insulin delivery systems user articulated this by saying.

  • “I try to be very sympathetic. I'd like to say I am, but there are those times where I will come home in the afternoon from work, and she'll be in bed. And she'll say “‘What time is it?” And I'll say “its 5:30?” And she'll say “Oh my God, I've been sleeping for two and a half hours.” That will get me annoyed. And I won't say anything to her, but internally it will bother me and I try to be caring. We'll have to take the kids somewhere, and she'll say “I can't drive right now, I'm feeling shaky.” And I'll have to take the kids somewhere. So, I think that it would certainly help our relationship.” (Garza et al., 2018:225).

In addition, parents have also noticed an improvement in their relationship with their children because they no longer need to constantly remind them to perform self‐management tasks, such as testing blood glucose (Lawton et al., 2019). It is significant that the relationship reaches a state of interdependence when children realize they have more control over their diabetes management and feel their parents' trust and support (Rashotte et al., 2014). However, these advanced diabetes devices may benefit the relationship between a child with diabetes and their parents, but not always (Rashotte et al., 2014). The usage of the combination of CSII therapy and SCG also brought some negative effects on the family relationship (Bomba et al., 2018; Rashotte et al., 2014). Overprotective and monitoring behaviours by parents include frequently asking their children for CGM readings, asking them "what are they doing wrong," or making them feel that CGM is "monitoring" their daily life (Lawton et al., 2021; Rashotte et al., 2014). the parent–child relationship stress caused by CSII therapy and CGM may result from the decrease of transparency and loss of privacy in children's daily life, which may lead to family conflicts (Bomba et al., 2018). One parent describes that:

  • “I was messaging him saying I see you're going low, make sure you put some carbs in… So of course, that really annoyed him, because he was a bit like, ‘why are you doing this? I know, I know, you don't need to send me a message to remind me’.” (Lawton et al., 2021).

This problem is extremely serious in families that have school‐age children with T1DM. Therefore, parents should be aware of the problem and deal with it in an appropriate way. The combined use of CSII therapy and CGM may introduce new and complex dynamic relationships between children with T1DM and their parents. In the future education programmes of using the combination of CSII therapy and CGM, parents should learn how to deal with this challenge.

Theme 6: Requirement and expectation to the use of advanced diabetes technologies

3.6

Some parents made advice and expectation for advanced diabetes technological devices (Bomba et al., 2018; Gildersleeve et al., 2017). Some parents think that the combination insulin pump and the CGM device did not perform either, such as having experienced fewer hypoglycaemia events. Thus, they hope that the advanced technology can promote more stringent glycaemic targets, or it may be necessary to make more use of specific features of the technology such as temporary base rates (Gildersleeve et al., 2017). Excepting to improve children's glycaemic control and Hb1Ac levels, they desire to achieve normal values without affecting the normal lifestyle of their children, wish to get the health insurance of these devices and different requirements on these devices (Bomba et al., 2018). They hope to take better care of their children by showing these demands and expectations.

Some of the suggestions for AP modifications from parents and caregivers have two main themes: (a) creating a modified flexible AP system to help children take more responsibility for their diabetes management; (b) Create a system that facilitates the participation and communication of different care providers (Gildersleeve et al., 2017). Parents also support AP system modifications to incentivize children to participate in positive diabetes management activities, such as the animation of the CGM trace and a points system (Gildersleeve et al., 2017). The realistic expectations of parents and caregivers of children with T1DM are the AP has the potential to alleviate parental and child stress related to diabetes.

DISCUSSION

4

This qualitative meta‐synthesis fills the gaps in the existing literature by synthesizing the qualitative experiences and perspectives of 184 parents on advanced diabetes technologies. These results show the inevitably links with the device itself, parents' emotional and psychological, family relationships, and the economic consequences. Current studies have indicated that whatever the use of CSII therapy, CGM or the combination of them, the influences of using advanced diabetes technologies on children with T1DM as well as their parents/caregivers will associate with frequent alarms, calibration issues and carrying more equipment (Gonder‐Frederick et al., 2016; Schaeffer, 2013). Parents reflect that alarms were annoying and easy to disturb the quality of sleep at night among parents and their children (Bomba et al., 2018; Gildersleeve et al., 2017). Young children will fear alarms so that they were afraid to sleep alone that may cause a temporary regression in personal development, which will increase worry on parents. This is the reason why some parents in the study suggested that these devices should have default alarm settings to limit the number and severity of high alarms and accompany with pictorial instructions (Gildersleeve et al., 2017). Thus, alarm settings can be personalized which can provide truly useful alerts and reduce hassle and strike a balance between device security and human availability (Shivers et al., 2013). This can help children and other care providers make treatment decisions, and to ease the anxiety of parents and school staff caused by alarms.

Moreover, CGM and CSII therapy have gradually developed to be more accurate and consistent in calibration, so signal‐processing‐induced time delays have been reduced and the performance of hypoglycaemia has been improved (Garcia et al., 2013). However, device issues such as calibration and troubleshooting may increase parents' nocturnal vigilance and affect the quality of sleep. Problems of equipment troubleshooting were also associated with calibration issues. Parents' reactions to calibration issues contain the difficulty of calibration, frequent calibration and hard to programme a CGM in this systematic review, especially at night (Barnard et al., 2014; Bomba et al., 2018; Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016). Even without these problems, devices such as CGM and insulin pumps can be high‐maintenance equipment, which may bring parents an extra burden (such as the short battery life during calibration) and pay in addition to caring for their children with T1DM and limit ongoing engagement. Therefore, clinical practitioners can use these integrated themes to set realistic goals and expectations for long‐term use, in order to support them about how to deal with relevant problems, such as troubleshooting and expectation setting.

The themes in terms of glycaemic information and the impacts on the quality of life of parents and children with T1DM can be used to maximize parents' experiences with using diabetes technologies. Advanced technology can monitor the change of glucose and automatically adjust insulin dose based on carbohydrate counting so that they can correct and protect children from prolonged hyperglycaemia if children forget to administer insulin before meals (Garza et al., 2018; Lawton et al., ,2018, 2021; Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016). CGM is helpful in continuously detecting high or low glucose levels, combining with CSII therapy can help to understand problematic trends and make the adjustment to the insulin regimen, and reduce caring burden of parents (Haidar et al., 2015). Parents become confident to participate in the diabetic management with their children. Nevertheless, the burden from economic cost and parents' psychology seems to have impacts to parents and their children with T1DM while accompanying the use of these advanced diabetes devices. Within the theme of cost, two of the included studies presented parental worry on the issue of cost management and insurance of the sensors, insulin pumps or CGM (Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016). Although cost is only a sub‐theme in this analysis, it warrants universal consideration. According to a research of the cost and reimbursement for CGM showed a high difference across the world, some countries provide more extensive payer coverage in private and governmental sectors (such as Switzerland, Germany and the USA), whereas others are substantially more limited or absent (such as France, Slovenia and Spain) (Graham, 2017). Another research demonstrated that the people from higher socioeconomic status were more possible to obtain CSII therapy compared to disadvantaged and minority families (Lin et al., 2013). While parents' experiences of these diabetes devices do not change due to the financial burden, the availability of treatment and the possibility of continuing treatment affect the overall psychology of parents and their children with T1DM and their quality of life. Therefore, even though all participants within the included studies come from the developed countries, low‐income family need to concern about the economic burden while using these advanced diabetes technologies. Moreover, parents worry devices are difficult to adapt at the beginning and make their child feeling pain and discomfort (Bomba et al., 2018). Their concern overwhelmed from the abundance of uploading data and constant vigilance would make them lose control of their children's care with using devices (Rashotte et al., 2014). It follows that even though CSII therapy and CGM, can help parents to more understand the situation of blood glucose of their children, it adds a relentless and constant reminder of diabetes to parents and affects their quality of life.

Furthermore, this qualitative synthesis also highlights important gaps in knowledge. Further qualitative research has explored parent–child relationships to determine how to strengthen partnerships between parents and children while using CSII therapy and CGM (Wong et al., 2015). In this review, Lawton et al. (2019) and Garza et al. (2018) described that CGM can decrease conflicts around daily self‐care tasks by providing constant monitoring and accessibility of blood glucose information so that parents did not have to remind their children to undertake self‐management task constantly. Children and adolescents wear AP system device and their parents reported greater adherence to diabetes management and less diabetes‐specific family conflict (Telo et al., 2015). Notably, unceasing burden of diabetes management also cause family conflicts and even depress children and adolescents with T1DM (Wong et al., 2015). Children believe that parents' over‐active use of the sensor to track their daily behaviour that will make them feel parents are deliberately monitoring (Bomba et al., 2018; Lawton et al., 2021; Rashotte et al., 2014). The issue of privacy and "transparency" in children may put a strain on the parent–child relationship and effect parent–children relationships. If parents let their control and vigilance go of, children would feel their parents' trust and support. Thus, there will be a shared responsibility between parents and children. Therefore, while the relationship with parents reached a state of interdependent partnership, parents and children can control diabetes co‐operatively much better. There are some qualitative data of the impact of CGM on parent–child relationships in this systematic review, but few qualitative data on CSII therapy. Hence, these are interesting areas of further studies.

Qualitative research adopts a holistic human‐centred research method and qualitative meta‐synthesis has strengths as rigorous systematic work adhering to qualitative principles (Goins et al., 2015). Thus, it is beneficial to this review to answer the review question of this systematic review. This can be an advantage of qualitative research, but it is easy to neglect participants' perspectives within the context of their environment (Boccia et al., 2015). The themes adopted here combine the clinical and basic research expertise of each team member. However, during the meta‐synthesis, there is no clear statement and discussion and exploration of parents' experiences on the new intervention (children using CSII therapy and CGM) and the comparator (children using MDI), which led to part of themes being not derived in an inductive way (Barnard et al., 2014; Lawton et al., 2018, 2021; Rashotte et al., 2014; Wysocki et al., 2016; Bomba et al., 2018; Garza et al., 2018). Interestingly, similar thematic analyses have also been found after synthesizing, such as safety in older adults with T1DM (Litchman & Allen, 2017), barriers to effective management of type 2 diabetes in primary care (Rushforth et al., 2016), and woman's perceptions and knowledge of postpartum gestational diabetes care (Van Ryswyk et al., 2015). This demonstrates the topic of transferability and meaningful sharing of experiences across different populations. Thus, the results of this study should consider the context of its limitations and reference to other existing literature during the process of themes synthesis. Despite the challenges of using technology to manage T1DM, overall, users' satisfaction and benefits were high, which is consistent with this review. Because of its apparent benefits, an increasing number of parents are raising new hopes for "AP system," such as gamification or better management of diabetes (Bomba et al., 2018; Gildersleeve et al., 2017). Although existing systems are a step towards automation of diabetes control, the gap between "ideal" device systems will require future improvements in diabetes technology to meet the various user needs listed above (Brew‐Sam et al., 2021).

This review has shown that there were challenges for parents to use advanced diabetes technology to manage T1DM in children. Participants from diverse ethnic and socioeconomic backgrounds also include in the co‐design process to advance T1DM technology in this review. The discussions of use and sharing of data that from different participants can provides a foundation on which relevant research in the future. In China, nurses provide medical care and psychological support to patients and their family, hoping to improve their life quality. This review can serve as a reference because it reflects the positive and negative effects of using advanced diabetes technology on the physical, emotional and relational aspects of life to parents and their children with T1DM. Therefore, healthcare professionals can provide nutritional and behavioural support to parents to ensure full implementation and continued use of the technology for clinical and reduce the life and psychological burden on parents and their children. Future research also enables us to place emphasis on the experience of parents and children with T1DM on the comparison between MDI therapy and the combination of CSII therapy and CGM. By drawing on the comparison of parents' perspective and experiences, we can identify ways to further improve the technology and enables us to realize the increasing number of individuals with T1DM and their family's expectations of diabetes technology.

LIMITATIONS

5

Beside parents' experiences and attitudes, some included studies also involved adults and other population. It was not possible to exclude these data from our analysis, and at times, these have been included in our analysis. We did not examine the grey literature and thus, we might have loss part of experiential data. We only examined studies reported in English, which excludes analysis of experiences in non‐English‐speaking countries and non‐English‐speaking participants' experiences in using advanced diabetes technology. Because of the rapid evolution of diabetes technology, there are challenges in evaluating extensive parents' experiences with a particular device.

CONCLUSION

6

Overall, the advanced diabetes technology which is combining the use of CSII therapy and CGM can bring significant different experiences to parents of children with T1DM. It is common to find that advanced diabetes technology has positive impacts in disease management for both children with T1DM and parents. However, parents may undertake and navigate a variety of additional physical and emotional challenges, such as cost, the size and visibility of devices, and the intrusiveness of alarms, which would attract the attention of Chinese researchers, users and their parents and carers to this fact. The reason for the continued use of diabetes technology is that its benefits outweigh the challenges. This can not only provide adequate support to Chinese parents who are using or going to use the AP system and closed‐loop system to manage T1DM in their children but also help Healthcare professionals support diabetes regimens to parents and their children with T1DM.

## INTRODUCTION

The incidence of Type 1 diabetes (T1DM) has increased significantly in many countries worldwide (Manuwald et al., 2017). Although there was a lower incidence of T1DM in Chinese children, the incidence amongst children under 15 has been rising dramatically over the past two decades (Weng et al., 2018). It is estimated that there are over 4,000 new cases of T1DM in children annually in China (Weng et al., 2018). Without appropriate insulin treatment and optimal glycaemic control, the condition of children with T1DM can exacerbate rapidly within 24 hr, resulting in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), permanent disability or even death (Cavan, 2018). Optimal diabetes management are important for children and adolescents with T1DM.

Taking care of children with T1DM who are receiving diabetes management can be challenging and requires care coordination between parents and children (Shulman & Daneman, 2010). In China, children with T1DM have been identified as a difficult age group to treat (Guo et al., 2013). Parents need to handle a diverse variety of problems related to diabetes management of their children, including adjustment of insulin use according to blood glucose level, nutritional and exercise control, prevention of complications and attention of children' emotional health (ADA, 2018; Lu et al., 2020). The unpredictability of diabetes affects children and requires parents to maintain vigilance, careful management and frequently make parents with psychological stress (Rifshana et al., 2017). Therefore, care‐giving burden of children with T1DM was significantly negatively associated with Chinese parents’ quality of life, and affects the quality of daily life of parents (Luo et al., 2021). It is particularly important to help children with T1DM stabilizing blood glucose through more convenient treatment.

To achieve optimal blood glucose control, blood glucose monitoring and insulin delivery are very important (Giuseppe et al., 2018; Pickup et al., 2015). Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) were successively introduced for people with T1DM, which can achieve continuous injection of exogenous insulin through an infusion pump and 24‐hr real‐time blood glucose monitoring (Forlenza et al., 2016). Such treatment is necessary to avoid complications, such as DKA, diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy (Atkinson et al., 2014; Ziegler et al., 2011). In 2006, the Artificial Pancreas project began (Forlenza et al., 2016). AP (artificial pancreas) therapy, SAP (sensor‐augmented pump) and automated insulin delivery systems also known as the closed‐loop diabetes system, combining CSII therapy with CGM, and adding a control algorithm insulin in a glucose‐responsive manner (Forlenza et al., 2016; Hovorka, 2015). Compared with single CSII therapy or CGM, the closed‐loop system can automatically adjust the amount of insulin that enters the body based on glucose sensor levels to optimize blood glucose on people with T1DM (Bekiari et al., 2018). Its clinical benefits have been evaluated in several studies and the optimistic results have been shown (ADA, 2018; Karageorgiou et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2017).

The closed‐loop diabetes system is now commercially available in some foreign countries (Boughton & Hovorka, 2021; Galderisi et al., 2017). However, some studies have found that the experience and views of using CSII therapy and CGM is mixed which might be related to the accuracy, frequent annoyances from alarms, insertion pain and the interruption to the daily life of users (Brew‐Sam et al., 2021; Messer et al., 2018). Notably, such advanced diabetes technology is only used in clinical research in China (Cai et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2020). Thus, most Chinese studies focus on the effectiveness and safety of the combined use of CSII therapy and CGM in adult population, also including some children and adolescents (Dai et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2020). Few Chinese findings describe the experiences on the combination of CSII therapy and CGM among people with T1DM and their parents or caregivers.

As these new features of CSII therapy and CGM mature, there will be more and more Chinese parents or caregivers need to choose treatment methods according to their children's needs. The major of diabetes technology developments in current and future also needs to concern about the breadth experiences and perspectives of parents or caregivers and their children with T1DM, and how to reduce their living burden. One way to understand these perspectives is to synthesize the existing qualitative literature related to parents’ experiences on advanced diabetes technology. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to explore the experiences and perspectives of the combined use of CSII therapy and CGM on parents of children with TIDM on their daily life, in order to support other new parents who plan to use CSII therapy and CGM to manage T1DM of their children in the future and design appropriate clinical interventions for adherence.
## METHODS

This systematic review is a meta‐synthesis of the qualitative existing literature, including a literature search, process of quality appraisal, themes synthesis and reciprocal translation (Goins et al.,2015; McSharry et al., 2016), analysing topics according to established standards to solve problems (Moher et al., 2009). This review used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review (PRISMA) flow chart as a guideline for searching and selecting all qualitative studies, in order to establish an acceptable review structure and avoid bias (Moher et al., 2009).

### Data sources and search strategy

The date of the literature search in the database was conducted from 2006–2021. This period can reflect the increased use with the combination of SCII therapy and CGM among children and teenagers with T1DM in the clinical field, and their commercial availability. The following electronic databases were used to search: CINAHL, MEDLINE, PubMed, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Cochrane Library and Scopus. The main search keywords included “parents,” “children with type 1 diabetes,” “insulin pump,” “CGM,” “artificial pancreas,” “closed‐loop control” and “experience." Other search terms included “mothers’ perspective,” “fathers’ views,” “CSII,” “continuous glucose monitoring,” “glycaemic control” and “advanced technology.” Publications only included studies published in the English language. Articles in the reference list were searched manually to identify any other potential studies that are suitable and relevant to the inclusion criteria. The search strategy is presented in Appendix 1. The search process is detailed in Figure 1.

### Literature selection

Studies were selected for inclusion by the first author if they contained original qualitative or mixed‐method research related to parents' experiences with using the combination of SCII and CGM to manage T1DM among their children were considered. Studies that only explore adults, teenagers and healthcare professionals' experiences are not included. Critical articles, editorials and grey literature are excluded from the final yield. All included studies described children with T1DM who used portable subcutaneous insulin pumps and CGM in their daily life (including sensor‐augmented pump, artificial pancreas systems and automated insulin delivery system). Studies only used one of SCII therapy or CGM were excluded. Data unrelated to CGM, CSII, artificial pancreas functionality or data with ambiguous topics were not analysed. ### Assessment of methodological quality

CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme) aims to help people develop the necessary skills to make sense of scientific evidence, and has produced appraisal checklists covering validity, results and relevance. Ten questions of the CASP checklist were used by the review author of this systematic review to assess the quality of eight qualitative research and the qualitative elements of one mixed‐method studies in this systematic review (CASP International Network, 2013). An appropriate scoring system provided by Butler et al. (2016) was used to evaluate the quality of included studies related to study design, qualitative methodology, sampling, data collection, data analysis and rigour. The articles were categorized into three categories of high‐, moderate‐ and low‐quality in terms of methods (cut‐off scores: high‐quality study [achieved score≥9 points], moderate‐quality [7 points < achieved score < 8.5 points] and the low‐quality study [achieved score ≤7 points]). Both researchers independently scored each part of the checklist. If the answer to each question in the CASP checklist is "yes," the score is 1. If the answer is "no," the score is 0 and if the answer is “uncertain,” the score is 0.5. Different scores were discussed in one item by two researchers until an agreement was reached. As a result, there are eight high‐quality studies and one moderate‐quality study within the included qualitative studies. The quality evaluation results of one mixed‐method studies are one high‐quality level. All included studies regardless of quality that were included in this review, with the expectation that the moderate‐quality studies may still make valuable contributions to the synthesis (Boland et al., 2017; Campbell et al., 2018). ### Data extraction and synthesis

In this systematic review, a structured meta‐synthesis approach was used to synthesize original qualitative data of included studies, which is called interpretive thematic integration, including extraction of important information from the research, developing the description of data‐driven themes and then synthesize themes through a process called reciprocal translation (Noblit & Hare, 1988; Thomas & Harden, 2008). First, each included study was analysed by the review author to determine research objectives, study setting, study design, research method, participants and results, and then the thematic contents of those studies were evaluated again to confirm each theme (Korhonen et al., 2013; Sandelowski, 2006). In this process, the entire qualitative content was regarded as data in each qualitative included study. Participant's quotations and authors' explanations will also be included in the qualitative content. The first and second author list topics related to the combined use of CSII therapy and CGM by parents' experiences for managing T1DM children. These articles were read to achieve a consensus on the proposed theme. This process was repeated many times. We carefully selected research topics that included CSII therapy and CGM, and included them only when our clinical judgement indicated that the topic was relevant to both.

The theme in each included study was indexed in the form of a table and create a comprehensive picture of common concept. Individual specific themes in this synthesis was classified and interpreted according to the concepts of those studies. According to the principle of Thomas and Harden (2008) and Noblit and Hare (1988), the process of "translating" synthesis concepts from one study to another is used to synthesize qualitative data across studies. The newly derived themes were aggregated into the final reciprocal translation table and reviewed by the authors' team and agreed upon. The final synthesis can provide new analytical themes and concepts about attitudes and views of parents on the combined use of CSII therapy and CGM to manage T1DM with their children. The data were extracted using the data extraction tool. Based on the data in each included study, the extraction tool was designed by the review author team (Butler et al., 2016).
