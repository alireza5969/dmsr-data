Adjunct therapy at time of exam-indicated cervical cerclage in singleton pregnancies: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Ann M. Bruno[1][2]

Ashley E. Benson[1][2]

Torri D. Metz[1][2]

Nathan R. Blue[1][2]

[1] Department of Obstetrics & Gynecology, University of Utah Health, Salt Lake City, UT;

[2] Department of Obstetrics & Gynecology, Intermountain Healthcare, Murray, UT

Author notes:

Correspondence to: Corresponding Author: Ann Bruno, Department of Obstetrics & Gynecology, University of Utah Health, 30 N 1900 E, Suite 2B200; Salt Lake City, Utah 84132, Phone: (801) 581-8425/ (573)268-2339, ann.bruno@hsc.utah.edu

Abstract

Objective:

Physical exam-indicated cerclage for cervical insufficiency prolongs gestation, but evidence on the addition of adjuncts to further prolong latency is limited. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to compare gestational latency between those who did and did not receive adjunct antibiotic or tocolytic therapy at the time of exam-indicated cerclage.

Study Design:

Electronic databases (1966 to 2020) were searched for randomized controlled trials (RCT) and cohort studies comparing adjunct antibiotic or tocolytic use versus non-use at time of exam-indicated cerclage, defined as placement for cervical dilation ≥ 1 cm, in a current singleton pregnancy. Studies including individuals with intra-amniotic infection, cerclage in place, non-viable gestation, or ruptured membranes were excluded. The primary outcome was latency from cerclage placement to delivery. Secondary outcomes included preterm birth, preterm premature rupture of membranes, birth weight and neonatal survival. Risk of bias was assessed using standardized tools. Heterogeneity was assessed using χ2 and I2 tests. Results were pooled and analyzed using a random-effects model. PROSPERO registration (CRD42021216370).

Results:

Of 923 unique records, 163 were reviewed in full. Three met inclusion criteria: one RCT and two retrospective cohorts. The included RCT (N=50) and one cohort (N=142) compared outcomes with and without adjunct use of antibiotic and tocolytic, while the second cohort (N=150) compared outcomes with and without adjunct tocolytic, with a sub-population also receiving antibiotics. The RCT was nested within one of the cohorts, and therefore only one of these two studies was utilized for any given outcome to eliminate counting individuals twice. Risk of bias was “critical” for one cohort study, “moderate” for the other cohort study, and “some concerns” for the RCT. Gestational latency could not be pooled and meta-analyzed. Adjunct tocolytic-antibiotic therapy was not associated with a decrease in risk of preterm delivery <28 weeks (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.65–1.26; χ2=0.0, I2=0.0%) or neonatal survival to discharge (RR 1.11, 95% CI 0.91–1.35; χ2=0.05, I2=0.0%).

Conclusion:

There is not enough evidence to robustly evaluate the use of adjunct tocolytics or antibiotics at time of exam-indicated cerclage to prolong latency.

Introduction

Cervical insufficiency (CI), or the process of painless cervical dilation, results in second trimester pregnancy loss or preterm delivery with its associated neonatal morbidity and mortality.1,2 Transvaginal placement of a non-absorbable suture around the cervix is used to maintain the physical integrity and closure of the cervix in a variety of clinical scenarios.1,3–5 Physical exam-indicated cerclage, also known as “rescue” or “emergency” cerclage, is used at the time of identification of CI in an ongoing pregnancy.6–9 Placement of exam-indicated cerclage prolongs gestation, but preterm delivery and its subsequent perinatal morbidity remain frequent in this population.10–12

Tocolytics for uterine quiescence and antibiotics for prophylaxis against intra-amniotic infection are adjunctive therapies at the time of exam-indicated cerclage, which may further prolong gestation. However, available data are limited to support these interventions and clinical practice remains varied and dependent on practitioner preferences.13,14 The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) does not recommend the routine use of adjunct antibiotics or tocolytics at the time of exam-indicated cerclage due to a paucity of data.1,13,15 Such adjuncts, if confirmed to prolong latency, reduce preterm birth, or improve perinatal morbidity and mortality, would likely be adopted. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have supported the use of exam-indicated cerclage but have not directly addressed adjunct therapies.10,16

The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to compare gestational latency between those who did and did not receive adjunct antibiotic or tocolytic therapy at the time of exam-indicated cerclage in singleton pregnancies. Additionally, we aimed to evaluate differences in preterm birth, preterm premature rupture of membranes, birth weight and neonatal survival between those receiving versus not receiving adjunct therapies.

Methods

We developed an a priori protocol outlining the study selection parameters, search strategy, and planned analysis. The study was prospectively registered on PROSPERO before our search was executed (#CRD42021216370).

We searched Medline and Embase from inception in 1966 through September 2020 using keyword and MeSH headings for the concepts of “cerclage” and “antibiotics” or “tocolytics” in various combinations and permutations. The concept terms were exploded to encompass the variety of subheadings for various antibiotic and tocolytic generic and brand names. Full search terms are available in the supplementary appendix (Appendix 1). We searched for unpublished studies in The Cochrane Controlled Trials Register, Clinicaltrials.gov, and Scopus conference abstracts. There were no date or language restrictions applied. Searches were limited to human studies. To improve the sensitivity of the search, a series of validated filters for randomized controlled trials (RCTs), observational studies, and meta-analyses were included.17–19 References from relevant reviews were evaluated for applicable citations. The last search date was September 28, 2020.

All search results were merged and duplicates removed. Initial title and abstract review were completed to eliminate irrelevant studies. The full texts of the remaining studies were obtained. Full-text review was completed assessing studies against inclusion and exclusion criteria. We included RCTs and cohort studies comparing adjunct use of tocolytics or antibiotics at time of exam-indicated cerclage versus non-use in individuals with singleton pregnancies and cervical insufficiency. Cervical insufficiency was defined by physical examination findings of 1 cm or more of cervical dilation between 16w0d and 24w0d. Adjunct tocolytic or antibiotic therapy was defined as any tocolytic or antibiotic described as used within 72 hours of the procedure. Case reports, case series, review articles, and commentaries were excluded. Studies were also excluded if the population studied included individuals with intra-amniotic infection, cerclage already in place, non-viable gestation, multiple gestation or ruptured membranes. Two reviewers (A.M.B., N.R.B.) independently reviewed all records for relevance as well as performed full-text reviews to assess for inclusion. No attempts were made to contact study authors.

A data collection form was piloted based on planned variables, revised and then used for extraction. Two reviewers (A.M.B., N.R.B) independently reviewed each eligible study and extracted data. Descriptive data collected for each study included author, publication year, study dates and location, participant characteristics, cervical cerclage technique, sample size, and details of the intervention with tocolytics and antibiotics. Extracted data on the specified outcomes included gestational age latency (in days), preterm birth < 28 weeks’ gestation, preterm premature rupture of membranes, neonatal birth weight, and neonatal survival to hospital discharge. The duplicate extracted results were compared and any discrepancies amongst the two reviewers for study selection and inclusion, or data extraction, were resolved by discussion with final adjudication by a third reviewer if necessary.

All included studies were assessed for risk of bias using either the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for RCTs, or the Cochrane Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for observational studies.20,21 These are validated tools that provide assessment into three (low risk, some concern, high risk) or five (low risk, moderate risk, serious risk, critical risk, or no information) risk categories, respectively. Two reviewers (A.E.B., N.R.B.) completed the quality assessment of each study, results were compared, and discrepancies adjudicated by discussion.

The primary outcome for this review was gestational age latency. As a continuous variable, this was defined as the interval in days between placement of cerclage and delivery. We also planned to assess gestational age latency as a dichotomous variable using latency greater than 28 days. Secondary outcomes included preterm birth at less than 28 weeks, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), neonatal birth weight, and neonatal survival to hospital discharge.

Study characteristics were summarized, and clinical heterogeneity reported. If there were at least two studies with data for a given outcome, data were pooled. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the χ2 test (Cochrane Q statistic) and the magnitude of heterogeneity was quantified using I2.22 For dichotomous outcomes, we planned to calculate the relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the Mantel-Haenszel fixed-effect method if no evidence of significant heterogeneity was present.23 If heterogeneity was identified, a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model was used instead.24 For continuous variables, the mean difference (MD), and if applicable, the standardized mean difference (SMD), between groups was calculated. If 10 or more studies were identified, we planned to use Egger’s test to evaluate for publication bias with presentation using a funnel plot.25 We planned a sensitivity analysis to assess the effect of studies considered at ‘low risk’ versus ‘high risk’ of bias. We planned for subgroup analysis to assess individually the adjunct use of either only antibiotics or only tocolytics if there was more than one study per group with these specifications.

Studies were combined and reviewed using Covidence (Melbourne, Australia). Data were analyzed using STATA 15.1 (College Station, Texas). This study was exempt from Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval as a meta-analysis of de-identified, published results. This manuscript follows PRISMA recommendations for reporting of meta-analyses.26

Results

Our initial search yielded 923 records. Following title and abstract screening, 163 full-text studies were reviewed for eligibility (Figure 1). The majority of full-text articles were excluded secondary to incorrect comparison groups, incorrect intervention, or study inclusion of the incorrect population (i.e., not patients undergoing exam indicated cerclage). No additional studies were identified from reference review. Three studies met inclusion criteria for qualitative and quantitative synthesis.27–29 The included studies were one RCT (n=50) and two retrospective cohorts (N= 150, N=142). Selected characteristics of the included studies are described in Table 1.

The included RCT was nested within one of the two retrospective cohort studies.27,28 In order to eliminate multiple encounters of the same data for analysis, only one of these two studies was included for quantitative synthesis via meta-analysis for a given outcome. The RCT was preferentially selected as a higher level of evidence when data were available from both studies.

The Miller et al RCT27 and Premkumar et al28 retrospective cohort were completed at the same single academic institution and both designed to assess the effect of adjunct indomethacin and antibiotics given at the time of exam-indicated cerclage. The clinical approach to exam-indicated cerclage was consistent in both studies and included a McDonald surgical technique using 5mm mersilene tape under regional anesthesia.4 Both studies also reported clinical practice of sterile saline preparation with membrane reduction using either a foley balloon or moistened sponge. Amniocentesis was not routinely performed before the procedure.27,28 The Berghella et al29 retrospective cohort was designed to assess the effect of indomethacin use on outcomes among women with a dilated cervix between 14–25 weeks and included women from 10 medical centers, whether or not they underwent a cerclage (N=222). Our analysis only included the sub-population of individuals who underwent exam-indicated cerclage (n=150). Surgical technique and suture type, pre-procedure amniocentesis to exclude subclinical chorioamnionitis, adjunct antibiotic and bed rest were at practitioner discretion with considerable potential clinical heterogeneity. Adjunct antibiotic was used in 65.8% of those receiving indomethacin, but also in 52.3% of those not receiving indomethacin.29 Across all three studies, individuals were excluded for chorioamnionitis, labor, PPROM, or vaginal bleeding.27–29

In critical appraisal using the ROBINS-I tool for the two observational studies, one demonstrated moderate risk28 for bias while the other was classified as critical risk.29 The overall critical risk assignment resulted from a critical risk within the domain of confounding based on unreported multivariable or adjusted modeling for the outcomes of the sub-population we included. Appraisal of the RCT using the RoB-2 for trials demonstrated “some concerns.”27

For the primary outcome, gestational age latency, results were available from the RCT and the observational study containing the nested RCT.27,28 Therefore, results could not be pooled for this outcome. In the Miller et al RCT, gestational age latency was not prolonged with adjunct therapies (median 97 days [IQR 57–125] with adjuncts vs 80 days [IQR 15–122] without adjuncts, p=0.18).27

Both observational studies reported on preterm birth at less than 28 weeks so results were pooled for meta-analysis.28,29 There was no evidence of statistical heterogeneity (χ2=0.0, p=0.962, I2=0.0%). In meta-analysis, adjunct use of tocolytics and antibiotics was not associated with a decrease in preterm birth at less than 28 weeks (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.65–1.26, Figure 2). Only one study reported each on PPROM and neonatal birth weight, so meta-analysis was not performed. Two studies reported neonatal survival to discharge.27,29 In meta-analysis, exposure to adjunct therapy was not associated with increased survival (RR 1.11, 95% CI 0.91–1.35, Figure 3).

In the setting of less than 10 included studies, a funnel plot with Egger’s statistical test for publication bias was not performed. Planned sensitivity and subgroup analyses were not performed because of the small number of studies.

Discussion

In systematic review of the literature, we identified three studies evaluating the adjunct use of antibiotic or tocolytic therapy at the time of exam-indicated cerclage. Only data from one trial evaluating duration of gestational latency in the setting of adjunct therapies could be included. Data on gestational latency could not be pooled and meta-analyzed secondary to redundant patient inclusion across two studies. Further, the two studies used to pool data for the secondary outcomes meta-analyzed (delivery <28 weeks and neonatal survival) used slightly different comparison groups, with one testing the effect of combined tocolytic-antibiotic therapy and the other testing only tocolytic therapy with or without antibiotic therapy. Thus, the available studies were insufficient to robustly evaluate the use of adjunct tocolytics and antibiotics at time of exam-indicated cerclage to prolong latency, or to evaluate other meaningful outcomes including preterm delivery and neonatal survival.

Exam-indicated cerclage has been demonstrated to prolong gestational latency and improve pregnancy outcomes. Ehsanipoor et al completed a systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating use of physical exam-indicated cerclage for cervical insufficiency and pregnancy outcomes. Eight studies contributed 657 participants to analysis of the primary outcome, finding that exam-indicated cerclage was associated with increased neonatal survival (71% vs 43%, RR 1.65, 95% CI 1.19–2.28).10 Based on this evidence, exam-indicated cerclage has been adopted into clinical care for cervical insufficiency in an ongoing pregnancy.1

Data evaluating adjunct therapies, however, remain limited.1,30 The use of tocolytics and antibiotics as adjunct therapies have garnered the most interest based on their theoretical disruption of the pathophysiologic pathways of uterine contractions and ascending infection. Maternal plasma concentrations of prostaglandins increase transiently following cerclage placement and may result in increased uterine activity, while administration of indomethacin, with its anti-inflammatory and anti-prostaglandin characteristics, may promote uterine quiescence.31,32 Antibiotics are hypothesized to prolong gestational latency at time of exam-indicated cerclage through suppression of vaginal pathogens and prevention of ascending infection in the setting of fetal membrane exposure. Despite the biologic plausibility of their utility, data supporting adjunct therapies are lacking, and current guidelines do not currently recommend use of adjuncts.1,13,14,33 In the Ehsanipoor et al meta-analysis that informed use of exam-indicated cerclage, there was significant clinical heterogeneity between included studies for use of adjunct therapies, including antibiotics, tocolytics, and bed rest, precluding extension of the findings to support a specific peri-procedure approach for adjuncts.10 In our study, we aimed to systematically review the literature to provide an updated evaluation of available data addressing the use of antibiotic or tocolytic adjuncts.

We identified three studies to evaluate whether adjunct antibiotics or tocolytics improve outcomes when administered at the time of cerclage. In Berghella et al, the use of adjunct indomethacin, with or without antibiotics, was not associated with increased neonatal survival, increased neonatal birth weight > 1500 g, or reduction in preterm birth less than 28 weeks.29 This study did not contribute data to the primary outcome of our analysis, and the absence of multivariable statistical adjustment likely introduced bias. Miller et al performed a single center RCT evaluating the addition of tocolytics and antibiotics for 24 hours peri-procedure on prolongation of gestational latency after exam-indicated cerclage.27 While adjunct therapy did not prolong the median gestational age latency, in post-hoc analysis considering gestational age latency categorically, adjunct therapies were associated with increased latency greater than 28 days (92.3% vs 62.5%, p=0.01). This increased 28-day latency did not translate to differences in secondary outcomes including gestational age at delivery, neonatal outcomes, or neonatal survival.27This was the only RCT contributing data to our systematic review and meta-analysis. At the same academic institution, Premkumar et al completed a retrospective cohort study evaluating adjunct antibiotic and tocolytic.28 The cohort (2009 to 2018) encompassed the study period of the Miller RCT with an institutional protocol change to use of adjunct therapies in 2014.27,28 In adjusted analyses, adjunct therapy was associated with increased post-procedure gestational age latency of greater than 28 days (aRR 1.21, 95% CI 1.05–1.4) and longer median gestational age latency (17.8 day difference, 95% CI 1.4–34.2, p=0.03). The use of adjunct cefazolin and indomethacin was also associated with an increased median neonatal birth weight.28

For this systematic review, the limited number of available studies resulted in an inability to perform meta-analysis for the primary outcome of gestational age latency, as well as PPROM and neonatal birth weight. In pooled analysis, we demonstrated no association between adjunct therapies and preterm birth less than 28 weeks or neonatal survival to hospital discharge. In the absence of additional studies, there are insufficient data to guide clinical practice.

Our study has several limitations. The small number of studies available precluded meaningful meta-analysis, but our systematic review provides an updated examination of available literature on this topic. The RCT nested within an eligible retrospective cohort study also limited analysis. The decision to not use both studies for meta-analysis of a given outcome reduced bias by eliminating multiple encounters of the same data. In another retrospective cohort study, variation based on practitioner discretion and inconsistent use of antibiotics in the studied population introduced clinical heterogeneity. The same study was considered at ‘critical risk’ of bias due to lack of controlling for potential confounders. Finally, this meta-analysis focused on tocolytic and antibiotic adjunct therapies, but other clinical practices such as pre-procedure amniocentesis or surgical technique may contribute to pregnancy outcomes.

This study also has strengths. This systematic review provides an updated evaluation of available data to inform clinical decision-making and guide future research surrounding use of adjunct tocolytics and antibiotics at time of exam-indicated cerclage placement. Our search strategy included evaluation of grey literature and did not limit the search by date or language restrictions, reducing the influence of publication bias and maximizing search comprehensiveness, respectively.

Our findings demonstrate the need for future prospective studies to further address the question of adjunct therapies at the time of exam-indicated cerclage. While the Miller and Premkumar et al studies demonstrated increased latency greater than 28 days with adjunct therapies, these studies overlap in the population studied, and the same findings have not been reproduced in another clinical center or population.27,28 Additionally, the clinical heterogeneity described in the Berghella cohort study demonstrates the high degree of variation present in management of cervical insufficiency. While clinically a multipronged approach may seem reasonable to address a multifaceted problem, the lack of high-quality evidence for individual parts of the care plan is likely to have unanticipated consequences. Finally, gestational latency is a surrogate outcome; future studies should consider clinically meaningful primary outcomes such as neonatal survival to discharge.
