Association Between Anesthesia Delivered During Tumor Resection and Cancer Survival: a Systematic Review of a Mixed Picture with Constant Themes

Abstract

Background

Surgery is required for cure of most solid tumors, and general anesthesia is required for most cancer surgery. The vast majority of cancer surgery is facilitated by general anesthesia using volatile inhalational agents such as isoflurane and sevoflurane. Only recently have the immunologic and oncologic effect of inhalational agents, and their alternative, propofolbased total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA), come under investigation.

Methods

Between January 2019 and June 2020, English language articles on PubMed were searched for the keywords “Propofol” “TIVA” or “IV anesthesia” and either “cancer surgery” or “surgical oncology.” Duplicates were removes, manuscripts classified as either in vitro, animal, translational, or clinical studies, and their results summarized within these categories.

Results

In-vitro and translational data suggest that inhalational anesthetics are potent immunosuppressive and tumorigenic agents that promote metastasis, while propofol is anti-inflammatory, anti-tumorigenic, and prevents metastasis development. Clinically there is a recurring association, based largely on retrospective, single institution series, that TIVA is associated with significant improvements in disease-free interval and overall survival in a number of, but not all, solid tumors. The longer the surgery is, the more intense the surgical trauma is, the more aggressive the malignancy is, and the higher likelihood of an association is.

Discussion

Prospective randomized trials, coupled with basic science and translational studies, are needed to further define this association.

Introduction

Surgery is central to the treatment of solid tumors, yet following surgical resection, upward of one in three patients will experience a local or distant recurrence of disease.1 Tissue trauma, tumor manipulation, and the physiologic stress inherent to surgery lead to suppressed cellular immunity that can last for up to 1 week.2–6 These physiologic perturbations activate neural and inflammatory signaling pathways that impair the ability of natural killer (NK) cells, the body’s primary defense against tumor metastasis to identify and kill metastatic cells successfully.3–5 Accumulating evidence suggests that these events promote the progression of disease or even enhance tumor burden by creating a “pre-metastatic niche” that traps circulating tumor cells,3,7 leading to cancer recurrence and eventual mortality. Despite this, surgical resection provides the best hope for long-term cure for most solid tumors, and general anesthesia is required for surgery.

General anesthesia is typically administered through two techniques — inhalation or intravenous, with inhalational techniques predominating. Until recently, the immunologic and oncologic impact of anesthesia itself were not well-understood.8–14 Inhalational volatile anesthetics such as sevoflurane or isoflurane are the most commonly used maintenance anesthetics for surgical procedures, and are used in over 90% of solid tumor resections requiring general anesthesia.15 These agents are preferred by anesthesiologists for their predictable physiologic effects, ease of titration, and rapid onset and offset.1 Intravenous anesthetics such as propofol are alternatives to inhalational halogenated anesthetics for the maintenance of general anesthesia. The term “total intravenous anesthetic” (TIVA) refers to the use of intravenous agents for the maintenance of general anesthesia in the absence of inhalational anesthetics. TIVA can refer to the use of a variety of intravenous agents such as opiates, local anesthetics, dexmedetomidine, or ketamine, with intravenous propofol used as the primary maintenance anesthetic agent. Common reasons to use propofol as a primary anesthetic include reducing the incidence of post-operative nausea and vomiting (PONV)15 or facilitating neuromonitoring during procedures in which the muscle relaxant effects of volatile anesthetics would interfere with somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) or motor evoked potentials (MEPs).16 Use of propofol as a general anesthetic has several other benefits including its role as a free-radical scavenger,15 reducing post-operative pulmonary complications in free flap patients,17 and potentially improving post-operative pain control.15 Propofol may also reduce post-operative cognitive dysfunction in elderly patients with preexisting mild cognitive impairment.18,19 More recently, data have emerged suggesting that propofol has anti-inflammatory and immunoprotective effects.1

Physiologic and Immunologic Responses to Surgical Trauma

Tissue damage during surgery increases the levels of circulating inflammatory mediators. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), glucocorticoids, catecholamines, and cytokines suppress the immune system by expansion of regulatory T cells (Treg), reducing the quantity and cytotoxic capacity of NK cells and activated CD8+ T cells, and shifting T cell differentiation from predominately anti-tumor TH1 cells towards tumor-promoting TH2 cells.20–22 Cyclooxygenase (COX) and PGE2 contribute to the development of the pre-metastatic niche, providing an environment in which circulating tumor cells can thrive.3 Under these conditions neutrophils become the predominant leukocyte, vastly outnumbering lymphocytes. A higher neutrophil-to-leukocyte ratio has been associated with increased risk of cancer recurrence in multiple tumor types,23 possibly because of the impact of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). NETs are a vital part of neutrophil host defense against bacteria24 but are tumorigenic,25,26 and contribute to post-operative metastasis.27,28 Increasing NET production has been associated with post-operative thrombosis26,29,30 and worse overall survival in cancer patients.24

Following surgical trauma, the pro-inflammatory phase of wound healing begins. Neutrophils and macrophages are recruited to the wound, resulting in upregulation of multiple factors that promote angiogenesis, tumor cell dissemination, and growth of previously undetected metastatic sites.22,31 Fibroblasts and mesenchymal stem cells recruited to the wound release other growth factors that stimulate circulating cancer cells.32 Exacerbated by patient anxiety, hypothermia, pre-operative fasting, and intraoperative metabolic derangements, the post-surgical period causes upregulation of the sympathetic nervous system. Circulating catecholamines agonize ß-adrenergic receptors, upregulated on tumor cells at baseline, resulting in increased transcription of metastasis-promoting factors such as VEGF and MMPs, remodeling of tumor-associated lymphatics, and eventual tumor recurrence.33–35

Physiologic perturbations during, and as a result of, cancer surgery significantly impact long-term disease recurrence. Complications following surgery, including the need for blood transfusions,36,37 wound infections,38 and anastomotic leaks,39 are all associated with an increased risk of cancer recurrence. In addition, platelet and tissue factor activation facilitates coagulation, while platelet activation and microclot formation help circulating tumor cells survive vascular shear stress and avoid NK cell detection,40 which increases circulating tumor cell survival and increasing the chances of post-operative metastasis. NETs have been implicated not only in hypercoagulability and thrombosis,26,29,30 but also in tumorigenesis 25,26 and post-operative metastasis development,27,28 and cancer-specific survival.24,41

In aggregate, the pro-inflammatory and hypercoagulable states induced by tumor resection surgery create an ideal milieu for the dissemination of tumor cells to distant sites while inflammation and tissue damage stimulate the release of inflammatory factors that stimulate tumor growth and push the immune system towards an immunosuppressed state. Increased catecholamine levels support the transcription of metastasis-promoting factors. Platelet activation and NETs “cloak” the circulating tumor cells from the body’s innate immune system. In the context of these factors, surgery remains required for cure for most solid tumors, and general anesthesia is required for surgery. We conducted this systematic review to highlight our current understanding of the immunological and tumorigenic impacts of major anesthetic agents, as well as the clinical impact of anesthetic technique, for surgeons and anesthesiologists alike.

Methods

Using the PRISMA guidelines,42 between January 2019 and July 2020, PubMed was searched for all English language articles with the keywords “Propofol” “TIVA” or “IV anesthesia” and either “cancer surgery” or “surgical oncology.” Institutional board review permission was not sought for this literature review. Following exclusion of 1149 articles, 105 were screened by the authors (LS and DJD) for inclusion and to assure that all appropriate articles were included. Referenced articles were crosschecked, but no additional citations were added (Fig. 1). One retrospective national series published after July 2020 was subsequently included in the manuscript.43 Following exclusion of review articles (n = 15), clinical trial protocols (n = 5), studies on the addition of regional anesthesia to inhalational anesthesia (n = 36), and studies on surgeries other than surgical oncology (n = 13), 35 articles were included in the qualitative synthesis (Fig. 1). Studies were roughly broken down into in vitro studies (n = 10; Table 1), animal studies (n = 3 unique studies, 5 total; Table 2), translational studies (n = 8; Table 3), and clinical studies (n = 14; Table 4), though there was some overlap. Studies with multiple outcomes were discussed in multiple sections, as necessary. In general, in vitro studies studied either the volatile anesthetics or propofol in isolation, while the translational and retrospective studies studied general anesthesia with either volatile anesthesia or propofol-based TIVA.

Results

Anesthetic Modulation of Immune Function

Contemporary inhalational anesthetics, such as isoflurane and sevoflurane, have properties that are immunosuppressive, tumorigenic, and induce metastasis. Brief exposures cause upregulation of hypoxia inducible factor-1 (HIF1) and transforming growth factor beta (TGFß), which confer a survival benefit to residual cancer cells.44,45,70,71 TGFß upregulates the transcription of multiple pro-metastatic genes such as VEGF, the MMPs, and angiopoietin, promoting tumor cell proliferation and migration.45,47,71 Inhalational anesthetics change circulating cytokine levels to a pro-inflammatory state,59,61,72 induce a shift from anti-tumor TH1 cells towards tumor-promoting TH2 cells,55 weaken innate immunity by decreasing the activity of NK cells,57 increase inflammatory cytokines,73 and increase the migration of tumor cells48 (Tables 1, 2, and 3).

Conversely, propofol is anti-inflammatory, anti-tumorigenic, and anti-metastatic. This anesthetic agent suppresses prostaglandin and inflammatory cytokine production,58–6 preventing immunosuppression.58,72 Propofol inhibits cancer cell migration,50 increases immune cell infiltration in the tumor,57 preserves NK cell function (unlike inhalational anesthetics),74 reduces metastases formation,50,51,74 and inhibits NET formation.52,53 These anti-inflammatory effects of propofol were investigated in a prospective randomized controlled trial of non-cancer patients undergoing open cholecystectomy who were randomized to receive either propofol-based TIVA or inhalational isoflurane.59 Post-operatively, plasma levels of the inflammatory cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF-alpha) were significantly lower in the propofol group,59 suggesting that propofol suppressed the inflammatory response to surgical trauma more than inhalational anesthetics.

Several translational studies have also investigated the immunomodulatory effects of different anesthetic techniques. Both Desmond et al.57 and Buckley et al.14 analyzed subsets of patients from a randomized controlled trial comparing general anesthesia with sevoflurane versus regional anesthesia with propofol and a paravertebral block.69 Desmond et al. noted that the women who received a paravertebral block and propofol had increased levels of NK cell and CD4+ cell infiltration of the primary tumor when compared to the women who received general anesthesia.57 Buckley et al. reported that NK cells isolated from post-operative serum in the women who received propofol and a paravertebral block exhibited significantly higher NK cell activity than cells isolated from women who received sevoflurane.14 Similarly, Ferrell et al. randomized patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (n = 10 in each arm) to receive either propofolbased TIVA or sevoflurane during their surgical resection. Exposure to sevoflurane was associated with a statistically significant increase in the expression of pro-oncogenic protein markers in tumor cells when compared to TIVA.60 In a randomized trial of 60 patients undergoing radical hysterectomy for cervical cancer to either propofol-based TIVA or sevoflurane, patients who received sevoflurane anesthesia had lower post-operative CD3+, CD4+, and NK cell counts, and a lower CD4+:CD8+ ratio than patients who received propofol-based TIVA.61 Finally, Yan et al. randomized 80 women undergoing either breast conserving surgery or modified radical mastectomy to propofol-based TIVA or sevoflurane. The group receiving propofol had no difference between pre-operative and post-operative VEGF concentrations. In the sevoflurane group, women who received breast conserving therapy had no difference in VEGF concentrations. However, women who underwent a modified radical mastectomy with sevoflurane anesthesia had significant increases in post-operative VEGF concentrations when compared to their pre-operative concentrations. This finding suggests that there is a relationship between the length and extent of surgery and the impact of anesthetic approach. Two-year recurrence-free survival was 78% in the sevoflurane group and 95% in the TIVA group (p=0.221), while 2-year overall survival was 97% in both groups.62

Collectively, the in vitro and translational data (Tables 1, 2, and 3) suggest that the inhalational anesthetics are potent immunosuppressive and tumorigenic agents, while propofol is anti-inflammatory, anti-tumorigenic, and prevents metastasis development. Yan’s study additionally suggests that this relationship is dose dependent. These foundational studies have prompted several groups to retrospectively examine the clinical outcomes associated with anesthetic agents delivered during surgery.

Long-Term Impact of Anesthetic Technique During Cancer Surgery

Cancer Recurrence

To our knowledge, the first clinical report comparing cancer outcomes by anesthetic technique was published by Enlund et al. in 2014.13 Using hospital records linked to a regional clinical research registry in Uppsala, Sweden, the authors evaluated oncologic outcomes among patients undergoing surgery for colon (n = 695), rectal (n = 306), and breast (n = 1837) cancers. The authors observed a dramatic rise in the use of propofol-based anesthesia from <1% at the beginning of their study to >85% at the end of their study. On initial analysis, patients anesthetized with propofol had significantly better 1- and 5-year survival than those anesthetized with sevoflurane (absolute difference in favor of propofol: 4.7% (p = 0.004) and 5.6% (p < 0.001)). However, these differences in oncologic outcomes diminished following adjustment for clinical and cancer-specific cofounders, though there was a trend towards improved survival with propofol at 3 years.13

Lee et al. investigated 363 patients with breast cancer undergoing consecutive modified radical mastectomies. The authors reported a lower rate of cancer recurrence within 5 years in the group anesthetized with propofol (n = 173, 20 recurrences, 11.6%) than the group anesthetized with sevoflurane (n = 152, 29 recurrences, 19.1%) [p = 0.037].75 However, there was no difference in 5-year overall survival between groups (propofol: 9 deaths in 173 patients; sevoflurane: 11 deaths in 153 patients, p = 0.383). Following multivariable analysis for important clinical factors tumor size (p = 0.01) and anesthetic technique (p = 0.014) were significantly associated with cancer recurrence.75

Makito et al. analyzed almost 200,000 patients in the Japanese Diagnosis Procedure Combination database undergoing esophagectomy, gastrectomy, hepatectomy, cholecystectomy, colectomy, or proctectomy for malignancy. With approximately 2-year follow-up (volatile anesthesia: median 639 days, IQR 234–1301 days; TIVA: median 768 days, IQR 286–1525 days) the authors reported no difference in unadjusted recurrence or overall survival. On instrument variable analysis, however, receipt of TIVA was associated with significantly improved recurrence-free survival (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.87–0.98, p = 0.01) but not overall survival.

Overall Survival

The first plausible link between anesthetic technique and overall survival was established by Wigmore et al. in 2016.12 Using a cohort of 11,395 patients who underwent cancer surgery between 2010 and 2013 in the Netherlands, the authors found that inhalational anesthetics were associated with worse overall survival (unadjusted hazard ratio (HR) 1.8, 95% CI 1.61–2.02; adjusted HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.31 to 1.64) compared to propofol after adjusting for all relevant clinical factors. This association was confirmed even after a sensitivity analysis on a subset of propensity score matched patients. Similarly, Jun et al. noted that patients who received inhalational agents had increased tumor recurrence (HR: 1.42 (1.12–1.79), p = 0.003) and decreased survival (1.58 (1.24–2.01), p ≤ 0.001) compared with patients who received TIVA in a cohort of patients undergoing esophagectomy.11 A similar sensitivity analysis in a cohort of propensity score matched patients confirmed this association (HR for recurrence: 1.36 (1.04–1.77), p = 0.024; HR for survival: 1.57 (1.19–2.07), p = 0.001).11

In a propensity score matched study of patients who underwent surgery for resection of colon cancer between 2005 and 2014, Wu et al. reported that patients receiving propofol as their primary anesthetic had significantly better overall survival.63 This was true regardless of final pathologic stage (HR for propofol, Stage I and II: 0.22 (0.11–0.42), p <0.01; Stage III and IV: 0.42 (0.32–0.55), p < 0.01).53 In a cohort of 2856 patient with gastric cancer, 53% (1506) of whom received propofol-based TIVA, Zheng et al. reported that TIVA was associated with improved overall survival (HR 0.61, 95% CI: 0.55–0.69, p <0.001).76 Anesthetic technique remained significant following a sensitivity analysis on a subset of propensity score matched patients (HR =0.65, 95% CI: 0.56–0.75, p < 0.05).76 Finally, in a retrospective analysis of patients undergoing hepatectomy for hepatocellular carcinoma, Lai et al. found that patients who received propofol-based anesthesia had significantly lower local recurrence rates (propensity score matched HR: 0.42 (95% CI 0.34–0.52, p<0.001), distant recurrence rates (0.25 (95% CI 0.15–0.41, p<0.001), and lower cancer specific mortality (propensity score matched HR: 0.55 (95% CI 0.44–0.69, p<0.001).64

The same group analyzed their institutional experience with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma65 and robotic assisted radical prostatectomy77 with similar results. In a matched sample of patients who underwent resection for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, patients who received propofol-based TIVA had significantly lower rates of post-operative metastasis (HR 0.36, 95% CI: 0.15–0.88, p = 0.025) and significantly improved overall survival (HR 0.51, 95% CI: 0.28–0.94, p = 0.032).65 Patients who underwent robotic assisted robotic prostatectomy with propofol also had significantly lower rates of biochemical recurrence (HR 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03–0.48, p = 0.003) and improved overall survival (HR 0.2, 95% CI: 0.05–0.91, p = 0.038) compared with patients who underwent surgery with desflurane anesthesia.77 These results are highlighted in Table 4.

In contrast to the above studies, several groups failed to find an association between anesthetic technique and long-term clinical outcomes. In a cohort of 2729 patients who underwent breast cancer surgery between 2005 and 2010, Kim et al. did not find an association between anesthetic technique and disease-specific or overall survival.66 Among these patients, only 54 received TIVA. They did not present baseline data on patients selected to receive volatile anesthesia or TIVA, which makes further comparison between the likelihood of recurrence impossible. Similarly, Yoo et al. also did not find an association between anesthetic technique and 5-year survival when retrospectively examining breast cancer patients who underwent surgery at their institution between 2005 and 2013.8 Likewise, Oh et al. found no association between anesthetic technique and recurrence-free or overall survival when examining a propensity score matched cohort of patients undergoing surgery for non-small-cell lung cancer.67 Interestingly, in this study all patients received propofol during induction, potentially confounding the results, as the interaction between propofol’s anti-tumor effects and the pro-tumor effects of the volatile anesthetics are not known. Finally, Oh et al. did not find an association between anesthetic technique and 1-year survival patients with gastric cancer patients receiving gastrectomy. Patients in this study were well-matched based on propensity scores for anesthesia techniques, but 1-year mortality was only 3%. Of note, the authors noted that a sample size of 228,402 patients would be needed to detect a 0.2% difference with 80% power and a type I error of 0.05. Clearly, their study is underpowered68 (Table 4).

In one of the few prospective randomized trials on this topic, Yan et al. randomized 80 patients undergoing breast surgery to receive either propofol-based TIVA or sevoflurane-based anesthesia.62 The authors measured postoperative pain levels and opioid administration, serum concentrations of TGF-B and VEGF-C, both known to contribute to tumor metastasis, and recurrence and overall survival at 2 years. The subset of patients who received sevoflurane and underwent modified radical mastectomy had significantly higher post-operative VEGF concentrations. Those who received sevoflurane and underwent breast conserving therapy did not have a change in post-operative VEGF concentrations. None of the patients who received propofol experienced a change in VEGF concentrations. Two-year recurrence-free survival was 78% in the sevoflurane group and 95% in the TIVA group (p=0.221), whereas 2-year overall survival was 97% in both groups. The study was underpowered to detect a 2-year survival difference.62 Sessler et al. randomized over 2000 patients undergoing mastectomy to general anesthesia with sevoflurane or paravertebral block and propofol. With 3 years of follow-up there was no difference in survival.69 Other translational studies did find that women with breast cancer who received propofol had significantly higher levels of tumor infiltrating immune and NK cells57 as well as higher activity than NK cells6 compared with individuals who received sevoflurane.

Discussion

Propofol-based TIVA is safe15 and results in several improved short-term clinical outcomes including decreased postoperative cognitive impairment,18,19 post-operative nausea and vomiting,15 earlier return of bowel function,2 and possibly decreased opioid utilization in the early post-operative period.15

Based on retrospective single institution studies, there is a growing body of evidence supporting the association between propofol-based TIVA and improved cancer-specific outcomes. However, this finding is not uniform,8,62,66–68 but is supported by a mechanistic understanding of the anti-inflammatory,1,15,45,47,49,56,78 immunosupportive,12,14,49,57–59,61,72 anti-angiogenic,12,60,62 and anti-metastatic50,60,74 properties of propofol when compared to the volatile anesthetics. These clinical associations appear to be dose dependent and disease specific.62

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Chang et al analyzed 19 studies, 17 of which had overall survival endpoints and 10 of which had recurrence endpoints. They found that receiving volatile anesthesia during cancer surgery was associated with worse overall survival than receiving TIVA (HR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.66–0.94, p = .008). There was no association between anesthesia type and recurrence rate (HR = 0.81, 95% CI 0.61–1.07, p = .137). Interestingly, on a subgroup analysis, they found differential survival impacts based on volatile anesthetic. Use of desflurane was associated with worse survival (HR = 0.54, 95% CI 0.36–0.80, p = .003), while neither sevoflurane (HR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.74–1.14, p = .439) or various volatile anesthetics (HR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.64–1.07, p = .156). Based on cancer type, volatile anesthesia was associated with worse overall survival for gastrointestinal cancers (HR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.58–0.75, p < .001) and hepatobiliary cancers (HR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.39–0.58, p < .001), but not in breast cancer (HR = 1.12, 95% CI 0.86–1.46, p = .382), colorectal cancer (HR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.33–1.29, p = .222), or glioma (HR = 1.00, 95% CI 0.78–1.28, p = .987).79

The more significant the surgical stress is, and the longer the duration of anesthesia is, the more significant the impact of the anesthetic agent is. In-vitro data demonstrated that the effect is dose dependent,48,51 which corresponds to studies showing that women who undergo modified radical mastectomy with sevoflurane have significant increases in VEGF concentrations while those who undergo partial mastectomy do not. Women who received propofol and underwent either surgical procedure did not have changes in VEGF concentration.62 The retrospective data showing an association between anesthetic technique and recurrence or survival is strongest with more invasive procedures and aggressive tumors such as colon cancer,63 gastric cancer,63 hepatocellular carcinoma,65 intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma,65 and prostate cancer.77 Propofol’s anti-tumorigenic and anti-metastatic effects appear most pronounced during longer and more invasive procedures. Therefore, it is possible that a greater oncologic benefit is derived from an anti-inflammatory and immunoprotective anesthetic technique such as propofol-based TIVA, but most of these studies are confounded by the biases inherent to retrospective studies.

Conclusion

When compared to volatile inhalational anesthesia, retrospective studies have established a recurring association between the propofol-based TIVA during curative-intent cancer surgery. This association is supported by in vitro, in vivo, and translational data suggesting that the inhalational anesthetics are immunosuppressive, tumorigenic, and pro-metastatic, while propofol is anti-inflammatory, anti-tumorigenic, and prevents metastasis development. Rigorous, prospective clinical trials are needed to further our understanding of the clinical implications of these mostly pre-clinical and translational studies. There are several ongoing or recently completed prospective trials investigating the role of propofol in oncologic surgery. The findings of these studies should help define the potential benefits of propofol-based TIVA for cancer patients undergoing surgery.
