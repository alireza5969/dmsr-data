Abstract

Oral cancer is typically related to environmental carcinogen exposure including tobacco and alcohol. Other less investigated risk factors may be related to a suppressed or dysregulated immune state, and in oral cancer, various levels of immune dysregulation have been found to affect survival and recurrence rates. The rationale for this systematic review was to investigate the possible role that a growing chronic host condition like an autoimmune disease may play in this disease. A systematic search of the literature was carried out using four electronic databases in order to identify original research of any analytic study design type that investigated the relationship between autoimmune disease and oral cancer. Out of 1,947 identified records, 24 observational studies were included for qualitative synthesis. The studies varied in end points ranging from overall survival (OS), standardized incidence ratio (SIR), and hazard ratio (HR). Due to the heterogenous sampling of studies even within the same study design group, a meta-analysis was not employed. The current state of the literature is varied and heterogenous in both study design and endpoints. Major limitations existed introducing significant bias especially in determining cancer risk such as lack of information surrounding known etiologic risk factors such as alcohol and tobacco consumption. Despite these limitations, a signal was seen between autoimmune disease and oral cancer outcomes and risk. Future studies investigating the relationship between autoimmune disease and oral cancer in a more focused and quantitative manner are therefore needed.

INTRODUCTION

The relationship between autoimmune (AI) conditions and cancer is not one that has been investigated as thoroughly as other considerably more apparent “host” variables. A broad-spectrum 2012 review identified the need for more extensive research in this area, as largely positive associations were highlighted between 23 specific AI diseases across cancer pathologies (1). While this review focused on all cancer types, little literature exists in niche oncological categories. Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), although it is the sixth most common cancer in the world, has very little research evidence on the role of AI disease in risk, prognosis, and even prevalence.

The rationale for this systematic review in the context of OSCC was to investigate the role that a chronic host condition may play in a primarily toxin driven disease. OSCC is of major concern in areas where betel nut, smoking, and alcohol usage are particularly high. (3) Therefore, questions arise when patients who lack these traditional risk factors, such as young women who do not smoke or drink alcohol, develop OSCC. How does the host play a role in OSCC and cancer? There are links between viruses and OSCC in an immunosuppressed state, as seen by an elevated risk for OSCC in HIV and organ-transplant patients. (3) Peripheral blood leukocytes (PBLs), as an example of host immune status, have been shown to predict outcome in patients specifically with OSCC (4–5). Across cancer types specifically within the tumor microenvironment, the role the immune response plays, from evading growth suppressors to the role of surveillance by the host as a protective measure, has led to outcome data that has furthered studies in this realm. (6–9) Further investigation is necessary not just to explain this phenomenon but also in the future, develop strategies to potentially prevent OSCC in this population.

While the role of the immune system is being investigated more thoroughly in OSCC on a microscopic and cellular level, AI disorders as a macroscopic presentation of a dysregulated immune system have little to show by way of information in the literature. This lack of information prompted a consolidation of this information in a systematic manner. The objectives and questions posed in this current review therefore are as follows: to identify the breadth of literature on the topic, to assess if patients with AI disorder are at higher risk for developing OSCC compared to the general population, and if OSCC patients with AI disorder had worse outcome than those without an AI disorder.

AI diseases range from systemic conditions, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), to organ-specific diseases, such as Celiac disease. Patients with AI diseases are treated with immunosuppressive medications in order to moderate their overactive immune systems. As a chronic illness group that affects millions of individuals in the developed world that also adversely impacts women, there is much interest in learning more about this heterogenous group. (2) Autoimmunity refers to one’s immune cells, such as T and B cells, reacting to self-antigens as opposed to external antigens. Causes for autoimmune disease range from infectious agents to genetics, but the effects on the body are similar where autoimmune damage can be systemically inflammatory. (2) AI diseases being so heterogenous played a role in why any and all AI disease were included in this review. Additionally, the limited number of studies available on just one AI disorder and OSCC, as well as, lack of knowledge of which AI disease is most associated with OSCC, led to the decision to include all types of AI disorders in this review.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This review protocol was registered with PROSPERO: CRD42020179253. The search methods will be outlined below. The review followed Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. (10)

Search Strategy

A systematic search of the literature was carried out using four electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE (NLM), EMBASE (Elsevier), CINAHL (EBSCO), and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Wiley) on 2/20/2020. The literature search strategy was developed in PubMed/MEDLINE and then translated to accommodate the controlled vocabulary specific to the other databases. A combination of subject headings and relevant keyword terms were used to search PubMed/MEDLINE (Medical Subject Headings, MeSH), EMBASE (Emtree), and CINAHL (CINAHL Subject Headings). An equivalent keyword search strategy was used to search the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. The database search results were not restricted by date, publication type, or language.

The literature search strategy consisted of three main concepts for which individual search hedges were first designed and then combined altogether: 1) Patients with Autoimmune /Autoinflammatory Diseases; Patients taking immunosuppressors AND 2) Oral Cavity Cancer; AND 3) Risk. The extensive list of search terms included in the first search hedge describing the AI concept was compiled primarily using the MEDLINE thesaurus MeSH® and the EMBASE Emtree® thesaurus, as well as, by consulting other relevant publications. (1, 41–43)

The Endnote citation management software program (Clarivate Analytics) was used to capture the database search results and to remove the duplicate citations. The citations were then imported into the Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation) for record screening, quality assessment, and data extraction.

Study Selection

Studies included in this review were required to be original research of any design style that investigated the relationship between AI disease and oral cancer of any subsite. Only studies available in English were included and exclusion criteria consisted of: systematic reviews, conference abstracts, letters to the editors, studies that did not mention an AI disorder or did not include oral cavity patients, theses and dissertations, and any other non-original research. Oral lichen planus (OLP) studies were excluded during the title/abstract review as it was decided that this condition, although a common AI disease that afflicts the oral mucosa, fell outside the scope of this review (see Discussion). All identified titles, abstracts, and full-text were independently reviewed and screened by two authors (AP and DA). Any identified conflicts at the title and abstract screening stage were discussed at length between reviewers. Full-text review was then conducted and screening discrepancies were subsequently reviewed using the same methodology. Any lingering disagreements were discussed again at length before final inclusion decisions were consolidated.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by reviewers (AP and DA). Any discrepancies in data collection were also discussed at length between the reviewers and consensus was reached for each data point. Study details such as sponsorship and author and institution data were collected as well as study design, population characteristics and outcome data. Any relevant outcome data was collected as the statistics varied across studies.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Two independent reviewers (AP and DA) conducted the risk of bias assessment using customized risk of bias template. The quality assessment analyzed the focus, exposure and outcome bias, ethical considerations and precision of the results of the studies. Utilizing these parameters, overall judgment across all studies indicated low bias and a consensus was reached on this assessment across all 24 studies.

RESULTS

Search Selection and Characteristics

Out of 2,184 identified records in the database, 238 records were found to be duplicates and were removed, leaving a total of 1,946 records that were subsequently screened. The 1,946 records were screened using title and abstract information, 95 articles were considered for full-text review, and 24 were included for qualitative synthesis (Figure 1). Eighteen studies were retrospective cohort studies, four were prospective cohort studies, and two were case-control studies. Across all the studies, a total of 213 participants had both an AI disease and OSCC. One study also included patients with other head and neck cancers (HNC) in the total and two studies did not provide the number of participants across both disease groups.

Studies identified, study parameters and association between AI and OSCC.

A total of 24 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis. Eighteen of these studies were retrospective cohort studies in nature. They ranged in end points as metrics such as overall survival (OS), standardized incidence ratio (SIR), and hazard ratio (HR) were used. Due to the heterogenous sampling of studies even within same study design groups, a meta-analysis was not employed. Of the 18 retrospective cohort studies identified (Table 1), 13 studies used SIR as a parameter with values ranging from 0.14–44.4. Ten of the studies, however, reported an incidence ratio greater than 1. Yu, et al. separated the SIR values based on disease group but did not indicate the sample size of each cohort analyzed. (15) Three studies used OR as their parameter and had a range of values from 1.3–2.86. In the study by Hongell, et al. the OR was calculated per disease site, i.e. tongue, floor of mouth (FOM), and other areas were assessed separately. (19) Two studies used HR’s as their parameters with a range of 0.71–5.4. (Table 1)

There were four case-control studies included for consideration in this review and these are shown in Table 2. Only one study used a parameter and that was for HR yielding a value of 2.08. (Table 2) There were 2 prospective cohort studies identified (Table 3) but these studies that were included did not have any parameters outside of number of patients included with AI and OSCC diseases. DISCUSSION

It is widely recognized that the immune system plays a large role in cancer inception, development and progression. This phenomenon has been established in a myriad of ways from HIV+ and transplant patients on immunosuppressive medications. (27–28) Cancer risk is generally increased with a suppressed or dysregulated immune state, whether it is due to host factors or from medications which are immunosuppressive. Within our own department, we have investigated the role that the host plays in oral cancer and reported how various levels of immune dysregulation affect survival and recurrence rates. (4–5)

The identification and emphasis on host factors and how they impact survival was seen through these investigations and one of the natural subsequent steps was to explore the role of AI disease as a macroscopic variation of immune dysregulation in OC. With a hyperactive immune state, as is the case in AI patients, a hyperinflammatory prolonged state can itself promote tumorigenesis while immunosuppressive treatment that is used for most AI diseases also has the same effect. (7) The chronic risk state that AI patients have in addition to the existing evidence surrounding host factors in OSCC has led us to further investigate this relationship.

To initiate and guide design of an interrogation of our institutional data on OSCC, an effort was made to understand the current state of the literature on the subject. Using a comprehensive review investigating 23 AI diseases and any cancer, it was determined that sufficient information existed in the literature to support a relationship between these two diseases. (1). Prior to embarking on a study of our institutional data, a focused literature review was deemed essential to understanding the need for further study and to assist in framing the questions to be studied.

This systematic review included 24 studies that investigated the potential association between an AI condition and OSCC. The studies ranged in design, sample size, metrics used to measure outcomes (from OR to SIR to HR), as well as which AI diseases were included. Some studies focused on one disease, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), while others focused on systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases (SARD’s). (15, 25). Some studies focused solely on specific cancer patients while others included OSCC patients within larger cancer cohorts. The heterogeneity of the studies was inevitable due to the scarce nature of current literature on the topic. While most of the studies did show an increased SIR, OR, or HR in association with having an AI disease and OSCC, a few studies reported a decreased risk.

One population-based retrospective cohort study looked at vitiligo patients diagnosed and identified through the Korean National health Insurance claims database from January 2007 to December 2016. (18) Controls were matched for age and sex in a 2:1 ratio to the patients with vitiligo. Over 100,000 patients with vitiligo were included in the study and malignancies developed were of interest in this study. Overall, the risk of malignancies was significantly lower in vitiligo patients than the control patients with a HR of 0.86 and CI of 0.82–0.89. (18) Forty-two patients in this cohort developed oral and pharyngeal malignancies. One main limitation with the study was that oral and pharyngeal cancers were grouped together making it difficult to determine if the patients were truly OSCC patients or if they had other primary sites of malignancies. Interestingly, the SIR of patients with vitiligo developing oral and pharyngeal cancer was 7.2 and the HR was 0.71. The SIR of patients without vitiligo developing oral and pharyngeal cancer was 9.8, noting a decrease in risk associated with a vitiligo diagnosis. A potential explanation, as provided by the authors, was that vitiligo patients primarily use topical ointments and phototherapy as opposed to systemic immunosuppressive medications. (18) This may contribute to the overall decrease in risk seen with vitiligo patients in developing mucosal malignancies and even skin-related malignancies as opposed to patients with other autoimmune disorders. This study had a few limitations such as excluding lifestyle factors that contribute to mucosal cancer risk such as tobacco and alcohol use.

While vitiligo is a disease that is organ-specific, rheumatic diseases such as SLE and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) affect the entire body and belong to an inflammatory group known as SARD’s. One study included in this SR investigated the role of SARDs, which include SLE, RA, Sjogren syndrome (SS), systemic sclerosis (SSc), Behcet disease (BD), polymyositis (PM), dermatomyositis (DM), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and vasculitis. (15) It is logical to note that inflammatory diseases and autoimmune conditions leading to over proliferation of lymphocytes have direct associations with lymphoma. This has been observed to be related to AI disease generally but not with other solid tumors. (15) This study similarly used a retrospective cohort study design and utilized the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database to identify patients with malignancies diagnosed after SARD diagnosis. Over 75,000 SARD patients were identified from January 1997 to December 2010. Most patients were diagnosed with RA and SLE and more females than males were observed. The SIR’s were highest for DM and SSc patients (1.94 and 2.04, respectively). However, RA, IBD, and BD patients had lower SIR’s (0.85, 0.36, 0.88, respectively). This study had major limitations including but not limited to study design, failure to take into account cancer risk factors such as smoking and alcohol habits, medications for treatment of the AI disorder (such as immunosuppressive medications, steroids and anti-TNF agents) However, the study was interesting because it delved deeper into the breakdown of SARDs and included multiple AI conditions as one entity.

While there were few case-control studies with sufficient data to truly examine the association between AI and OSCC (besides the number of OSCC cases within the AI population), one study investigated the correlation between Behcet disease (BD) and cancer. (21) Over 14,000 patients with BD were included in the study and an age- and sex-matched control population was found at a ratio of 10:1. The Korean National Health Insurance Database was utilized for case selection from 2007 to 2014 and 451 cancer diagnoses were found in the patients with BD during the follow-up period. The HR for oral cavity and pharyngeal cancer was 2.113 (1.102–4.052), showing an elevated risk within BD patient population than the normal controls did. This has been attributed to oral ulcers that BD patients experience, though further investigation is likely needed. Chronic inflammation is linked to increased tumor risk. Therefore, oral ulcers as a site of chronic inflammation may be the avenue by which BD patients are predisposed to elevated OSCC risk. The study also linked genetic aberrations such as the MICA A6 allele, abundant in both OSCC and BD patients, as another factor involved in this phenomenon. (21) Major limitations of the study included lack of cancer-associated covariate information as seen with the previous studies such as alcohol and tobacco usage as well as type of immunomodulatory treatment for BD.

One of the most common AI diseases to afflict the oral mucosa is oral lichen planus (OLP), a T-cell mediated condition. However, due to the nature of this condition being unable to differentiate as inherent vs. treatment related, it was not included in the criteria for inclusion for this systematic review. When analyzing the search results, such studies investigating lichen planus patients were excluded for that reason. A large majority of the excluded studies fell into this category as there is a myriad of literature investigating whether OLP is pre-cancerous or not specifically in oral cancer patients. The academic community has yet to reach a consensus as to whether OLP is truly a risk factor for developing cancer especially considering its’ direct site-specific association. (29)

This systematic review had challenges in the availability of homogeneous studies in spite of the sheer volume of the literature available on the topic. During the initial query, the purpose of this effort was to assess the current status of academic knowledge on the relationship between AI conditions and OSCC. Although associations had been identified thus far between a wide array of such diseases and nonspecific cancers, there remains uncertainty regarding the relationship between AI disease and oral cancer.

In conclusion, the current state of the literature is varied and heterogenous in nature. The primary method of investigating the correlation of AI disease and OSCC has been through retrospective cohort studies within larger cancer groups. The number of patients reported in these studies has also been limited, restricting the power of the results. Major limitations existed introducing significant bias especially in determining cancer risk such as lack of information surrounding alcohol and tobacco consumption which are well recognized etiologic factors for OSCC. Given the current state of the literature, we do see a signal in terms of a correlation with AI disease and OSCC but further investigation needs to be conducted. As has been indicated throughout the various studies, specific AI diseases likely need dedicated investigation in correlation with OSCC since the underlying biological and overarching epidemiological factors range widely throughout. For patients who have an AI condition, it is prudent to both understand an increased risk of developing OSCC, as well as, to be aware of potential poorer prognosis associated with the AI diagnosis.
