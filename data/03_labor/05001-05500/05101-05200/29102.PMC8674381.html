Abstract

Background:

Emergency departments (ED) interface with large numbers of patients that are often missed by conventional HIV testing approaches. ED-based HIV self-testing (HIVST) is an innovative engagement approach which has potential for testing gains among populations that have failed to be reached. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated acceptability and uptake of HIVST, as compared to standard provider-delivered testing approaches, among patients seeking care in ED settings.

Methods:

Six electronic databases were systematically searched (Dates: January 1990 – May 2021). Reports with data on HIVST acceptability and/or testing uptake in ED settings were included. Two reviewers identified eligible records (kappa=0.84); quality was assessed using formalized criteria. Acceptability and testing uptake metrics were summarized, and pooled estimates were calculated using random-effects models with assessments of heterogeneity.

Results:

Of 5,773 records identified, seven met inclusion criteria. The cumulative sample was 1,942 subjects, drawn from three randomized control trials (RCTs) and four cross-sectional studies. Four reports assessed HIVST acceptability. Pooled acceptability of self-testing was 92.6% (95% confidence interval [CI]:88.0%−97.1%). Data from two RCTs demonstrated that HIVST significantly increased testing uptake as compared to standard programs (risk ratio [RR]=4.41, 95% CI:1.95–10.10, I2=25.8%). Overall, quality of evidence was low (42.9%) or very low (42.9%), with one report of moderate quality (14.2%).

Conclusions:

Available data indicate that HIVST may be acceptable and may increases testing among patients seeking emergency care, suggesting that expanding ED-based HIVST programs could enhance HIV diagnosis. However, given the limitations of the reports, additional research is needed to better inform the evidence-base.

INTRODUCTION

Globally, there are an estimated 38 million people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).1 Progress has been made towards the United Nations General Assembly’s 90-90-90 targets, which include 90% of all people living with HIV knowing their HIV status, 90% of all people diagnosed with HIV being on antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 90% of all people receiving ART being virologically suppressed; however aggregated global data indicates that all targets will be missed.1 Although scale-up of HIV testing has occurred, approximately 20% of people living with HIV (PLHIV) remain unaware of their status,1 and there are multiple populations, such as men, adolescents, young adults, and men who have sex with men (MSM), who have persistent and substantially inadequate engagement in HIV care.2–6 As testing is the requisite first step in linking PLHIV to care and achieving epidemic control, there persists a need for expanded and enhanced testing approaches.

Emergency care settings provide access to large populations of patients that otherwise do not regularly interact with health services, particularly for injury care by young adults and men.7–9 Studies have reported high HIV prevalence among patients seeking emergency care across low- middle- and high-income countries alike.10–13 As such, emergency departments (EDs) represent important venues with unimpeded access to populations that can be leveraged to advance the provision of HIV services and help contribute to achieving global HIV control targets. Due to this, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends ED-based HIV screening and linkage to treatment programs,14,15 and this has been supported by societies that guide emergency care practice.16 Although research demonstrates that ED patients are willing to engage in HIV care,7,17–19 provider-delivered testing approaches, in which testing is offered by healthcare personnel to those attending health facilities, including EDs, have not yet achieved testing goals, indicating the need for alternative and innovative approaches.2,11,20–24

HIV self-testing (HIVST) is a pragmatic strategy to enhance access to and provision of testing. HIVST is the self-administration of accurate and simple-to-use assays (saliva or blood based) provided to those desiring testing with accompanying information on usage, interpretation and follow-up. HIVST is highlighted by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an approach to improve engagement of commonly missed populations, making emergency care settings prime arenas for implementation of self-testing into existing HIV programming.25 In non-emergency care populations HIVST has been shown to addresses testing barriers pertaining to resources, stigmatization and patient preferences,26,27 and has been found to efficacious based on increase testing uptake in community and facility-based programs.27–29 While these data support HIVST modalities, little is known around the implementation of ED-based programs, in which there exists immense potential for testing gains among populations that have failed to be reached by existing HIV services. To address this gap in the knowledgebase, a systematic review and meta-analysis was performed assessing the literature pertaining to patient acceptability and efficacy of HIVST programs among populations seeking emergency care.

METHODS

Search Strategy

A search strategy was developed directed by a health sciences medical librarian using Cochrane Collaboration guidelines and iteratively refined with a final executed on 15 May 2021.30 The systematic search was designed to identify reports evaluating the acceptability and/or efficacy of ED-based HIVST programs, as compared to standard provider-delivered testing approaches among patients seeking emergency care. Acceptability outcomes included any assessments of theoretical or actual acceptance of ED-based HIVST, and associated preferences, in the source reports. Efficacy outcomes of interest were pre-defined as testing uptake and linkage to care post-testing. Emergency care settings were defined using previously put forth parameters as any healthcare venue in which patients sought unscheduled care for an acute health problem.31,32

CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Embase, Global Health, Pubmed and Web of Science databases were searched without language restrictions from 1 January 1990. The date restriction was based on the development and earliest availability of self-testing approaches for HIV.33 Search protocols for each database used combinations of key words, free text terms, subject headings, subsets and exploded medical subject headings (MeSH) (Supplement 1). Reports of laboratory science, case reports, case studies, solely qualitative studies, reviews, meta-analyses and editorials were excluded. Non-English reports, reports that did not provide data on HIVST and those not from emergency care settings were also excluded.

Reference sections of included reports were manually reviewed for additional relevant records, with selected cited reports cross-referenced and assessed for eligibility. To evaluate for unpublished data from grey literature, digital conference proceedings from the International AIDS Society (years: 2001–2016), the Conference on Retroviruses and Opportunistic Infections (years: 2014–2020), the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene (years: 2004–2019), the Infections Disease Society of America (years: 2003–2017), the Society for Academic Emergency Medicine (years: 1990–2020), and the Research Forum of the American College of Emergency Physicians (years: 2004–2019) were electronically searched using key terms of “HIV self-testing” and “Emergency” and then screened based on the same criteria used in the main protocol. The primary authors of reports that met inclusion criteria were contacted to request any additional published or unpublished data relevant to the review.

Data Processing:

Search results were compiled, and duplicates were removed using EndNote X9™ reference management software (Clarivate Analytics; Philadelphia, USA). Titles and abstracts of records were screened for eligibility and two independent investigators reviewed the full texts using pre-specified criteria. Disagreements were adjudicated by an independent third investigator. Data were extracted using a pre-designed digital template for reports that met inclusion criteria. Data elements included: report characteristics, participant aspects, measures of HIVST acceptability and preferences, HIV testing program types and testing uptake outcomes based on program type. HIVST acceptability was coded using the categorizations provided from the source reports which used ordinal and Likert scale data in the domains of preference and willingness for self-testing and location for performance of tests. Forms of HIV testing programs used for both self-test and provider-delivered testing approaches were categorized. Additionally, data on linkage to HIV care services were extracted, if provided.

The quality of included reports were assessed via the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria. The GRADE approach was chosen to provide a unified evaluation of report quality among the study types which included both interventional and observational designs. GRADE quality assessments were completed by two independent study investigators with adjudication by a third investigator as needed. The investigators assessed for limitations in the quality of the reports based on five a priori GRADE characteristics for specific study designs and rated quality based on the structured GRADE approach.34 The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement was adhered to in reporting.35 The systematic review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (Number: CRD42020180262).

Statistical Analysis:

Analyses were performed using STATA v16.0 (StataCorp; College Station, USA). Inter-observer agreement for included reports was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ).36 Report characteristics and associated study populations were described using standard summary statistics. Measures of HIVST acceptability and efficacy were collated and analyzed within and across reports. For randomized controlled trials (RCT), analyses were performed consistent with the data provided from the source reports as either intention-to-treat or per-protocol. Proportional testing uptake and differences in testing uptake based on HIV program type (self-testing versus provider-delivered testing) were calculated for each report with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Meta-analyzed pooled results for acceptability and testing uptake were derived using random effects models with associated 95% CIs. For the efficacy outcome of testing uptake, risk ratios (RR) were calculated utilizing data from RCTs. A secondary sensitivity analysis was performed in which testing uptake was evaluated using both observational and RCT reports. Forest plots were derived from the meta-analytical models with statistical heterogeneity evaluated using I2 statistics. Publication bias was assessed graphically via funnel plots. As no included reports provided data on linkage to HIV care services no analyses pertaining to that efficacy outcome could be completed.

RESULTS

Characteristics of Included Reports:

The search strategy returned 5,773 reports and after review of titles and abstracts, 39 full-text reports were screened. From these reports seven met inclusion criteria for quantitative synthesis (Figure 1). No additional reports were identified via screening of conference proceedings, references of primary included reports or from contacting corresponding authors. Inter-observer agreement for report selection was very good (κ=0.84, 95% CI: 0.68–1.00).

The seven included reports were published between 2001 and 2020 and all were conducted in the United States of America (USA) from a single emergency care setting in Baltimore, Maryland.37–43 The cumulative sample across the seven reports was 1,942 subjects. Three reports were RCTs (42.9%),40,41,43 and the remaining four (57.1%) were prospective cross-sectional designs. All studies enrolled subjects ≥18 years of age. Two reports enrolled subjects determined to be at increased or high risk for HIV,40,41 and two enrolled subjects who had previously declined provider-delivered HIV testing (Table 1).40,43 HIV-ST and provider-delivered testing approaches varied across studies, and no data on HIVST costs to health systems or patients was provided in any of the reports (Supplement 2). Men accounted for a range of 34.0% to 47.6% of participants in reports that provided sex-specific data.37–39,41–43 Oral fluid based HIV assays were used as the self-testing modality, with three using the OraQuick Advance HIV1/2 test™,37,38,42 two using the OraQuick In-Home HIV Test™,41,43 and two studies not reporting the specific assay used.39,40 One report also used a blood-based HIV test (Unigold test™) in addition to the oral assay (Table 1).37

HIV Self-Testing Acceptability:

Four reports accounting for 1,388 subjects provided data on the acceptability of HIVST. For three reports that used Likert scales to assess HIVST acceptability, 95.4%, 96.9%, and 97.9% of subjects across the reports indicated that they would “definitely” or “probably” use a HIVST kit.37,38,42 A fourth report assessed the acceptability of HIVST before undergoing self-testing among 100 subjects declining provider-delivered HIV testing, and among an additional 100 subjects deemed to be at high-risk for HIV. In that report 74.5% were willing to use an HIVST kit (Table 1).40 The pooled prevalence of HIVST acceptability across all reports was 92.6% (95% CI: 88.0%−97.1%), with significant statistical heterogeneity (I2=94.5%, P<0.001) (Figure 2).

Two reports assessed whether subjects in emergency care settings preferred HIVST, provider-based testing, or had no preference.38,42 Among 710 cumulative subjects, 33.1% reported HIVST as the preferred modality, 36.2% preferred provider-based testing and 30.7% had no preference (Figure 3A). These two reports, and an additional third report, investigated subjects’ location preference for HIVST categorized as at home, in the ED/clinic or no preference.38,40,40 The ED/clinic settings was the most frequently identified preferred location at 42.3% (Figure 3B). In one report, 51.1% of subjects reported home as the preferred performance location for HIVST.42

HIV Self-Testing Efficacy:

HIVST uptake was assessed as an outcome in five reports, two RCTs and three observational studies, for a cumulative sample of 1,166 subjects (Table 1). The two RCTs had a total of 200 subjects who were randomized to HIVST or control groups. The control subjects received an informational pamphlet on the importance of HIV testing and the location for nearby testing facilities but were not provided access to HIVST in the ED.41,43 In the RCT by Kim et al., 64.7% of subjects in the HIVST arm reported HIV testing at 3 months of follow-up versus 18.9% of those randomized to the control arm.41 For the RCT by Patel et al., 48.3% of participants in the HIVST arm completed self-testing for HIV at 1-month follow-up as compared to 5.7% in the control arm.43 In pooled analysis, this resulted in a more than four-fold increase in HIV testing among participants randomized to HIVST as compared to controls (RR=4.41. 95% CI: 1.95–10.10) No significant heterogeneity or publication bias was observed in these results (I2=25.8%, P=0.246) (Figure 4A & 4B).

The three cross-sectional studies assessing HIVST uptake enrolled 1,064 subjects.38,39,42 In one report 96.1% performed an ED-based HIVST.42 In the two additional observational studies, which used electronic kiosk systems to implement the HIVST program, uptake of tests was 98.7% and 48.2% (Table 1).38,39 In sensitivity analysis including both interventional and observational reports ED-based HIVST programs resulted in a non-significant increase in testing uptake (RR=8.73, 95% CI: 0.49–155.7) (Supplement 2).

Quality of Evidence:

For the included RCTs the quality of evidence using the GRADE criteria was found to be very low for two reports.40,41,43 Lack of blinding and the use of unvalidated outcome measures were identified as deficiencies for all three RCT reports.40,41,43 For observational studies, three reports were found to be low or very low quality of evidence,37,38,42 and one report was moderate.39 The use of imprecise outcome measures was identified as a deficiency in all four observational reports,37–39,42 and three insufficiently controlled for confounders (Supplement 3).37,38,42

DISCUSSION

This systematic review demonstrates that HIVST programs in emergency care settings are acceptable to some patients and that programs could result in an increase in testing uptake based on the controlled interventional trial data. These results suggest that ED-based HIVST may represent an impactful approach which could augment testing provision, particularly among patients who have failed to be adequately engaged in HIV services, and who frequently only interface with health systems for emergent health needs.7–9 Although these findings align with WHO recommendations on expanding HIVST initiatives,25 the geographic and sampling limitations, as well as the methodological heterogeneity of the source reports, makes further study in more diverse emergency care settings necessary.

The acceptability for HIVST in the emergency care settings was high across individual reports, and in pooled analysis, found to be greater than 90%.37,38,40,42 This finding is congruent with evaluations of HIVST programs from other facility-based and community settings in which self-testing has been found to be highly acceptable by patients.26,27 As research has shown that self-testing approaches are perceived as easy and convenient, and also support greater patient autonomy as compared to provider-delivered testing these factors may also be driving acceptability of HIVST in emergency care settings.26,27 However, given the limited sampling frames among the included reports pertaining to the single study site and inclusions criteria for the populations, a more robust assessment of acceptability is needed to better characterize the external validity of these results. This is highlighted by the observed distribution of preferred testing locations in which the data demonstrated variability in preference for facility versus home-based settings, indicating that adaptive programs able to support self-testing within and external to emergency care facilities in conjunction with, and as an adjunct to provider-delivered testing may be the most comprehensive approach.38,42 Furthermore, as there were no data identified on acceptability from non-patient emergency care stakeholders, such as practitioners, administrators or policy makers, a more comprehensive assessment of ED-based HIVST programming is needed to understand challenges and opportunities across the full spectrum of actors. This aspect of staff acceptance is an area of interest which has been previously highlighted as a priority research focus for ED-based HIV programming in general,44 and understanding through an HIVST lens is as well key. As such future studies should take into account setting specific characteristics inclusive of HIV testing policies, resource availability and sociocultural expectations (of both patients and providers), and as such leveraging mixed quantitative and qualitative methods approaches will likely provide the most comprehensive insight into informing successful HIVST programs across these complex domains.

The meta-analysis of the RCTs on HIVST efficacy found a more than four-fold increase in testing uptake as compared to standard provider-delivered approaches among control group subjects.41,43 Additionally, the beneficial impacts on testing uptake among emergency care patients were maintained in the sensitivity analysis incorporating the observational data, though this finding was not statistically significant. As patient visitation for emergency and acute care is common, with approximately 145,000,000 encounters in the USA per year45, and data from low- and-middle-income countries (LIMCs) showing a median of 30,000 annual visits per facility,8 which is an order of magnitude greater than primary care encounters in similar settings,46 the ability to access large numbers of patients for the provision of HIV testing via emergency care venues is substantial. Furthermore, in LMICs where the majority of global HIV burdens are found,1 emergency care populations are largely comprised of younger populations and males,8,9,11 making improved provision of HIV testing during emergency care a potential prime opportunity to access these groups.11,47 As the reports included in this systematic review were carried out at a single site in the USA, and utilized variable HIVST programmatic approaches,37–43 there is a need to more robustly evaluate the impacts of ED-based HIVST to better ensure validity of impacts and setting appropriateness. Specifically, research in countries with high HIV prevalence, and hence the largest potential margins for benefits with early diagnosis at a populations level, should be undertaken.1,20,48 Furthermore, given the existing, and sometimes limited, infrastructure in emergency care settings, especially in LMIC, for provider-delivered HIV testing, implementation science research designed to evaluate integration of self-testing as an adjunctive path in existing HIV care cascades would likely provide the most actionable data to inform effective development and rollout of ED-based HIVST.

Linkage to HIV services among those with positive results in emergency care settings was an a priori outcome of interest; however no data were identified in the systematic search for that outcome. HIV testing and identification of PLHIV is a crucial step in the care cascade, but it is only a first step. In order for HIVST initiatives to be impactful in reaching global targets, confirmatory follow up HIV testing and, if indicated, linkage to care must be ensured.1,2,49 Although HIVST research has demonstrated encouraging rates of linkage to care and subsequent ART initiation in other high risk populations, and in high-burden LMICs, the generalizability of these linkage outcomes to ED-based populations with HIVST programing is not certain.50–53 Due to the unique characteristics of emergency care encounters, with shorter relative provider interactions and competing patient health needs, the best approaches to augment linkage to care are not yet known and represent an additional area in need of more robust and setting-specific data.

Limitations

This review employed rigorous methodologies in the systematic search, assessment of reports and meta-analysis; however, limitations do exist that require acknowledgement30,34,36,54. The inclusion criteria were restricted to English language, which could have resulted in selection bias. As highlighted, a substantial limitation was the single setting where all the included reports were derived from.37–43 Although this is not a barrier that could be addressed methodologically, this aspect does limit the generalizability of the findings to alternative settings with differing burdens of HIV, sociocultural aspects and resources. The majority of reports were also found to be of low or very-low quality which limits confidence in the overall results. The varying HIVST methods used across the included reports introduces clinical heterogeneity, the differing assessment approaches for acceptability and the lack of data on costs to patients, are limitations that must be considered in interpreting the conclusions around testing uptake. However, the beneficial impacts consistently observed across all identified reports do support efficacy in increasing testing uptake with ED-based HIVST. To address this, future studies should explicitly assess how differing HIVST programs implemented in emergency care settings impact outcomes. This could include the use of varying approaches to screening and HIVST delivery either through digital interfaces as has been used in other sexually transmitted infection research,55 or via more traditional patient-provider interactions. As well, the consideration of how ED-based HIVST programs best incorporate into the HIV-specific, and broader public health agendas from clinical applications and cost valuation perspectives in a given setting are key, as has been highlight by prior emergency care consensus recommendations.44

Although the HIVST assays used by the included reports are adequately accurate, self-testing does require follow up confirmation testing and no data was provided on this from the included reports. This limitation would not impact the primary efficacy outcome but is a key consideration for future studies to collect data on. All of the included reports selected study participants based on age and/or HIV risk criteria and as such the results may not be representative of overall emergency care populations in general. Unfortunately, the fidelity of data from the included reports was not sufficient to incorporate age or sex into the analyses to better explore impacts in these sub-groups, but this should be evaluated in future studies.

Conclusions

This systematic review provides the a comprehensive assessment of the available evidence pertaining to self-testing approaches for HIV in emergency care settings and demonstrates that HIVST is acceptable to some patients and may result in increased testing uptake. Although these findings support recommendations by the WHO on HIVST initiatives,25 and ED-based self-testing programs may serve as a mechanism to help reach global control targets by increasing testing among high-risk populations, further study is needed to better inform programmatic development and implementation. Future research should be broader in geographic representation, aim to provide a more robust characterization of challenges and opportunities across the full spectrum of emergency care stakeholders, evaluate best practices to integrate HIVST approaches into already existing ED-based HIV programming, and assess metrics of not only testing uptake but also confirmatory testing and linkage to HIV care.
