Abstract

BACKGROUND & AIMS:

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with a rapidly changing landscape of treatments. In the past 20 years, numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have aimed at improving outcomes across disease stages. We aimed to analyze the current evidence and identify potential factors influencing response to therapies.

METHODS:

We conducted a systematic review of phase III RCTs (2002–2020) across disease stages. A meta-analysis was designed to examine the relationship between etiology and outcome after systemic therapies with either tyrosine-kinase inhibitor (TKI)/antiangiogenic or immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy.

RESULTS:

Out of 10,100 studies identified, 76 were phase III RCTs. Among them, a rigorous screening algorithm identified 49 with high quality including a total of 22,113 patients undergoing adjuvant (n = 7) and primary treatment for early (n = 2), intermediate (n = 7), and advanced (first-line, n = 21; second-line, n = 12) stages of disease. Nine of these trials were positive, 6 treatments have been adopted in guidelines (sorafenib [2 RCTs], lenvatinib, atezolizumab+bevacizumab, regorafenib, cabozantinib and ramucirumab), but 2 were not (adjuvant CIK cells and sorafenib plus hepatic arterial infusion with FOLFOX). Meta-analysis of 8 trials including 3739 patients revealed ICI therapy to be significantly more effective in patients with viral hepatitis compared with nonviral-related HCC, whereas no differences related to etiology were observed in patients treated with TKI/anti–vascular endothelial growth factor.

CONCLUSIONS:

Among 49 high-quality RCTs conducted in HCC during 2002–2020, 9 resulted in positive results. A meta-analysis of systemic therapies suggests that immunotherapies may be more effective in viral etiologies.

Liver cancer is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally and its incidence is on the rise, with one million worldwide new cases projected annually in 2025.1 Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for the majority of primary liver cancers.1,2 Management of patients with HCC has adopted the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system in distinct guidelines.3,4 In 2003, a systematic review summarized evidence from phase III randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (January 1978 to May 2002)5 and reported 2 meta-analyses that established chemoembolization as the standard of care for intermediate-stage HCC and discarded tamoxifen as an effective therapy.5

Since then, there have been considerable advances in the management of HCC. Sorafenib was approved as a result of the SHARP trial (A Phase III Study of Sorafenib in Patients With Advanced Hepatocellular Carcinoma; NCT00105443) in 2007,6 and remained the only evidence-based treatment in advanced HCC for almost a decade. Recent years have seen the emergence of several practice-changing RCTs in advanced HCC, such as the establishment of lenvatinib as an alternative to sorafenib in the first line7 and regorafenib, cabozantinib, and ramucirumab in the second line after sorafenib.8–10 In addition, the combination of the anti–PD-L1 agent atezolizumab and the anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) bevacizumab has established the efficacy of immunotherapy in the field and has become the recommended first-line therapy.11 Indeed, the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) is revolutionizing care of HCC, and these drugs are currently being tested in combinations across disease stages. Nonetheless, very recent data suggested that ICIs are significantly less effective in patients with nonviral HCC, such as nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH).12

Methods

We followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) guidelines and check-list for the reporting of systematic reviews.13 These guidelines provide an evidence-based set of items aimed at improving quality of systematic reviews and meta-analyses that focus on RCTs. We followed the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) criteria for trial design and end points consensus conference to define appropriate selection criteria regarding target population and control arms.14

Study Objectives

The aims of this study were first to provide a thorough systematic review and analysis of high-quality phase III RCTs across HCC disease stages reported from 2002 to 2020, emphasizing aspects of trial design, inclusion criteria, and reasons for failure. To evaluate the current high-quality evidence, we reviewed the literature, critically assessed studies based on objective quality criteria (detailed in the Supplementary Material), and summarized the available data for the different tumor stages. Second, we aimed to assess the impact of different treatments on the survival of HCC patients by performing a meta-analysis of trials analyzing the same experimental treatment. And third, we sought to analyze the role of the underlying liver disease, particularly viral and nonviral etiologies, in outcomes related to tyrosine-kinase inhibitor (TKI)/monoclonal antibody and immune therapies.

Results

Our search captured 10,100 individual studies, of which 273 were identified as potentially relevant clinical trials evaluating treatment across different HCC stages from June 2002 to December 2020 (Figure 1). After removing non–phase III studies, 76 reports were selected to undergo quality assessment. Overall, 49 of them (64.5%) were high-quality trials including a total of 22,113 patients and were analyzed (Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Exclusion reasons for the remaining RCTs (Supplementary Table 3) are detailed in Supplementary Table 4. Quality assessment and details on study design for the included studies are presented in Supplementary Tables 5–14.

Early Stage

Treatment of early-stage disease incorporates 3 mainstay strategies: resection, liver transplantation,15 and local ablation (ie, radiofrequency ablation [RFA]). We identified 2 high-quality trials out of 8 total trials (25%) performed from 2002 to 2020 on curative therapy16,17 (Figure 1, Table 1, and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). These studies compared ablative therapies with the standard of care (RFA) in patients with unresectable early-stage tumors. One study compared cryoablation vs RFA for unresectable HCC with a maximum of 2 lesions ≤4 cm.17 The trial missed its primary end point of local tumor progression, an end point not recommended in a recent AASLD consensus statement14 (5.6% vs 10% in the cryoablation and RFA arms, respectively; P = 0.11). On the other hand, the HEAT trial (Phase 3 Study of ThermoDox with Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA) in Treatment of Hepatocellular Carcinoma; NCT00617981)16 failed to elicit a benefit of lyso-thermosensitive liposomal doxorubicin along with RFA vs RFA (median progression-free survival [mPFS] of 13.9 months in both arms) with an HR of 0.96 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.79–1.18; P = 0.71).

Adjuvant Treatments

To date no adjuvant strategy is recommended by American or European Guidelines.3,4 Our systematic review identified 7 high-quality trials out of a total of 15 trials (46.7%) testing adjuvant strategies (Figure 1, Table 1, and Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). The primary end point of all studies was recurrence-free survival (RFS).

Among adjuvant trials, only 1 open-label trial evaluating immunotherapy with CIK (cytokine-induced killer) cells after either percutaneous ethanol injection, RFA, or surgical resection was positive for its primary end point. In this Korean study, 230 patients were randomized to receive CIK cells during 16 cycles spanning 60 weeks, vs no therapy. The administration of CIK cells provided a 14-month RFS benefit over placebo (median 44 vs 30 months, respectively), with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.63 (95% CI 0.43–0.94; P = 0.01).18 Likewise, CIK cell therapy also improved overall survival (OS) with an HR of 0.21 (95% CI 0.06–0.75; P = 0.008), without increasing the rate of serious adverse events. Baseline characteristics were significantly unbalanced, with smaller tumors favoring the CIK arm (median size 1.8 vs 2.3 cm; P = 0.02).

Two trials tested adjuvant interferon (IFN) in patients undergoing resection.19,20 The first trial,19 comparing either IFN-α or control for 48 weeks in 150 patients, missed its primary end point (RFS after 45 months: 24.3% vs 5.8% in IFNα vs control arm; P = 0.49). Similarly, the second trial20 randomized 268 patients in 10 Taiwanese centers to either IFNα-2b or observation for 53 weeks and was also negative.

In 2011, a double-blinded RCT evaluating the use of adjuvant vitamin K2 after resection or ablation in 548 patients reported no differences in mRFS compared with placebo (17.8 months in both arms; HR 1.15 (95% CI 0.843–1.57).21 Similarly, the use of a synthetic retinoid, peretinoin (600 mg or 300 mg vs placebo), was negative for RFS (P = 0.434).22

Sorafenib was tested in the STORM (Sorafenib as Adjuvant Treatment in the Prevention of Recurrence of Hepatocellular Carcinoma; NCT00692770) trial23 under the hypothesis that the antitumoral effect could be harnessed to delay recurrence. In this double-blind placebo-controlled trial, 1114 patients were randomized at 202 centers to receive either sorafenib or placebo for a maximum of 4 years after resection or ablation. The trial did not meet the primary end point (mRFS 33.3 vs 33.7 months in sorafenib and control arms, respectively; HR 0.94,95% CI 0.78–1.134; P = 0.26) or the secondary end point of OS (HR 0.995, 95% CI 0.761–1.33; P = 0.48).23

In the 2016 SILVER (Immunosuppression in Patients Undergoing Liver Transplantation for Hepatocellular Carcinoma; NCT00355862) trial, 525 patients who underwent liver transplantation for HCC were randomized to compare an mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor sirolimus-based vs an mTOR inhibitor–free immunosuppressive regimen.24 Treatment with sirolimus did not improve the primary end point of RFS conducted after 8 years: RFS 70.2% vs 64.5%, respectively, HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.62–1.15 (P = 0.279).24

Intermediate Stage

The meta-analysis of 2003 provided the groundwork to establish chemoembolization as the standard of care for unresectable HCC5 and showed that tamoxifen therapy was futile. Since then, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) has become the standard treatment for patients in intermediate-stage HCC (BCLC B).5 We identified 7 high-quality trials out of 12 phase III studies (58.3%) in the intermediate stage setting that met our quality inclusion criteria (Figure 1, Table 2, and Supplementary Tables 9 and 10).25–31

A phase III trial evaluating tamoxifen alone or in combination with TACE did not meet the OS end point, with median OS (mOS) of 13.8 and 11 months, respectively.27 In a Japanese open-label trial including 161 patients, the injection of lipiodol plus zinostatin stimalamer to the hepatic artery with or without subsequent embolization did not observe differences in mOS (21.2 months with vs 22.3 months without embolization).25

Two high-quality phase III trials assessed sorafenib after TACE in Asian28 and European29 patients. The Asian trial randomized 458 patients with previous objective response to a first TACE cycle and led to a negative outcome for time to tumor progression (TTP; 5.4 vs 3.7 months in the sorafenib and control group; HR of 0.87 (95%CI 0.70–1.09). In the TACE2 UK trial,29 313 patients were randomized. The study was halted in a preplanned interim futility analysis because it did not meet the primary end point of mPFS (7.8 months vs 7.7 months in the treatment and control arms, respectively; HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.75–1.42). Median OS was low (~20 months in both arms), probably explained by the inclusion of BCLC C patients (symptomatic patients or with Vp1–Vp3 portal vein invasion). We conducted a meta-analysis of these 2 trials to assess OS and TTP, yielding negative results (Supplementary Figure 1). It should be noted, however, that the limited number of studies constrains the value of this meta-analysis.

The TKIs brivanib and orantinib also have been assessed in combination with TACE30,31 with negative results. The BRISK-TA trial comparing brivanib or placebo following TACE31 was terminated owing to negative phase III studies with brivanib in advanced-stage HCC. Results showed no improvement in mOS (26.4 vs 26.1 months for placebo; HR 0.9, 95%CI 0.66–1.2]). In the 2018 ORIENTAL trial, 889 patients were randomized in Asian centers to receive either orantinib or placebo following TACE.30 Orantinib failed to improve OS: 31.1 months vs 32.3 months (HR 01.09, 95% CI 0.878–1.352). Despite these negative results, both trials have provided a modern benchmark for OS rates (25–30 months) for intermediate-stage HCC treated with TACE. Finally, a Japanese trial tested the combination of conventional TACE with miriplatin vs epirubicin and failed to meet its primary end point.26

Advanced Setting—First-Line Treatment

The treatment of advanced HCC was limited to sorafenib for almost a decade.6 Our systematic review rendered a total of 26 first-line phase III trials in advanced HCC, 21 of which (80.8%) were considered to be of high quality (Figures 1 and 2, Table 3, and Supplementary Tables 11 and 12). Six of these trials were first-line placebo-controlled studies initiated before sorafenib approval.6,32–37 The seminal SHARP trial was a placebo-controlled double-blinded study that randomized 602 patients to sorafenib or placebo. It was the first trial to improve OS, with an HR of 0.69 (95% CI 0.55–0.87), and to provide an absolute increase in mOS of 2.8 months (10.7 vs 7.9 months, respectively).6 In parallel, the 271 patients in the Asia-Pacific trial attained a similar magnitude of benefit (HR of 0.68, 95% CI 0.5–0.93) but lower median survival (6.5 months) owing to recruitment of patients with more aggressive tumors32 (Supplementary Table 12). The 4 other high-quality placebo-controlled trials, testing octreotide,33,34 thymostimulin,36,37 and megestrol acetate,37 were negative.

After the positive SHARP trial, 3 out of 13 high-quality trials using sorafenib as control arm7,11,38–46 were positive according to the primary end point.7,11,38 First, lenvatinib was compared with sorafenib in the open-label REFLECT trial, which randomized 954 patients. The trial was positive regarding the primary end point of OS tested for noninferiority, with an HR of 0.92 (CI 95% 0.79–1.06) and a similar mOS (13.6 vs 12.3 months).7 Second, the Chinese 247-patient SoraHAIC open-label trial reported superior efficacy of the combination of sorafenib and hepatic intraarterial chemotherapy of oxaliplatin, fluorouracil, and leucovorin (ie, FOLFOX) vs sorafenib alone in advanced HCC with portal vein invasion (Vp1–Vp4),38 with an absolute increase of OS of 6.24 months (13.37 vs 7.13 months) and an HR of 0.35 (CI 95% 0.26–0.48).

Finally, the 2020 IMbrave150 trial (A Study of Atezolizumab in Combination With Bevacizumab Compared With Sorafenib in Patients With Untreated Locally Advanced or Metastatic Hepatocellular Carcinoma; NCT03434379)11 was a phase III open-label sorafenib-controlled trial that randomized 501 patients in a 2:1 fashion to atezolizumab-bevacizumab or sorafenib. While mOS in the sorafenib-treated control arm was 13.2 months, mOS was not reached in the atezolizumab-bevacizumab arm, although recently reported mature OS data revealed an mOS of 19.2 months.47 The study was halted at the interim analysis owing to positive results favoring the combination arm, with an HR of 0.58 (CI 95% 0.42–0.79).11 The co-primary end point of PFS was also positive, with an HR of 0.59 (95% CI: 0.47–0.76; P < .0001). In addition, patient-reported outcomes were significantly better for the combination compared with sorafenib alone (time to deterioration 11.2 months vs 3.6 months, respectively). Finally, objective response rate was significantly better in the combination arm vs sorafenib according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 and modified RECIST (27%–33% vs 12%–13%).

The remaining 10 trials that used sorafenib as a control arm showed no survival benefit, including the TKIs brivanib,42 sunitinib,41 and linifanib,40 as well as the combinations of sorafenib with erlotinib,48 doxorubicin,39 pravastatin,49 or TACE.46 The reasons behind these negative results are numerous and varied, including inappropriate trial design, marginal efficacy, and liver toxicity.50 SEARCH,48 PRODIGE-11,49 and CALGB 8080239 combined the experimental treatment (erlotinib, pravastatin, and doxorubicin, respectively) with sorafenib and compared this with sorafenib alone. Importantly, systemic doxorubicin showed no antitumor effect and was definitively discarded from the treatment armamentarium of advanced HCC.39 Similarly, the STAH trial46 tested the combination of TACE and sorafenib compared with sorafenib alone in advanced HCC in 339 Asian patients did not meet its primary end point (mOS 9.3 vs 9.4 months; HR 0.91, CI 95% 0.69–1.21).

Besides systemic drugs, 2 high-quality trials testing transarterial radioembolization (TARE) with yttrium-90 in first-line advanced HCC have been reported.43,44 The SARAH43 and SIRveNIB44 trials compared TARE with sorafenib alone in advanced HCC patients with no extrahepatic spread. Whereas the SARAH trial included mostly patients with BCLC stage C, more than 50% of the enrolled patients in the SIRveNIB trial presented with BCLC stage B. Neither SARAH nor SIRveNIB attained the end point of superiority of TARE vs sorafenib, with HRs of 1.15 (CI 95% 0.94–1.41) and 1.12 (CI 95% 0.9–1.4), respectively. In this regard, given the similar design and inclusion criteria of both trials in 819 patients, we performed a meta-analysis demonstrating no significant differences between sorafenib in both the intention-to-treat population (HR 1.14, CI 95% 0.98–1.32) and the per-protocol population (HR 0.92, CI 95% 0.79–1.07) (Supplementary Figure 2). Based on these results, TARE is not recommended as an alternative to systemic therapy in the advanced setting.

Finally, 2 high-quality trials included a suboptimal control arm (neither placebo nor sorafenib). Both trials were negative: comparing octreotide plus tamoxifen vs tamoxifen alone51 and FOLFOX4 vs doxorubicin.52

Advanced Setting—Second-Line Treatment

We identified 12 phase III trials in second-line treatment, all of which were considered to be of high quality, and all but 1 targeting patients solely progressing to sorafenib (Figures 1 and 2, Table 4, and Supplementary Tables 13 and 14).

The RESORCE study (Study of Regorafenib After Sorafenib in Patients With Hepatocellular Carcinoma; NCT01774344)8 was the first positive phase III study in second-line advanced HCC leading to an improvement in mOS from 7.8 months with placebo to 10.6 months with the multi-TKI inhibitor regorafenib (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.5–0.79). Of note, only patients who tolerated sorafenib were included in the trial. Two additional phase III clinical trials have been positive in the second-line setting: the CELESTIAL study in 2018 (Study of Cabozantinib [XL184] vs Placebo in Subjects With Hepatocellular Carcinoma Who Have Received Prior Sorafenib; NCT01908426),9 showing mOS of 10.2 months with the multi-TKI cabozantinib (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.63–0.92); and the REACH-2 trial in 2019 (A Study of Ramucirumab [LY3009806] Versus Placebo in Participants With Hepatocellular Carcinoma and Elevated Baseline Alpha-Fetoprotein; NCT02435433),10 demonstrating an mOS of 8.5 months with the VEGF receptor 2 monoclonal antibody ramucirumab in the poor-prognosis population of patients with α-fetoprotein (AFP) ≥400 ng/mL (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.53–095). Of note, the latter became the first positive biomarker-driven phase III trial for an enriched population in HCC. The rationale for selecting patients with high AFP serum levels came from a post hoc sub-group analysis of the negative REACH trial53 targeting all comers.

None of the remaining studies for second-line drugs were able to meet their primary end points. The BRISK-PS trial,54 reported in 2013, was the first trial to test an agent after progression or intolerance to sorafenib, and failed to show OS benefits despite obtaining significant improvements in secondary end points (TTP, objective response rate [ORR], and disease control rate). Likewise, the mTOR inhibitor everolimus did not significantly improve OS in the EVOLVE-1 trial.55 Standard chemotherapy with S-1, an oral fluoropyrimidine, was not superior to placebo in the 334-patient randomized double-blind Japanese S-CUBE trial.56 The RELIVE trial57 failed to show clinical efficacy with doxorubicin-loaded nanoparticles. Other strategies, such as degrading arginine external sources via an arginine deiminase enzyme, have been unsuccessful, as noted in the ADI-PEG 20 study.58 The METIV-HCC trial, was the first phase III study to incorporate a population enrichment based on positive immunohistochemical staining for Met. Tivantinib failed to show superiority compared with placebo (mOSs of 8.4 vs 9.1 months, respectively),59 probably owing to the low specificity of the drug as a Met inhibitor and its predominant tubulin-inhibitor activity.60 A similar negative result in JET-HCC phase III trial confirmed the futility of tivantinib in Met-high tumors.61

Single-arm phase II studies targeting PD-1 inhibitors showed ORRs around 15%–20% in the second-line setting,62,63 thus priming the design of the phase III trial KEYNOTE-240 comparing pembrolizumab vs placebo. The trial did not reach its co-primary end points of superiority in OS (HR 0.781, 95% CI 0.611–0.998) and PFS (HR 0.775, 95% CI 0.609–0.987).64 Although the P value was below commonly used thresholds (P = 0.0238), it did not hit the pre-specified statistical criteria for superiority (P = 0.0174) in the final analysis.

Population Disparities and Influence of Disease Etiology in Advanced HCC Trials

Inclusion criteria were not homogeneous across trials testing systemic therapies (see Supplementary Tables 15–17). For example, main portal vein invasion (Vp4) was specifically excluded from REFLECT and KEYNOTE-240, a feature that must be taken into consideration because untreated Vp4 patients show a dismal prognosis of only 3–5 months.65,66 Regarding BCLC staging, all trials testing systemic therapies accepted patients with BCLC B progressing to locoregional therapies, except for SoraHAIC, which targeted only patients with portal vein invasion.

Nonviral HCC etiologies (ie, NASH and alcohol) have been associated with significantly decreased response and survival with ICI therapies compared with viral etiologies.12 However, it is uncertain if this difference is unique to ICI therapies or is also found in other treatment modalities, such as TKI or anti-VEGF therapies. Therefore, we performed a meta-analysis to assess the impact of molecular and immune therapies in outcome according to HCC etiology. To enhance the power for this specific analysis, we performed an additional manual search of phase III interventional HCC studies registered at clinicaltrials.gov and also searched for conference abstracts (Figure 1). This strategy identified 81 phase III trials, from which 17 were already included in the systematic review as published papers. Four studies were found reported in abstract form, but only 1—CheckMate 45967—was eligible for final inclusion.

Therefore, in terms of etiology-based meta-analysis, among a total of 34 RCTs (33 full papers, 1 abstract) assessing systemic therapies, 8 were finally included (Figure 1), 5 assessing TKI/anti-VEGF (REACH, REACH-2, METIV-HCC, CELESTIAL, and JET-HCC; a total of 2083 patients) and 3 assessing immunotherapies (CheckMate-459, IMbrave150, and KEYNOTE-240; a total of 1656 patients). No specific meta-analysis investigating the role of etiology in sorafenib-treated patients was performed, because the reporting of the etiology-specific stratified HR was heterogeneous in those trials and could not be combined in a meta-analysis.

The pooled HR for OS in patients with viral-related HCC treated with ICIs was 0.64 (95% CI 0.5–0.83) compared with those in the standard-care group. Conversely, the pooled HR for OS in patients with nonviral-related HCC treated with ICIs was 0.92 (95% CI 0.77–1.11). The difference in efficacy between viral- and nonviral-related HCC treated with ICIs was significant (P of heterogeneity = 0.0259) (Figure 3A). These results were supported by a sensitivity analysis (Figure 3B). Importantly, the effect of ICIs was remarkably similar in hepatitis B virus– and hepatitis C virus–related HCC (HR 0.64 [95% CI 0.49–0.83] vs HR 0.68 [95% CI 0.47–0.98], respectively) (Supplementary Figure 3).

No impact of etiology in outcome was observed with TKI/anti-VEGF therapies. Indeed, the HRs for OS were 0.81 (95% CI 0.71–0.92) in patients with viral etiologies and 0.82 (95% CI 0.67–1.01) in patients with nonviral etiologies . The difference in efficacy was not statistically significant (P = 0.8828) (Figure 3 and Supplementary Figure 3).

Discussion

The present study provides an up-to date, evidence-based, systematic review of all high-quality RCTs reported from mid-2002 to December 2020, encompassing all HCC disease stages. This work is based on a rigorous quality assessment and thoroughly described the trial design, inclusion criteria, characteristics, control arms, and outcomes. Among a total of 10,100 studies identified, we analyzed 49 high-quality phase III studies including a total of 22,113 patients amenable for analysis. The systematic review provides insight regarding 9 positive studies, among which 7 RCTs established the accepted drugs for advanced HCC. Results of 2 Asian studies at early stage have not been adopted in international guidelines, as discussed below. Finally, in the setting of a meta-analysis of pooled data, we observed 1) that immunotherapies are less effective in nonviral etiologies than in viral etiologies; importantly, this effect was not observed with TKI/anti-VEGF therapies, suggesting that this feature was unique to ICI; 2) the lack of efficacy of TARE compared with sorafenib in advanced HCC, and 3) the lack of benefit of adding TACE to sorafenib in intermediate-stage HCC.

At early stages of the disease, 2 trials assessing primary therapies for BCLC 0/A HCC were negative for establishing the superiority of cryoablation or RFA plus thermosensitive doxorubicin vs RFA alone.23,24 It is noteworthy that pre-specified inclusion criteria allowed for the enrollment of patients with a tumor burden exceeding early-stage disease.

Adjuvant therapies after resection or local ablation are a major unmet medical need.14 Seven high-quality RCTs were included in the present analysis, but only 1 met its primary end point. Adoptive immunotherapy with CIK cells, generated by incubation of patients’ peripheral blood mononuclear cells with interleukin-2 and an antibody against CD3, was reported to improve RFS compared with placebo.18 The study has major caveats, including the fact that it was an open-label trial randomizing patients to no intervention and that the inclusion criteria allowed the enrollment of multinodular tumors up to 5 cm in size. Nevertheless, the concept of boosting effector cells of the immune system to reduce recurrence may be a viable strategy in the backdrop of emerging immunotherapeutic drugs. In fact, immunotherapies are currently dominating the strategies to prevent recurrence after curative techniques.1 Similarly, immunotherapy is increasingly explored in a neoadjuvant setting,68 where the rationale is to use the viable tumor to prime effector cells that can target micrometastatic disease. A pilot study has been reported in this arena.69

In intermediate-stage HCC, TACE has been reported to provide objective response rates of 50%–60% and median survival rates of 24 to 30 months.70 Several RCTs assessed combination of molecular therapies with TACE. Sorafenib did not improve survival in this setting in 2 phase III trials.28,29 Similarly, other TKIs, such as brivanib31 and orantinib,30 failed to improve patients’ outcomes. All of these trials showed that adding TKIs to TACE is feasible and safe, but not effective. Lack of efficacy might be related to the heterogeneity of targeted populations in the BCLC B stage, suboptimal treatments explored, or the need for surrogate end points beyond survival. BCLC B is a heterogeneous class, encompassing patients with small tumor burden with prognoses resembling those of BCLC A patients as well as individuals with large tumor burden and worse outcome. Therefore, it is likely that treatment interventions may benefit only a subset of patients. In parallel, new end points might help in elucidating which patients benefit from combination therapies with TACE. Surrogate end points might represent a cleaner measure of a given effect, because OS can be affected by subsequent therapies, especially in early and intermediate disease. Indeed, a recent consensus conference report has adopted PFS as co-primary end point in patients with intermediate HCC,14 whereas other end points, such as time to unTACEable progression,71 were not considered to be surrogate measurements of survival.

After the revolution triggered by the SHARP trial,6 the first relevant advancement in the first-line setting was the emergence of lenvatinib as an alternative to sorafenib (Figure 2). More recently, the treatment scenario dramatically changed following the positive results of the IMbrave150 trial.11 This breakthrough trial established the combination of atezolizumab plus bevacizumab as the first-line treatment option in patients with no contraindication for any of the regimens and in the absence of untreated esophageal varices as assessed by means of endoscopy 6 months before initiation of treatment. Moreover, 3 new drugs have proven efficacy for patients with progression to sorafenib: regorafenib, cabozantinib, and ramucirumab. Overall, mOS for advanced HCC has been progressively expanded in the past decade owing to 1) the concurrent use of subsequent therapies administered beyond progression (up to w50%–60% of cases72 (Supplementary Table 18), 2) a plausible better management of side-effects to TKIs gained through these years (see improvements in sorafenib outcome from the initial 10.7 months to 13–14 months in recently published trials11,67 despite similar baseline characteristics), and 3) a switch of the target patient population to earlier stages owing to improved surveillance programs.

A study evaluating sorafenib plus intraarterial FOLFOX (SoraHAIC) was positive in the first-line treatment in patients with portal vein thrombosis,38 but issues were concerning about the final outcomes. First, a total of 818 patients were screened and assessed for eligibility, but only 247 patients were randomized. Among excluded patients, almost 60% declined to participate in this open-label trial, and chose other treatment modalities. Second, some patients achieving objective response were allowed to undergo resection, which occurred significantly more often in the SoraHAIC group compared with the sorafenib group (12.8% vs 0.8%; P < .001). Finally, the results were not confirmed by a parallel SILIUS trial45 with a similar design (using a combination of low-dose cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil instead of FOLFOX). Further research is needed to clarify the role of hepatic intraarterial chemotherapy in the management of HCC, which so far is not accepted in Western guidelines.6,7

The SHARP trial6 established the pillars of trial design in HCC based on 3 concepts14: selection of patients with preserved liver function to prevent competing risks of death, selection according to the BCLC criteria, and defining OS as the primary end point. Results from recent phase III trials have shed light on issues related to trial design that complement these fundamental pillars. First, as the increase in therapeutic options for subsequent treatment may mitigate any difference observed between study arms, achieving positive outcomes in survival will likely require a disproportionately higher drug potency. PFS has shown a correlation with OS at trial level (R = 0.84)73 and has been proposed as surrogate end point of OS. For this reason, a surrogate threshold effect of HR ≤0.6 based on the analysis of 27 RCTs is proposed to be highly predictive of a significant improvement in OS,14,73 although a cutoff of 0.61–0.7 defines uncertainty and >0.7 a lack of OS benefit. On the other hand, objective response has been independently associated with better OS at individual level,74 but the levels of surrogacy are low, probably because the current level of ORR (<30%) for systemic therapies does not suffice to provide a strong surrogacy of OS. Second, several trials have underscored the importance of balancing treatment efficacy and toxicity, epitomized by the results of the SUN1170 trial.41 Third, performing rigorous phase I/II trials thoroughly exploring safety is crucial to improve the assessment of risk in phase III investigations (eg, GI endoscopy in the IMbrave150 study11).

Our subgroup meta-analysis suggests that immunotherapy is less effective in nonviral etiologies than in viral-related HCC, which can be associated with an increased immunogenicity of viral-derived neopeptides.75 Recent evidence suggests that NASH-related HCC is less responsive to ICIs owing to the presence of a dysfunctional infiltration of CD8+PD1+ T cells.12 These findings are in line with the observation that obesity induces metabolic changes in the tumor microenvironment leading to CD8+ T-cell impairment.76 Thus, additional studies to decipher these mechanisms in viral-, alcohol-, and NASH-related HCC are warranted.

We acknowledge some limitations of our meta-analysis. First, we rely on pooled data and not on individual patient data. Second, specific data on NASH- and alcohol-related HCC were not available among nonviral etiologies. Finally, the trials included are heterogeneous, comprising different lines of treatment compared with distinct standards of care, namely, sorafenib for first-line therapies and placebo for second-line. Nevertheless, we do not think this affected our results, because we aimed at assessing whether the efficacy of immunotherapies was significantly heterogeneous according to etiology and conducted a stringent sensitivity analysis with 4 alternative methods yielding similar results.

Nowadays, other trials are ongoing comparing combination regimens of TKIs plus immunotherapies or 2 immunotherapy strategies. Most of these novel combination regimens are tested in properly designed high-quality RCTs, and thus results will be instrumental in expanding the landscape of therapies for this highly lethal disease.

WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW

BACKGROUND AND CONTEXT

The treatment landscape of HCC is rapidly evolving, and new therapies have emerged in recent years. A comprehensive systematic review of all high-level trials from 2002–2020 may help to shape the evidence-based management of this lethal disease.

NEW FINDINGS

We provide a comprehensive overview of 49 high-quality phase III randomized controlled trials, among which 9 rendered positive results. Meta-analysis revealed a unique link between outcomes after checkpoint inhibitor therapy and the etiology of the underlying liver disease.

LIMITATIONS

The meta-analysis of 8 phase III RCTs is constrained by the use of trial-level data rather than individual patient–level assessment. In addition, in 2 RCTs the standard of care comparator was sorafenib (first-line studies), whereas in 6 cases it was placebo (second-line studies).

IMPACT

Our data provide a thorough characterization of high-quality RCTs conducted in HCC for the past 2 decades. Findings from a meta-analysis support the etiology-based stratification of patients in immunotherapy-based trials.
