Risk factors for monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance: a systematic review

[http://orcid.org/0000-0002-2376-7656] Maira A. Castaneda-Avila[1]

Christine M. Ulbricht[1]

Mara Meyer Epstein[1][2]

[1] Department of Population and Quantitative Health Sciences, University of Massachusetts Medical School, Worcester, MA, USA

[2] Meyers Primary Care Institute and the Department of Medicine, University of Massachusetts Medical School, 365 Plantation Street, Biotech 1, Suite 100, Worcester, MA 01605, USA

Author notes:

Correspondence to: [✉] Mara Meyer Epstein, mara.epstein@umassmed.edu

Abstract

Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), precursor of multiple myeloma, is an asymptomatic plasma cell disorder that overproduces serum monoclonal protein. Older age, male sex, black race, and family history of MGUS increase the risk of MGUS, yet other risk factors are known. We systematically reviewed observational epidemiological studies that examined sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral risk factors for the development of MGUS. The protocol for this study was registered on the PROSPERO registry for systematic reviews. We identified epidemiological studies from PubMed and Scopus. Articles were limited to those written in English and published before February 2019. Five case-control and three cohort studies were eligible for data extraction. Studies evaluating factors associated with MGUS risk are limited, with conflicting conclusions regarding risk associated with obesity. Despite the limited research, a significant elevated risk for being diagnosed with MGUS was associated with several specific prior infections, inflammatory disorders, and smoking. The sparse existing literature suggests an increased risk of MGUS associated with several risk factors related to immune function. Further research is needed to explore the potential mechanisms underlying the development of MGUS and to confirm risk factors, both modifiable and non-modifiable.

Introduction

Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is a disorder of the plasma cell overproducing monoclonal protein (M protein) [1]. Patients with MGUS are usually asymptomatic; therefore, MGUS is often incidentally diagnosed by blood tests ordered for other medical concerns [2]. MGUS is the first pathogenic step in the development of multiple myeloma (MM) [3–5], and patients with MGUS are also at an increased risk of developing other lymphoproliferative conditions [6]. Each year, approximately 1% of patients with MGUS progress to MM [7]. The etiology of MGUS is to a large extent unknown. However, a few risk factors have been identified that are associated with the development of MGUS. The known risk factors for MGUS are largely non-modifiable and include older age [8], male sex [9], black race [10, 11], and family history of MGUS and related diseases [12].

Previous systematic reviews evaluated the prevalence of MGUS, and the evidence for potential associations between MGUS and previous autoimmune disease [13], family history [14], and occupational/environmental risk factors [8]. A previous systematic review related to MGUS risk factors [8] found that MGUS prevalence is different among categories defined by race, sex, family history, immunosuppression, and pesticide exposure and increases with age [8]. In addition, a previous systematic review of three studies found that autoimmune diseases and their treatment were associated with MGUS [13].

Recent studies suggest that patients with MGUS may have shorter life spans and higher morbidity than the general population [15–20]. However, studies have also observed improved survival of MM patients who previously received an MGUS diagnosis [15, 21]. In the current study, we aim to present a more comprehensive review focused on a broad range of MGUS risk factors. Identifying risk factors for MGUS may help elucidate the natural history and underlying mechanisms of this disease, provide insight into the mechanisms behind the improved survival of MM patients who previously received an MGUS diagnosis [15, 21], and further identify people at high risk for MGUS. In this review, we systematically describe and evaluate the literature examining the risk factors for MGUS based on findings from the published literature, focusing on sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral risk factors. This review will focus only on factors associated with MGUS, excluding papers that evaluated risk factors associated with the transition to multiple myeloma.

Methods

Data sources and searches

This review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Supplemental Table 1) [22]. The protocol for this study was registered on the PROSPERO registry for systematic reviews (CRD42019125799) [23]. Searches on PubMed® and Scopus® electronic databases were performed in February 2019 to identify relevant articles. Text headings and medical subject heading (MeSH) terms used in the searches included MGUS, paraproteinemia, monoclonal, gammopathy, monoclonal gammopathy, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, and risk (Supplemental Table 2). In addition, we reviewed the reference lists of identified papers for potentially relevant studies.

Study selection

Abstracts and available full texts of articles resulting from the search were reviewed by one investigator (M.A.C.A) to determine which articles assessed risk factors for the development of MGUS. This review included studies that (1) investigated the association between MGUS and potential risk factors; (2) used a cohort or case-control design; (3) were written in English; (4) were published in a peer-reviewed journal or as a conference paper; and (5) were published before February 2, 2019. Excluded studies did not meet the inclusion criteria or were a case study/series, abstract (with no accompanying full text), review article, or cross-sectional study.

Data extraction

One investigator (M.A.C.A) extracted information pertaining to the association between MGUS and sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral risk factors. M.A.C.A reviewed all publication titles and abstracts to eliminate articles that covered a subject not relevant to this review and determined inclusion for assessment of the full text. One investigator (M.A.C.A.) extracted data onto a standardized data collection form. Extracted information included study title and authors, year of publication, source of participants and sample size, mean age and gender composition of the study sample, risk factor(s) evaluated, and effect estimates with accompanying 95% confidence intervals. Extracted information and data were then reviewed by another author (M.M.E.).

Risk of bias

We used questions adapted from the 32-item checklist developed by Downs and Black to assess the methodological quality both of randomized and non-randomized studies of healthcare interventions [24]. The adapted 17-item checklist was previously used in other systematic reviews, where items irrelevant to observational studies not studying an intervention as the primary exposure were removed [25]. We utilized this adapted checklist to assess the quality of abstracted information, including the clarity of reporting study methods, error, and bias (Supplemental Table 3).

Data synthesis

The articles included in this systematic review assessed a broad range of risk factors for MGUS grouped into three categories (sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral). Because the studies were heterogeneous, we could not conduct a metaanalysis. Instead we provide a descriptive synthesis of the results obtained from the studies included in this review.

Results

We retrieved 4189 studies from keyword searches in PubMed and 833 studies through Scopus. After excluding duplicate studies (n = 529), the preliminary search yielded 4493 articles for title review, 99 articles for abstract review, and 56 articles for full-text review. Twenty-three book chapters, commentaries, editorials, meeting abstracts, case series, review articles, and cross-sectional studies were excluded. Studies which failed to examine risk factors for MGUS (n = 6); did not evaluate sociodemographic, clinical, and/or behavioral risk factors (n = 8); and studies that focused on the progression to MM (n = 11) were also excluded. A total of eight articles satisfied our eligibility criteria and were included in this review (Fig. 1) [26–33].

MGUS study characteristics

The eligible articles were published between 1996 and 2018 (described in Table 1). Five case-control and three cohort studies were reviewed. The studies were conducted in the USA (n = 2) [27, 28], the UK (n = 1) [26], Sweden (n = 1) [29], Italy (n = 2) [30, 31], and Iceland (n = 2) [32, 33]. Of the studies included in this review, only one had a sample size of less than 1000 participants [30], and two were performed in only men or women [27, 28]. Two analyses conducted in Iceland used the same study population [32, 33]. MGUS case identification criteria used in each study are shown in Table 2. MGUS risk factors were grouped into three categories: sociodemographic, medical/clinical, and behavioral. Studies are described in detail in the following sections.

Sociodemographic factors

In addition to established MGUS risk factors including male sex [9], African American race [10, 11], and age older than 50 years [8], Pasqualetti et al., [30] in a hospital-based case-control study among MGUS cases from Italy, evaluated the association of socioeconomic status (SES) with risk of MGUS. No significant (P > 0.05) association with MGUS risk was observed for SES and residence [30]. Moreover, a cohort study by Landgren et al. [28] evaluated if obesity and race are related to MGUS risk, using a subset of patients who had serum samples from the US population-based Southern Community Cohort Study (SCCS). This study was conducted among women only, with almost three-quarter of the women not attending any college and more than half having annual household incomes less than $15,000 [28]. Factors such as low education status (attended vs not attended any college) and annual household income (<$15,000) were assessed as proxy markers for SES. They found no association between MGUS risk and low education status (OR: 1.83, 95% CI: 0.88–3.84) and low annual household income (OR: 1.16, 95% CI: 0.65–2.08) after adjusting for age, race/ethnicity, education status, household size, obesity, and annual household income (Fig. 2) [28].

Clinical and medical conditions

In the Italian case-control study by Pasqualetti et al. [30], a positive association (OR: 1.47, 95% CI: 1.09–1.97) was reported between overall chronic antigenic stimulation with the risk of MGUS (Supplemental Table 4). Chronic antigenic stimulation was defined as the presence of any of the following conditions: allergies, chronic bronchitis, chronic hepatitis, collagen-vascular diseases, frequent infections, glomerulonephritis, inflammatory bowel disease, peptic ulcer disease, prostatitis, pyelonephritis, skin disease, thyroiditis, and urinary tract infections. However, they did not find any individual condition to be associated with MGUS risk (P > 0.05). A later cohort study by Brown et al. [27] among male US Veterans, using a computerized inpatient records, evaluates the role of specific prior autoimmune, infectious, inflammatory, and allergic disorders in the etiology of MGUS. The authors report significantly elevated risk of MGUS associated with infectious and inflammatory disorders, but not allergies. They also found that specific prior infectious disorders caused by bacteria or viruses (pneumonia, hepatitis, meningitis, septicemia, herpes zoster, and poliomyelitis) and inflammatory disorders (glomerulonephritis, nephrotic syndrome, and osteoarthritis) were significantly associated with MGUS (Fig. 3, Supplemental Table 4). Brown et al. also found that history of pneumonia 5 years prior MGUS diagnosis was a predictor of MGUS risk (RR: 1.72, 95% CI: 1.53–1.93; Supplemental Table 4) [27]. Furthermore, a Swedish case-control study also evaluated the association between infections, and inflammatory conditions with MGUS development include personal and family history of each condition [29]. They found that a personal history of infections (OR: 1.6, 95% CI: 1.5–1.7) and inflammatory conditions (OR: 1.4, 95% CI: 1.2–1.5) was associated with a significantly increased risk of MGUS (Fig. 3). The Swedish study also found that family history of autoimmune disease was associated with a significant increased risk of MGUS (OR: 1.1; 95% CI: 1.0–1.2; Supplemental Table 4) [29].

Obesity has been inconsistently linked to an increased risk of MGUS despite it being the only modifiable risk factor for MM recently recognized by the World Health Organization [34, 35]. As described earlier, Landgren et al. [28] screened black and white women of similar SES for MGUS to evaluate the relationship between MGUS risk with obesity and race. They found that after adjusting for age, race/ethnicity, education status, household size, and annual household income, MGUS was twice as common among obese versus non-obese women independent of race (OR: 1.80, 95% CI: 1.03–3.14; Fig. 3).

Concerns about whether reported associations between metabolic risk factors and MGUS risk were true associations or the result of detection bias led Boursi et al. [26] to evaluate metabolic risk factors such as obesity, diabetes, and antidiabetic therapies, taking into account the number of laboratory tests conducted prior to the MGUS diagnosis. They conducted a nested case-control study using the Health Improvement Network (THIN), a large primary care database in the UK [36]. Cases were defined as incident diagnoses of MGUS (N = 2363). For every case, four eligible controls, matched by age, sex, practice site, and duration of followup, were selected (N = 9193). Obesity (OR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.02–1.29) and diabetes (OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.13–1.50) were associated with an increased MGUS risk after adjusting for diabetes, smoking, obesity, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, and autoimmune disease. However, after adjustment by the number of laboratory tests prior to index date, they no longer found an association with these factors [obesity (OR: 1.08, 95% CI: 0.96–1.22) and diabetes (OR: 1.08, 95% CI: 0.93–1.25)] (Fig. 3). Although not statistically significant, there was a suggestive positive association between obesity and MGUS for females (OR: 1.21, 95% CI: 0.96–1.53) but not for males (OR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.63–1.06), which is trending towards an inverse association. In addition, insulin and oral antidiabetic drugs were evaluated, and they found a nonsignificant decrease in MGUS risk among diabetes patients (Supplemental Table 4). Landgren et al. [28] also evaluated diabetes, but it was not found to be associated with an increased risk of MGUS in that population [28].

With the objective of disentangling the inconsistent findings of the association between obesity and MGUS, Thordardottir et al. [32] conducted a study aimed at determining if obesity is associated with a higher risk of MGUS in an Icelandic population. This cohort study found no association between MGUS and eleven baseline obesity measures including weight, BMI, percent body fat, body fat, total body fat, visceral fat, subcutaneous fat, and two measures of abdominal circumference. This is in contrast to the study by Landgren et al. [28] who found a twofold increased risk of MGUS in obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) vs non-obese women [28]. Both studies relied only on BMI as a marker for obesity (Fig. 3).

Behavioral factors

Pasqualetti et al. [30] evaluated the association between smoking and the risk of MGUS in their Italian case-control study. A 40% (95% CI: 1.03–1.89) higher odds of having MGUS among participants who reported current tobacco use was observed. In contrast, Pasqualetti et al. [31] in their later matched case-control study found that only heavy smokers present a significant increased risk for MGUS suggesting a strong relationship with the amount smoked. Boursi et al. [26] also evaluated smoking as a positive control in their study and found a higher risk of MGUS among smokers (OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.18–1.44) compared to never smokers.

Finally, the only study to date that evaluates the effect of diet on MGUS was a recent population-based analysis in Iceland led by Thordardottir et al. [33] They found that an intake of fruit at least three times per week during adolescence (14–19 years old) was associated with a lower risk of MGUS when compared to lower fruit consumption (OR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.41–0.95), suggesting that adolescent fruit intake may reduce the risk of MGUS later in life. This population-based analysis also observed an association between midlife intake of whole wheat bread at least five times per week and a lower risk of MGUS when compared to lower wheat bread intakes. No other association between dietary factors and MGUS risk achieved statistical significance (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The results of this systematic review suggest that there is evidence of an increased risk for MGUS associated with certain sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral factors, although studies evaluating these factors are limited and have inconsistent findings. Notwithstanding, two studies found that an elevated risk for being diagnosed with MGUS was associated with several specific prior infectious disorders and inflammatory disorders. In addition, three studies evaluated smoking, finding similar positive results with MGUS risk. Only one study reported a reduction in MGUS risk associated with diet, specifically high intake of fruit during adolescence and whole wheat bread during midlife, suggesting that additional studies into the role of diet in MGUS pathogenesis are warranted.

Males [9], African Americans [10, 11], and adults older than 50 years old are known to be at a higher risk of developing MGUS [8]. However evidence relating to other risk factors is not well studied. It has been suggested that overall chronic antigenic stimulation may play an important role in inducing MGUS [30]. Furthermore, the elevated risk of MGUS associated with prior bacterial or viral infections, and also in immunocompromised patients, may be a manifestation of underlying immune disturbances that may be a trigger for MGUS development [27].

Approximately one-half of MGUS patients have clonal plasma cells carrying translocations that involve a locus involved in the initiation and support of clonal proliferation [29]. It has previously been proposed that infections could be the trigger event for these translocations and thereby lead to clonal proliferation of plasma cells; findings from two different populations support this potential hypothesis [27, 29]. MGUS occurs more frequently in those who are immunocompromised, such as patients who are infected with HIV [8]. An alternative explanation for the observed associations between immune-related conditions and MGUS could be reverse causality. It is possible that immune-related conditions are markers of immune dysregulation, which may manifest as a result of a nascent plasma cell disorder. Most associations reported between MGUS and inflammatory and autoimmune diseases maintained statistical significance when the condition was diagnosed more than 5 years before the diagnosis of a plasma cell disorder, suggesting that certain infections and inflammatory conditions likely precede the development of MGUS or may be an early sign of immune system dysfunction [27].

The behavioral risk factors identified in this systematic review to be related to MGUS development include smoking status and some dietary factors. It seems that smoking, and in particular heavy smoking, is associated with an increased risk of MGUS; however, to date, there is no convincing evidence of an association between smoking and MM [37]. Potential explanations include that heavy smoking could stimulate the immune system, and the chronic antigenic stimulation could subsequently increase the risk of MGUS [30]. The literature does not currently support obesity as a risk factor for MGUS; however, evidence suggests that obesity may play a role in the transformation from MGUS to MM. Thordardottir et al. [32] argue that BMI is not the best measure of obesity as it does not provide information on fat distribution and abdominal obesity [38]. There is not enough evidence in the literature to confirm the role of obesity in the development of MGUS. Future research should take in considerations that obesity consists of many different components, both physiological and behavioral, and could therefore be a proxy for these factors that might independently influence MGUS risk. Differences in geography, sex, study design, and race distribution, as well as differences in recruitment of participants and case identification methods, might explain the discrepancy between the studies reviewed here. There is a higher probability of detecting MGUS among patients being treated for other medical conditions that require more frequent medical interactions compared to the general population. Therefore, it is possible that detection bias may be present in previously reported associations of various factors for MGUS development, since MGUS is not screened for at the population level and is often discovered incidentally through routine blood tests. Further, the lack of precision in estimates among some of the risk factors suggests that additional research in larger prospective, population-based studies is needed to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms of MGUS pathogenesis. In particular, studies are needed to investigate the possible role of early and midlife exposures since MGUS has been detected, although rarely, in individuals as young as 10–19 years old [39]. In addition, it is important to consider that there could be a genetic component involved in MGUS development, although genetic factors were not included in this review.

Conclusions

In summary, the existing literature, although sparse, suggests an increased risk of MGUS associated with some modifiable risk factors that are potential triggers for MGUS incidence or represent underlying conditions resulting from or existing alongside MGUS. These results also highlight the need to further explore the biological mechanisms involved in the association of sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral factors with MGUS risk. Future studies should replicate reported associations in other study populations, and with prospective study designs, where risk factor assessment clearly precedes MGUS diagnosis. Moreover, the use of novel case identification methods that allow for the efficient identification of true MGUS cases, such as algorithms applied to electronic health data sources, including health claims and electronic health record data is encouraged [40]. Studies of risk factors for MGUS are necessary to better understand the etiology of this understudied premalignant condition and to identify high-risk groups who might benefit from closer clinical surveillance.
