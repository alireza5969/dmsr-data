Abstract

Brain development is a dynamic and lengthy process that includes cell proliferation, migration, neurogenesis, gliogenesis, synaptogenesis, and pruning. Disruption of any of these developmental events can result in long-term outcomes ranging from brain structural changes, to cognitive and behavioral abnormality, with the mechanisms largely unknown. Emerging evidence suggests non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) as pivotal molecules that participate in normal brain development and neurodevelopmental disorders. NcRNAs such as long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) are transcribed from the genome but not translated into proteins. Many ncRNAs have been implicated as tuners of cell fate. In this review, we started with an introduction of the current knowledge of lncRNAs and miRNAs, and their potential roles in brain development in health and disorders. We then reviewed and discussed the evidence of ncRNA involvement in abnormal brain development resulted from alcohol, anesthetic drugs, nicotine, and viral infections. The complex connections among these ncRNAs were also discussed, along with potential overlapping ncRNA mechanisms, possible pharmacological targets for therapeutic/neuroprotective interventions, and potential biomarkers for brain developmental disorders.

Introduction

1.

Definition, biogenesis, and regulation mechanisms of non-coding ribonucleic acids (ncRNAs)

1.1.

Emerging evidence suggests that ncRNAs are pivotal molecules that participate in brain development as well as neurological diseases. ncRNAs are referred to as heterogeneous RNAs transcribed from the genome, yet without protein-encoding potential (Mattick and Makunin, 2006). Nearly 80% of the human genome is composed of non-coding elements (Fig. 1A). The category of ncRNAs is expanding from time to time since its discovery. For now, broadly, ncRNAs include structural RNAs such as ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and transfer RNA (tRNA), as well as regulatory RNAs including long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), microRNA (miRNA), circular RNA (circRNA), small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA), small nuclear RNA (snRNA), small interfering RNA (siRNA), piwi-interacting RNA (piRNA), and et al. MiRNA and lncRNAs recently, have risen to the foreground of players in terms of regulation and function in brain development. This review focuses on the importance of these two types of ncRNAs and their involvement in abnormal brain development resulted from exposure to alcohol, anesthetic drugs, nicotine, and viral infections. Literature searches were performed using mainly Google Scholar and PubMed using a variety of keywords (e.g., ncRNA mechanisms, ncRNA/lncRNA/miRNA AND brain development, ncRNA/lncRNA/miRNA AND alcohol/FASD/anesthetics/nicotine/viral infections AND brain developmental disorders/neurotoxicity).

Our understanding of ncRNAs remains in its infancy. During the past several decades, great progress has been made in deciphering the regulatory functions of ncRNAs in diverse biological processes. Figure 1 (B–C) depicts the major biogenesis of miRNAs and the mechanisms of lncRNAs. Briefly, miRNAs are defined as endogenous RNAs that can play important roles in the regulation of gene expression through pairing to the mRNAs and directing their post-transcriptional repression (Bartel, 2009). MiRNA biogenesis undergoes a coordinated cropping and dicing process from the original primary transcript to the eventually functioning molecule (Kim, 2005; Krol et al., 2010). Eventually, the mature miRNA interacts with the target mRNA within special machinery termed RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). Thus, the mature miRNA is capable to exert the post-transcriptional regulation as translational inhibition or mRNA destabilization (through mRNA deadenylation or degradation) (Filipowicz et al., 2008; Krol et al., 2010)

LncRNAs, on the other hand, are transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that are not translated into proteins (Kopp and Mendell, 2018). When compared with protein-coding genes, lncRNA sequences are poorly conserved between mammals (Kutter et al., 2012). They play distinct roles in various locations such as nuclear, nucleolar, cytoplasmic, and even individual cellular compartments. Compared with miRNAs, the regulation mechanisms for lncRNAs are much complicated and can be summarized as recruitment, inhibition, indirectly through the act of transcription, genome organization, and the architecture of the nucleus. For instance, lncRNAs can help recruit regulatory proteins (such as transcriptional factors) to certain genes and hence influence the gene expression. LncRNAs can also suppress gene expression by directly binding to RNA polymerase II (Pol II) and affecting the preinitiation complex. The transcription of one lncRNAs may regulate the transcription of several nearby genes through indirect mechanisms (e.g. lncRNA transcription-dependent activation or repression of neighbor genes, lncRNAs as scaffold for chromatin modifying complex). Additionally, it has been reported that lncRNAs brings distant chromosomatic DNA fragments into proximal organization and promote the expression modification within these regions (Long et al., 2017; Marchese et al., 2017) (Figure 1).

Role of ncRNAs in brain development

1.2.

The mammalian central nervous system (CNS) development involves a series of intricately coordinated events in which highly precise spatial and temporal control of gene expression is needed at both the transcriptional and translational levels. Fine-tuning of gene expression is a fundamental requirement for the development of brains where thousands of different neuronal and glial cell types are derived from collective stem cell populations in a temporally and quantitatively well-orchestrated manner (Follert et al., 2014). Both miRNAs and lncRNAs are involved in multiple brain developmental events (e.g., neurogenesis, cell proliferation, and pruning). Here we summarize a few key research examples of miRNA and lncRNA involvement in normal brain development before moving towards how ncRNAs are related to different developmental disorders.

Various miRNAs are enriched in the brain, and most of them are highly cell type- or cell lineage-specific, while some of them are altered dynamically within the brain both before and after birth, suggesting a need for different miRNAs throughout development. miRNAs are implicated in several brain development events from the development of the neural tube to neural maturation (Sun and Shi, 2015) by regulation of cell fate specification and differentiation of neural stem cells (NSCs), neuronal cortical development, synaptogenesis, and microglial development (Cho et al., 2019). For instance, one of the most well-studied miRNAs is miR-9, a brain-specific miRNA that has been extensively linked to neurogenesis (Zhao et al., 2009), and was also linked to an increased risk of schizophrenia, a disease that has been hypothesized to have neurodevelopmental origins (Owen et al., 2011). As such, miR-9-related dysregulation of neurogenesis could be one of the many factors leading to an increased predisposition to the disease. In the case of miR-137, complete loss of the miRNA led to postnatal lethality, while heterozygous knockouts and conditional knockout (central and peripheral nervous system, including neuronal and glial cell precursors) mice showed dysregulated synaptic plasticity and synaptic pruning deficits (Cheng et al., 2018). This demonstrates that in both progressive and regressive developmental events, i.e. neurogenesis and pruning, miRNAs play pivotal roles.

Furthermore, miR-138 is known to inhibit the transcription factor SOX4 (Yeh et al., 2013), a repressor of oligodendrocyte differentiation (Braccioli et al., 2018; Potzner et al., 2007). Although this relationship between miR-138 and SOX4 was firstly described in cancer studies, both the miRNA and the transcription factor were highly enriched in the brain and have been described in neurodevelopmental processes (Siegel et al., 2009). Sox4 specifically was present at early stages, possibly regulating neuronal maturation alongside Sox11 (Cheung et al., 2000). Investigations of these complex regulatory interactions between miRNAs and their targets are crucial to understand and unveil the mechanisms behind different events during the development of the CNS, where, as shown above, miRNAs are crucial players.

Despite its ubiquity, the precise roles of most lncRNAs remain unknown. They are expressed in both adult and developing brains, and in a variety of time points and different regions. For instance, Mercer et al have shown that a group of examined lncRNAs expressed in the adult mouse brain were expressed in specific brain cell types, subcellular compartments, and different brain regions, suggesting that IncRNAs play brain region-, cell type-, and cellular activity-specific roles (Mercer et al., 2008). In another study, Liu and colleagues used single-cell RNA sequencing to identify cell-type-specific lncRNA expression during human neocortical development at various time points [gestational week (GW) 13/14.5, 16, 21, and 23]. They found that the lncRNA MEG3 was highly expressed in the brain and especially enriched in interneurons at GW21. They also revealed a role for another lncRNA (LOC646329) which was specific to GW16 radial glial cells, where it regulated the proliferation of those cells (Liu et al., 2016). In this case, MEG3 was already known to be brain and neuron-specific. Liu et al also found time and cell-type specificity of MEG3. Thus, time-, cell-, and tissue-specific expressions of MEG3 indicate that MEG3 might play important specific roles in brain development. Recent studies have also identified a neural-specific lncRNA Pinky (Pnky), which served as a regulator of neuron differentiation in embryonic and postnatal brains through interactions with PTBP1 and related transcripts (Ramos et al., 2015). The binding interaction and positive transcriptional regulation of PTBP1 by Pnky was validated, with a loss function of either PTBP1 or Pnky leading to a decrease in neurogenesis. Another lncRNA, named distal-less homeobox 1 antisense (Dlx1as), was found to be up-regulated during GABAergic differentiation and down-regulated during oligodendrocyte differentiation, participating in neural cell fate determination and oligodendrocyte elaboration (Ramos et al., 2013; Shi et al., 2017). Metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (Malat1), by far one of the most well-studied lncRNAs in the nervous system, was predicted to regulate pathways related to synapse formation and dendrite growth (Zhang et al., 2017b). Malat1 is now known to be involved in the regeneration following traumatic brain injury possibly through modulation of inflammation-related pathway (Patel et al., 2018). The impact of Malat1 on brain development and synapse formation was validated after gain- and loss-of-function in cultured mouse hippocampal neurons during the period from postnatal day 0 (PD0) to PD28. These studies also demonstrated that this regulation of synaptogenesis occurred through 2 key proteins: neuroligin1 (Ngln1) and synaptic cell adhesion molecule 1 (Bernard et al., 2010). In the presence of Malat1 antisense, cultured hippocampal neurons showed a significant decrease in both proteins, although the effect was more preeminent in the expression of Ngln1. The lack of Malat1 in this experiment was also directly linked to the formation of new synapses in vitro, shown by number of synapses and immunoreactivity to synapsin 1. Although our understanding of lncRNAs in brain development remains in its infancy, these studies have clearly shown that lncRNAs are important to regulate different aspects of neurodevelopment. The dysregulations of brain ncRNA expression could result in brain developmental disorders followed by long-term cognitive dysfunction and behavior problems.

NcRNAs in environmental stress-related developmental brain disorders

2.

The human CNS stands with remarkable complexity as unique, given the precision of all the patterns and mechanisms governing its development. This process starts around the third GW3, after the formation of the neural tube. Following the patterning of the neural tube into a dorsal and ventral region, neurogenesis begins and continues through mid-gestation in most brain regions. This process starts by first drastically increasing the population of NSCs, and further differentiating those NSCs into neurons (Stiles and Jernigan, 2010). Once differentiated, these neurons migrate radially from the ventricular region to the developing neocortex. Once in place, neurons start to form complex connections in a process called synaptogenesis that will peak, depending on the brain region, from GW18 until 2–3 years of age. Glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and microglia) play crucial parts during both brain development and in the adult brain with more than half of the cells in the human brain being glia. As a general rule, gliogenesis occurs after the major formation of neurons. Of these cells, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs), serve an essential role in myelinating neurons from GW16 and continuing after birth (Johnson, 2001). Finally, regressive events, such as programmed cell death, or apoptosis, as well as the pruning of specific synapses and axons, serve a key role in eliminating cell populations that may only play a transient role in the brain (Tau and Peterson, 2010). An overview of the major events during brain development can be seen in Figure 2.

Because of this complex and protracted series of events, the developing brain is especially susceptible to any disturbance from internal genetic factors and external environmental stress. With the advent of improved technology and novel methodology to evaluate brain structure and function over the past decades, investigators can explore the significance of developmental processes in sculpting cognitive dysfunction, neuropsychiatric disorders and implement control (MacMaster et al., 2016). Recent studies also suggest that some adult-onset neurodegenerations have roots in early development (Edamakanti et al., 2018). For instance, the molecular mechanisms behind schizophrenia have been implicated in both development and aging models, with candidate genes in schizophrenic patients being directly linked to the process of neurodevelopment (Gupta and Kulhara, 2010). Disrupted in schizophrenia 1 (DISC-1), for instance, has a disrupted protein structure that might predispose individuals to schizophrenia. DISC-1 also plays a critical role in neural proliferation, differentiation, and migration (Ishizuka et al., 2006; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). Another such gene is neuregulin 1 (NRG1), which is mutated in certain populations of schizophrenic patients, while still having a role in neuron migration and axon guidance (Fornasari et al., 2016; Stefansson et al., 2002; Unda et al., 2016).

Internally, genetic factors can affect the morphogenesis of different brain regions such as in Chiari malformations and spina bifida, and influence connections and function of CNS such as in fragile X syndrome, cerebral palsy, and Angelman syndrome (Elster and Chen, 1992; Fombonne, 2009; Mitchell et al., 2004; Warkany and Kalter, 1961). Externally, this can take form as exposure to certain substances, such as Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASDs) related conditions (Hoyme et al., 2005; Streissguth and O’Malley, 2000), as well as more nuanced phenotypes, such as the ones present following exposure to general anesthesia (Nair et al., 2017), nicotine and other tobacco products (Haustein, 1999; Mitchell and Milerad, 2006). Moreover, foreign invaders such as viruses and bacteria (Azam et al., 2001; Mlakar et al., 2016) are also capable of altering normal brain development. These acute developmental brain injuries are known to result in neurodevelopmental disorders that can affect people throughout their lifetime.

Neurodevelopmental disorders include physical as well as functional abnormalities. These brain dysfunctions can influence a child’s cognition and behavior causing learning and memory deficits, intellectual disabilities, dyslexia, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and autism. The severity of pathology and symptoms depend upon the dose and timing of exposure to harmful substances. Neurodevelopmental behavioral disorders frequently occur in industrialized countries. Up to 15% of children were reported with learning disabilities, developmental delay, ADHD, autism, low intelligence quotient, and cerebral palsy (Dietrich et al., 2005). The molecular mechanisms of such brain developmental disorders are largely unknown.

As described above, in addition to the regulatory roles of ncRNAs in various stages of CNS development, emerging evidence implicates the association of dysregulation of ncRNA biogenesis and their regulatory role with genetic factor- and environmental stress-related brain developmental disorders such as FASD, anesthetic-induced developmental neurotoxicity (AIDN), and neurotoxicity associated with nicotine, tobacco exposure, and viral infections as discussed in the following sections. The latest studies linking developmental neurotoxicity as one of the major teratogenic changes in brain development and neurological diseases to the emerging field of ncRNAs were discussed in detail in the following sections. We focused on reviewing the importance of ncRNAs in the following four types of external stress-induced developmental brain disorders: FASD, AIDN, and neurotoxicity associated with nicotine exposure and viral infections during development. We also discussed mechanisms and overlapping systems, focusing on the possibility of using these molecules as biomarkers, as well as the importance of these ncRNAs as possible therapeutic targets for future translational studies. Deciphering the core signaling pathway in this ncRNA regulating network in multiple developmental brain disorders could help us better understand how the acute neural lesion ends up with chronic functional changes.

Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASDs)

2.1.

FASDs are caused by prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE), which can lead to lifelong physical, behavioral, cognitive, and psychological problems. The severity of alcohol-induced brain dysfunction in humans varies greatly, from subtle cognitive dysfunction and neurobehavioral deficits to obvious structural abnormalities such as the agenesis of corpus callosum (O’Connor and Paley, 2009; Reynolds et al., 2011; Riley and McGee, 2005; Zhang et al., 2018). Of the diagnoses under the umbrella of FASDs, Fetal Alcohol Syndrome (FAS) is the most severe, implicating in craniofacial abnormalities and growth impairments as well as the aforementioned effects to the functions of CNS (Gupta et al., 2016). It has been estimated that individuals affected by FASD are between 19 and 40 times more likely to become involved in the Criminal Justice System (Popova et al., 2015). FASDs have no clear mechanism of action, and therefore no specific cure or therapy, apart from palliative care (Sokol et al., 2003). Despite well-known adverse consequences, 1 in 10 pregnant women reported having consumed alcohol during pregnancy (Tan et al., 2015). Different sociocultural factors, including the high number of unplanned pregnancies (Finer and Zolna, 2016) and alcohol use disorder (AUD), not only make it difficult to prevent drinking with education alone but also indicate that the actual prevalence of FASDs might be higher than estimated (Chasnoff et al., 2015; Ernhart et al., 1988). As a result, in the USA, approximately $5.4 billion per year is spent on treating FASDs (Popova et al., 2011). This information suggests that FASDs are concerning on a societal level and the research into the mechanisms behind ethanol (alcohol)-induced developmental neurotoxicity (EIDN) is severely important. Though a lot of studies investigated different genetic and epigenetic aspects of FASDs, it is only in the past 15 years that the studies on what roles ncRNAs play in these disorders are emerging (Lussier et al., 2017; Mason and Zhou, 2015). Here we summarized studies that looked into ncRNA mechanisms through the investigation of cause-and-effect relationships, as well as the potential of ncRNAs to be biomarkers for FASDs. MicroRNAs

2.1.1.

In general, exposure to ethanol affects various organs of the fetus. There have been reports of dysregulations of miRNA expression in many tissues and organs such as the liver, pancreas, heart, and many organs other than CNS (Mandal et al., 2018). The earliest description of miRNA dysregulation following ethanol exposure in CNS came from Sathyan et al, where they showed that in vitro primary cultured neuroepithelial cells exposed to ethanol suppressed the expression of miR-21, miR-335, miR-9, and miR-153 (Sathyan et al., 2007). As mentioned above, miR-9 is known to be important in the regulation of neurodevelopmental processes including stem cell proliferation and maturation (Coolen et al., 2013). Out of these four downregulated miRNAs, they found that antisense-mediated suppression of miR-21 expression resulted in apoptosis, and miR-335 inhibition promoted cell proliferation and prevented death induced by concurrently suppressing miR-21. These data suggest that miR-21 is an antiapoptotic factor and miR-355 is a proapoptotic, antimitogenic factor (Sathyan et al., 2007).

In the case of the fetal animal brain, one of the earliest studies, from Wang et al, showed the upregulation of 6 miRNAs, and downregulation of 8 others, out of 509 miRNAs in fetal mouse brains exposed to ethanol during pregnancy (Wang et al., 2009). Interestingly, this study showed the opposite regulation of miR-9 when compared to the findings from Sathyan et al (Sathyan et al., 2007) in response to the ethanol treatment described above, in this case, miR-9 was increased following ethanol, while Sathyan showed a decrease in miR-9 expression. The authors suggest that this might be due to differences between ex vivo and in vivo preparations. The subjects used are also different in these two studies. Wang et al used female mice, which were administrated with 2.0, 4.0, and 6.0 g/kg/day ethanol via gavage twice a day for 10 days during pregnancy, while in Sathyan et al, the 13 mM to 70 mM ethanol were diluted in the culture medium of neurospheres for 24 hours or 5 days. Nonetheless, this does point to the possible importance of miR-9 in EIDN. Out of differentially expressed genes, the mechanism and Function of miR-10a were also dissected, known to regulate embryonic patterning and organogenesis (Barrow et al., 2000). This study not only solidified the regulatory relationship between miR-10a and its neighboring Hox gene (Hoxa1 to be specific) but also provided a possible suppression of teratogenic effects of ethanol, namely the diameter of the yolk sac, crown-rump length, and head size, by introducing folic acid supplementation. This highlights the importance of studying ncRNA signaling and thereby the development of neuroprotective treatments that can alleviate the brain injury associated with PAE.

Following the studies above, other investigators also found dysregulation in miRNA expression following early alcohol exposure, in different animal and human models, and for both short and long-term changes. Suppression of the aforementioned miR-9 in zebrafish and mouse models culminated in phenotypes strikingly similar to FAS patients such as cranial deficits, pointing towards a relatively conserved role of miR-9 in EIDN (Mahnke et al., 2018; Pappalardo-Carter et al., 2013). Another miRNA identified in early studies and further dissected is miR-153, a brain-enriched and evolutionarily conserved miRNA (Sempere et al., 2004), now also known to be neuroprotective against ethanol exposure (Tsai et al., 2014). In humans, miR-153 was found to be upregulated in the forebrain of alcoholic patients in comparison to matched controls (n=14) (Lewohl et al., 2011), as well as in HEK293T, SH-SY5Y, and 1321 N1 cells (Van Steenwyk et al., 2013). Interestingly, recent studies have Shown possible interactions between miR-153 and α-synuclein (Recasens et al., 2016), a protein associated with ADHD in human studies (Gerlach et al., 2019) of which many FASD patients suffer as a secondary condition (Peadon and Elliott, 2010; Petrenko et al., 2014). Moreover, miR-153 was also shown to regulate the translation of α-synuclein in models of Parkinson’s Disease, acting through a mitochondrial reactive oxygen species pathway (Je and Kim, 2017). In this and other studies, the presence of miR-153 was a protective (sometimes in combination with miR-7) against 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium neurotoxicity – a common neurotoxin used to model Parkinson’s Disease and α-synuclein dysregulation (Doxakis, 2010; Fragkouli and Doxakis, 2014). Two other studies by Mandal and colleagues showed that even in the same model (embryoid bodies formed from NCCIT cells), different exposure times (2 and 7 days) resulted in dysregulation of different genes (Mandal et al., 2016). This indicates that miRNAs and their regulated gene expression changes could be highly dynamic, displayed as dose/timing dependency. Besides, the differences in the gene dysregulation underlying the myriad of symptoms present in FASDs likely come from the effects of more than one miRNA. These studies build up the potential importance of ncRNAs in the adverse effects PAE on brain development followed by long-term behavioral or psychiatry disorders.

For miRNA studies, there have been significant efforts in trying to identify specific transcripts that can act as biomarkers to predict the severity of FASD. Gardiner and colleagues identified 55 significantly altered miRNAs in pregnant women who reported drinking during pregnancy compared with a control group who reported abstinence. The study was conducted by analyzing miRNA expression in the serum, at the time of admission for delivery. Further bioinformatics analysis via Ingenuity Pathway Analysis revealed that all the dysregulated miRNAs were involved in pathways related to cell growth and signaling, especially in the cardiovascular system suggesting a specific effect during pregnancy (Gardiner et al., 2016). Although it consisted of a small sample size of 14, this study was the first report of alteration in serum miRNA expression that was associated with alcohol use during pregnancy.

To determine if these possible biomarkers can predict behavioral outcomes, Balaraman et al looked into plasma miRNAs of pregnant women divided into three groups (each group recruited 22–23 mothers): moderate to heavily-exposed mothers with an FASD-affected child; moderate to heavily-exposed mothers with apparently unaffected child; and low alcohol-consuming or unexposed mothers with an unaffected child. Eleven significantly altered miRNAs was identified in the FASD group. Interestingly, these 11 miRNAs were different than the ones identified by Gardiner et al. The authors attributed those differences to different assessment platforms [microarray hybridization vs. reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)], biofluid source (serum vs. plasma), geography, and population ethnicity (Balaraman et al., 2016).

Two more recent studies in humans have investigated the circulating miRNAs of both infants exposed during pregnancy and adolescents with moderate to severe acute alcohol intoxication (Ibáñez et al., 2020; Mahnke et al., 2021). In the first case, Mahnke et al examined a cohort of mothers in Cape Town And their children. PAE was associated with dysregulated miRNA expression profiles at both 2 weeks (18 dysregulated miRNAs) and 6.5 months (26 dysregulated miRNAs) of age. Corroborating with earlier research that shows sex differences in the presentation of FASD (May et al., 2017; Woods et al., 2018), the group found differences in miRNA expression between sexes, although further and larger samples are needed to confirm these findings (Mahnke et al., 2021). This is the first study to directly use infant miRNAs as biomarkers for the effects of PAE, which is crucial to develop appropriate therapies for children at greater risk to be affected. The second study looked at adolescents and young adults admitted to the hospital for acute alcohol intoxication. Herein they demonstrated that specific anti-inflammatory miRNAs (mir-146a-5p, mir-21–5p, and mir-182–5p) were dysregulated and this dysregulation was also sex-specific, with levels decreasing in females and increasing in male adolescents. These results were then replicated in adolescent mice (Ibáñez et al., 2020). Despite adolescents not being the same as FASD patients, this study does reiterate not only the biomarker potential of miRNAs following alcohol exposure but also the possibility that these markers vary across sexes.

The studies presented above converge on the fact that human miRNAs are altered by drinking during pregnancy. Armed with that knowledge, future studies can go different directions: 1) targeting specific miRNAs for therapeutic interventions, 2) identifying biomarkers for ethanol exposure, which can then be used to start different protective measures through investigating of the effects of many variables (duration, dose, age, sex, etc.) on the expression of these circulating miRNAs (Mandal et al., 2018), and 3) using alterations in miRNA expression to further study their function and mechanisms behind EIDN.

LncRNAs

2.1.2.

Despite being a relatively new field, the study of functional lncRNAs has already generated exciting results in different fields related to FASDs and EIDN. As of the writing of this publication, LNCipedia, a public database for lncRNA sequence and annotation has over 50,000 annotated genes, most of which with an unknown function (Volders et al., 2019). As described in a recent review by Mahnke et al, most of the data that is presently known about lncRNAs and alcohol exposure comes from AUD studies (Mahnke et al., 2018). For example, multiple lncRNAs were found dysregulated in the prefrontal cortex region from alcoholics (Farris and Mayfield, 2014). Besides, the lncRNA Malat1 was found to be upregulated in different brain regions (cerebellum, hippocampus, and brain stem) from nine alcoholic patients, measured postmortem (Kryger et al., 2012). The authors did not find an elevation of Malat1 expression in the frontal and motor cortex, again pointing to brain-region-specific roles for the lncRNA. As we mentioned above, Malat1 is a highly conserved gene, thought to be involved in brain developmental events such as synaptogenesis (Bernard et al., 2010). Interestingly, Malat1 is also known to be regulated by the aforementioned miR-9. Thus, complex interactions like this one could be further explored for therapeutic purposes (Leucci et al., 2013). Additionally, Malat1 has been shown to regulate vascular growth in the brain, which has been linked to different types of alcohol-induced damage, such as the increased risk for embolic stroke in adults, and fetal angiogenesis in the brains of developing fetuses (Hillbom et al., 1999; Ramadoss and Magness, 2012; Wang et al., 2019). Brain-derived neurotrophic factor antisense (BDNF-AS), another lncRNA, was, in turn, found to be upregulated in postmortem tissues of eleven early-onset alcoholics – more specifically in the amygdala (Bohnsack et al., 2019). BDNF-AS played the role of BDNF inhibition via antisense complementary binding. Curiously, the same lncRNA was not changed in late-onset (after the age of 21 years old) alcoholics. Further studies with larger cohorts would help in investigating these phenomena. Very recently as well, Saba et al have described the role of the lncRNA Lrap in modulating alcohol consumption in rats, with Lrap knockouts having an increased alcohol consumption and preference (Saba et al., 2021). The role of ncRNAs in AUD, hence, is being increasingly noticed (Mayfield, 2017), but there is a lot that the field of FASD can learn from it. These studies highlighted, for instance, that lncRNAs might be involved in complex networks, e.g. lncRNA-miRNA, lncRNA-mRNA, and lncRNA-DNA, and that the effects of alcohol are likely to be age, sex, duration, and dosage-specific. Such as with miRNAs, there is hope that lncRNAs can be used as biomarkers for FASDs and EIDN. One recent study showed that the lncRNAs AK054921 and AK128652 are potential serum biomarkers for patients with alcoholic cirrhosis (Yang et al., 2017). Similar studies could be designed to examine biomarkers for alcohol exposure during pregnancy, as well as to examine the severity of different FASD phenotypes including brain injury.

Anesthetics-induced developmental neurotoxicity (AIDN)

2.2.

In 2016, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration warned that surgeries for children under three years old or women during late pregnancy, as well as multiple or lengthy use of general anesthetics might affect neonatal brain development (Moine et al., 2018). The total involved population reaches up to a couple of millions annually. However, as with many surgeries, especially in infants, the use of general anesthetics is inevitable.

The potential side effects on brain development from pediatric exposure to anesthetics were experimentally validated and emphasized (Bosnjak et al., 2016; Hudson and Hemmings, 2011; Mellon et al., 2007; Rappaport et al., 2015; Sanders et al., 2013; Steen and Michenfelder, 1979). In a structural sense, acute AIDN presents as increased neuronal apoptosis (Cattano et al., 2008; Yan et al., 2017), extensive neurodegenerative changes (Jevtovic-Todorovic et al., 2003), and the reduction of neurogenesis (Erasso et al., 2012; Stratmann et al., 2009) and synaptogenesis (Lunardi et al., 2010). While the chronic outcome of AIDN manifests as cognitive sequelae, such as learning and memory impairments (Jevtovic-Todorovic et al., 2003; Mellon et al., 2007; Sun, 2010; Wilder et al., 2009).

The aforementioned period of synaptogenesis starts from the third trimester of pregnancy to the first three years after birth (Tymofiyeva et al., 2014). The main hypothesis in the field of AIDN is that the brain is particularly more vulnerable during this period. In rodents, this stage occurs in the first two weeks after birth peaking at PD7 which is widely considered as the most vulnerable time to environmental stress, hence used in many developmental neurotoxicity studies (Sanders et al., 2013). The molecular mechanisms underlying acute and/or chronic AIDN are critical for developing protective intervention strategies, yet still largely unknown. Here, we reviewed how miRNAs and lncRNAs were involved in the developmental neurotoxicity caused by exposure to either propofol or sevoflurane, the two most commonly used intravenous and volatile anesthetic drugs, respectively (Table 2).

MicroRNAs

2.2.1.

Propofol

2.2.1.1.

Since 2014, we and other groups started to detect miRNA expression profiles in models of propofol-induced developmental neurotoxicity, especially on cultured immature neurons, and intact developing mouse brains. Data, from our lab, for instance, showed that propofol induced significant neuron death in a dose and exposure time-dependent manner and down-regulated 20 miRNAs in human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-derived neurons. This in vitro study further demonstrated that miR-21-mediated Sprouty2-signaling was involved in propofol-induced cell death which could be attenuated by overexpression of miR-21 (Twaroski et al., 2014). Further in silico analyses helped draw networks between the dysregulated miRNA expression profiles with their target genes and predicted signaling pathways. For example, propofol exposure resulted in abnormal expressions of miR-19a/miR-137 and their target gene EGR2 (early growth response-2), as well as miR-19b-2/miR-214 and their target gene ARC (activity-regulated cytoskeleton-associated protein) in rat primary NSCs. These dysregulated miRNAs were related to neural developmental processes, including neurogenesis of NSCs and dendritic spine morphogenesis (Fan et al., 2016). Another study by our group found that 3 hour-exposure to 50 mg/kg propofol on PD7 could lead to acute apoptosis in the hippocampi, impaired memory function of mice, the acute alteration of 100 mRNAs and 18 miRNAs. The long-term (PD60) effect of neonatal propofol exposure showed the 49 dysregulated mRNAs and 4 dysregulated miRNAs that were closely associated with the observed long-term cognitive dysfunction (Jiang et al., 2018). The study from Zhang et al showed that miR-132 expression decreased and its potential target gene p250GAP increased at both RNA and protein levels in the hippocampus of PD7 Sprague Dawley rats (Zhang et al., 2017a), especially 24 hours after propofol treatment. MiR-132 has been reported to be concentrated in neurons and plays an important role in the regulation of brain development (Magill et al., 2010). Thus, the propofol-mediated changes of miR-132 signaling might contribute to the impaired learning and memory in rats (Zhang et al., 2017a).

Other studies have demonstrated several functional miRNAs that were related to propofol-induced developmental neurotoxicity in both cell culture and animal models as described below. Cultured human embryonic stem cell (ESC)-derived neurons were exposed to 50 μM propofol for 6 hours and significant cell death was observed via activation of miR-206/PUMA (a proapoptotic gene) signal pathway (Li et al., 2017). Propofol could also inhibit the proliferation, neuronal differentiation, and migration of NSCs by up-regulating miR-141–3p (Jiang et al., 2017). It was also found that miR-665 affected propofol-induced rat astrocyte neurotoxicity by targeting the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2L1 (Sun et al., 2015; Sun and Pei, 2015). The function of miRNAs in AIDN was further confirmed by in vivo animal studies. In a rat study, a single 30 mg/kg propofol exposure led to apoptosis of developing hippocampal astrocytes, upregulation of miR-665, and down-regulation of its target BCL2L1. A miR-665 antagonist attenuated propofol-induced neurological outcomes by targeting BCL2L1 (Sun and Pei, 2016). These novel studies suggest that miRNAs are involved in the propofol-induced neurotoxicity by interfering multiple brain developmental events. The findings open the door to possible human studies that can investigate the potential of these miRNAs as biomarkers.

Sevoflurane

2.2.1.2.

Similar to the findings from propofol above, mounting evidence has demonstrated that microRNAs are also associated with sevoflurane-induced developmental neurotoxicity in various models. Firstly, neonatal sevoflurane exposure led to acute and long-term abnormal miRNA profiles in rodent brains (Fujimoto et al., 2015; Ye et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2017). For instance, many miRNAs were only transiently changed after exposure (Lin et al., 2018) while other miRNAs such as miR-20a, miR-20b, miR-532–5p, and miR-99a in hippocampi, and miR-300, miR-29a, and miR-145 in whole-brain showed significant changes in both neonatal and adult mice following 2.4% sevoflurane exposure on PD7 mice for 2 hours (Lin et al., 2018). Additionally, miR-27b-LIMK1-signaling dysregulation was found in repeated sevoflurane exposed (3% for 2 hours per day for three days) neonatal rats (Yu et al., 2016). To corroborate that, recent studies have also shown that different sets of miRNAs (miR-92b-5p, miR-125a-3p, miR-194–5p, miR-1180–3p, and miR-1296–3p) were dysregulated in the frontal cortex of monkeys exposed to sevoflurane (Liu et al., 2020). These findings suggest the importance of miRNAs in brain-region specificity of the neurotoxic effects from the anesthetic. This type of particular effect can be further expanded in future studies by investigating the potential therapies via modification of the expression of these dysregulated miRNAs in AIDN models.

Several studies showed that the sevoflurane-induced dysregulated miRNAs were involved in the disturbance of several brain development events such as cell proliferation and neurogenesis. In vitro studies showed that the let-7a-Lin28 signaling participated in the 4.1% sevoflurane exposure (6 hours)-induced inhibition of neural differentiation in mouse ESCs (Yi et al., 2016). Gain- and loss-of function assays of miR-183 demonstrated its functional effect on regulating hippocampal NSC proliferation and differentiation. Sevoflurane was further found to inhibit cell proliferation and differentiation via up-regulating miR-183 and down-regulating its target gene NR4A2 in hippocampal NSCs isolated from neonatal rats (Shao and Xia, 2019). Additionally, there were multiple functional studies investigating downstream effectors and mechanisms of miRNAs, e.g. miR-181 inhibition was shown to alleviate hippocampal apoptosis following sevoflurane exposure in neonatal rats via negatively regulating post-synaptic density protein (PSD) 95 and phosphate CREB [cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate) response element-binding protein]/CREB ratio (Chen et al., 2016a). Other than that, gain- and loss- of function assay of miR-34a and its validated target gene Wnt1 demonstrated that miR-34a inhibition could protect sevoflurane-induced hippocampal apoptosis via up-regulating Wnt1 and further Wnt/β-catenin pathway in neonatal mice (Zhao et al., 2018). Lastly, miR-27a-3p inhibition ameliorated sevoflurane-induced learning and memory impairment via targeting peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) signaling in neonatal mice and primary hippocampal neurons (Lv et al., 2017). These studies highlight the therapeutic potential of directly targeting specific miRNAs. More than just descriptive mechanisms, these data can be used in future studies to evaluate how the amelioration of these symptoms in mice translates to human populations.

LncRNAs

2.2.2.

Our group was one of the first ones to show the dysregulation of hundreds of lncRNAs alongside mRNAs by propofol. In this recent study, 50 mg/kg propofol for 3 hour-exposure led to neuroapoptosis, together with 159 dysregulated lncRNAs and 100 dysregulated mRNAs in hippocampi from PD7 neonatal mice. These dysregulated lncRNAs and mRNAs were then predicted to be involved in multiple neurodegenerative pathways (Logan et al., 2018). As for sevoflurane, it was found that sevoflurane exposure (2.3%, 6 hours) resulted in significant dysregulation of 31 apoptosis-related lncRNAs (19 up-regulated and 12 down-regulated) and 25 mRNAs in hippocampi from PD7 mice. Among them, the up-regulated ENSMUST00000136025 was predicted to induce the over-expression of Bcl2l11 (Bim) and eventually promote mitochondria-mediated apoptosis (Chen et al., 2016b). Lu et al, furthermore, found that 2.4% sevoflurane exposure for 6 hours could lead to severe differential lncRNA expression (1183 up-regulated and 1416 down-regulated) in rat hippocampal NSCs. Among them, lncRNA Gadd45a and its nearby gene Gadd45a were found both significantly upregulated (Lu et al., 2018). More recently, our group has also described the differences in lncRNA and mRNA expression patterns (148 mRNAs and 301 lncRNAs) in the hippocampi of P7 mice exposed to 3% sevoflurane for 6 hours. The same study bioinformatically analyzed the predicted lncRNA-mRNA networks that were dysregulated by sevoflurane, finding 34 neurodegenerative signaling pathways (Jiang et al., 2021). Once again, while these initial studies might have a descriptive nature, simply pointing to distinct dysregulations, we believe that this is an exciting start to more complex studies that can start targeting these dysregulations in a translational manner.

Mechanistic and functional studies on the implication of individual lncRNAs in sevoflurane-induced developmental neurotoxicity are also emerging. For instance, Lu and colleagues found that 2.4% sevoflurane exposure for 6 hours impaired rat hippocampal NSC proliferation and up-regulated lncRNA WNT5-AS. Further knockdown of WNT5A-AS rescued NSC neurogenesis by promoting the proliferation and survival of NSCs via suppressing WNT5A and reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling (Lu et al., 2019). In a different study, repeated sevoflurane (3%, 2 hours per day) exposure for 3 days to PD6 mice and mouse ESCs decreased neural differentiation and lncRNA Rik-203 expression. It was found that Rik-203 regulated neural differentiation in ESCs via miR-101a-3p/glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β) signaling (Zhang et al., 2019). LncRNA Gm15621 expression was found to decrease in primary hippocampal neuron cells exposed to sevoflurane (1, 2, and 4%) for 6 hours. LncRNA Gm15621 overexpression significantly reduced the apoptosis and inflammation response, and ameliorated the neurotoxicity via Gm15621’s target miR-133a and miR-133a’s target gene Sox4 (Zhao and Ai, 2019) pathway. Lastly, downregulation of the already mentioned lncRNA Malat1 was found to reduce the hippocampal neuroapoptosis, improve the pathological change, ultrastructure, spatial learning, and memory function, and alleviate hippocampal nerve cell loss in 2% sevoflurane (6 hours)-treated neonatal rats (Hu et al., 2019). Similar to the miRNA functional studies, these lncRNA-related findings suggest a close relationship between specific dysregulated lncRNA molecules and pathophysiological events. LncRNA regulation mechanisms are complicated depicted in Figure 1. Additionally, it is less likely that single lncRNA plays key roles in environmental stressor-induced neurodevelopmental disorders. Thus, both omic (e.g., RNA sequencing and proteomics) and function studies are needed to unveil IncRNAs’ roles and the underlying mechanisms. We are enthusiastic about the ever so increasing predictive power of different computational tools. For instance, Yi et al developed a deep learning algorithm capable of predicting complex ncRNA-protein interactions with high accuracy (Yi et al., 2018). Other than that, Fan et al also developed an algorithm capable of predicting different associations between lncRNAs and diseases (Fan et al., 2019). These new tools will be essential in investigating new pathways that are dysregulated by anesthetics. In that sense, these studies we cited above act as the groundwork for further investigations.

Developmental neurotoxicity associated with nicotine

2.3.

Smoking during pregnancy could cause a variety of possible pregnancy issues, including preeclampsia and placental pathologies, as well as with poor fetal outcomes such as premature birth, stillbirth, and sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) (Bruin et al., 2010; Haustein, 1999; Holbrook, 2016; Lambers and Clark, 1996). Nevertheless, it is estimated by the Center for Disease Control (CDC) that 7.2% of women in the U.S. smoke at some point during their pregnancies, with other estimates by other countries being as high as 20% (Cnattingius, 2004; Drake et al., 2018; Mitchell and Milerad, 2006). With a rise in the use of e-cigarettes, especially among younger people (Kapaya et al., 2019), it is pressingly necessary to understand how nicotine and the associated tobacco cause their adverse effects on the developing fetus, and further develop proper therapies and protective strategies. Here, we summarized recent efforts in examining the role of dysregulated ncRNAs in developmental models exposed to nicotine. A summarized list can be found in Table 3.

MicroRNAs

2.3.1.

In one of the first studies to investigate the role of ncRNAs in the neurotoxicity associated with nicotine exposure, Maccani et al showed that miR-16, miR-21, and miR-146a were downregulated in the placenta of smoking mothers. While not affecting directly the developing brain, this dysregulation in the placenta can lead to poor nutrient delivery and subsequently impaired brain development (Maccani et al., 2010). Further research by Keller et al showed that rats perinatally exposed to nicotine had upregulation of miR-140–5p and miR-140–3p in dopaminergic (DA) neurons; while miR-140–3p and miR-212 were differentially expressed in non-DA neurons – both types of neurons from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) of the brain (Keller et al., 2017). The VTA has been highly studied in the field of reward and addiction, and indeed smoking during pregnancy was associated with a higher possibility of drug abuse of the offspring (Buka et al., 2003; Hellstrom-Lindahl and Nordberg, 2002; Ross et al., 2015). Early dysfunction in these DA neurons could thus explain some of the phenotypes associated with this later addiction. Interestingly, there is some overlap between studies that investigated the effects of ethanol and nicotine in the developing brain. For instance, the aforementioned miR-153 studied as a neuroprotective against EIDN was also shown to significantly induced by nicotine, resulting in the downregulation of the downstream target genes Nfia and Nfib (Tsai et al., 2014). Another study, by Balaraman et al, showed that ethanol and nicotine had opposite effects on the same set of ncRNAs (Balaraman et al., 2012). Using neurosphere cultures from fetal rodents exposed to either ethanol or nicotine, the authors showed that in the neurospheres of ethanol-exposed mice there was a suppression of miR-140–3p, while in the nicotine-exposed cells there was a dose-dependent increase in the expression of the same miRNA. When treated with both, ethanol blocked the effect of nicotine, by acting directly on the fetal nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs). While difficult to measure, there is evidence that polysubstance use during pregnancy is common (Forray, 2016). This study unveils complex actions of smoking alongside other drugs of abuse, such as alcohol in pregnancies.

LncRNAs

2.3.2.

While, to the best of our knowledge, no study to this date looks directly into lncRNAs of the fetal brain exposed to nicotine, there are lessons to be taken from other studies (Machtinger et al., 2019; Pinson and Miranda, 2019). Earlier studies that looked at changes in cigarette smokers already found that H19 and SCAL1, two lncRNAs, were elevated in the lungs of smokers, as well as lung cancer cell lines (Gabory et al., 2006; Kaplan et al., 2003; Thai et al., 2013). H19 is known to be strongly expressed during embryogenesis, although its full function is yet to be determined (Gabory et al., 2006). Conceivably, future studies could investigate the lncRNA/mRNA expression profiles in postmortem human fetal brain tissues from deceased patients whose mothers are smokers compared with non-smokers in the case of SIDS.

Viral infection-induced developmental neurotoxicity

2.4.

Different viral infections have the potential for negative neurological effects, with some of them even displaying a higher susceptibility during the neonatal period, or more likely to go from an acute neuronal injury to a chronic functional impairment even after the initial infection is resolved (Cordeiro et al., 2015). This is especially the case when considering maternal viral transmission from mother to fetus (Shi et al., 2005). For instance, cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is one of the leading prenatal causes of neurodevelopmental birth defects worldwide, and 10–15% of the infected neonates might suffer from microcephaly and intellectual disabilities (Cheeran et al., 2009; Nelson and Demmler, 1997). Expression of the green fluorescence protein reporter gene with CMV immediate early promoter was found mostly activated in developing neurons compared with neurons from adults, suggesting the higher affinity and infection rates of CMV in the developing brains (van den Pol and Ghosh, 1998). On a different virus, the mosquito-borne Zika virus has caused large-scale epidemics and hence aroused much attention in recent years (WHO, 2016). Multiple European and American countries are still struggling with a significant outbreak. Pregnant women with Zika virus infection are very likely to have abnormally developed newborns with microcephaly and other neurological symptoms (Shao et al., 2016; Valentine et al., 2016). Meanwhile, the possible implication of the commonest human influenza virus in developmental neurotoxicity is also concerning. Animal studies showed that the maternal infection with the influenza virus could lead to the adult offspring with significant behavioral abnormalities, though viral RNAs were not detected in fetal brains. The underlying mechanism for that type of developmental neurotoxicity was considered long-lasting but indirect (Shi et al., 2005). While not completely understood, viral infection-induced developmental neurotoxicity is very common and widely recognized. As such, multiple ncRNAs were found implicated in viral infection-induced developmental neurotoxicity (Table 3), below we summarized and discussed a few.

MicroRNAs

2.4.1.

In a recent study, Castro et al. demonstrated that expression of miR-145 (the only up-regulated miRNA after Zika virus infection) and miR-148a (exclusively expressed while Zika virus infection) were induced in Zika virus-infected SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells and postmortem human fetal brains from stillborn with severe Zika Congenital Syndrome. Bioinformatic analysis on target genes of the dysregulated miRNAs after Zika virus infection (especially for miR-145 and miR-148a) indicates that these miRNAs might be implicated in pathways related to the CNS and cellular migration (Castro et al., 2019). Moreover, a different report with evidence both in vitro and in vivo (Dang et al., 2019) showed that Zika virus infection in human NSCs could lead to dysregulated miRNA-mediated mRNA repression. These changes affected pathways related to the normal cell cycle which might be involved in the microcephaly seen in these patients. In this study, the dysregulated let-7c and miR-124–3p were predicted to be associated with microcephaly. Even more, RNA-sequencing, miR-sequencing, and AGO-iCLIP-sequencing data showed that Zika virus infection upregulated let-7c and downregulated its target gene high-mobility group AT-hook 2 (HMGA2, a reported NSC self-renewal regulator) in NSCs. Zika virus infection upregulated miR-124–3p and downregulated miR-124–3p-targeted gene transferrin receptor (TFRC) expression in NSCs and mouse brain tissues. MiR-124–3p overexpressed NSCs ended up with a significant reduction in size for NSC-composed neurospheres, indicating that miR-124–3p might be the regulator of microcephaly phenotype. These convincing functional studies demonstrated that miRNAs are important players in Zika virus infection-induced neurodevelopmental deficits.

LncRNAs

2.4.2.

Besides the fact that the host’s ncRNAs are found dysregulated and are potential players in viral infection-induced developmental disorders, multiple viruses are capable of encoding ncRNA themselves, making these studies even more interesting. To the best of our knowledge, most of these virus-encoded miRNAs and lncRNAs are still connected to the basic viral homeostasis and life cycle, and there is no direct supporting evidence of their implication in developmental disorders. However, it has been proposed that a CMV-encoded lncRNA (Beta2.7 RNA, in this case) can have a potential effect, mainly due to its role in enhancing cell survival and stabilizing mitochondrial membrane potential during lytic Human Cytomegalovirus (HCMV) infection (Reeves et al., 2007). Thus, one of focuses in the future study is to examine viral-induced dysregulation of lncRNAs in brains. The related findings might lead to new lncRNA-related therapeutic targets. As mentioned in the other sections, there is a variety of methodologies that researchers can follow to further identify and functionally characterize the role of lncRNAs in viral infection-induced developmental neurotoxicity.

Conclusion

3.

Brain developmental disorders can result in long-term outcomes ranging from brain structural changes, to cognitive and behavioral abnormality, with the mechanisms largely unknown. This review summarized and emphasized the importance of two ncRNA (miRNA and lncRNA) transcripts in the search for mechanisms and therapeutic targets of FASDs, AIDN, developmental neurotoxicity associated with nicotine and viral infections. As reviewed above, ncRNAs are exciting and prevalent players in these environmental stressor-induced neurotoxicities. MiRNAs and lncRNAs can regulate brain developmental events such as NSC proliferation, neurogenesis, cellular migration, maturation, synaptogenesis, synaptic pruning, and apoptosis. Dysregulation of these ncRNAs likely leads to acute or even chronic neurodegeneration.

The study of the physiological roles of lncRNAs and their regulative mechanisms in brain development in healthy and disorders is still in its infancy, which in part explains the reason behind some of the studies described here being simply descriptive. Only very limited types of lncRNA mechanisms have been explored so far, such as the aforementioned antisense lncRNAs and competing endogenous RNA-based transcriptional regulation on mRNAs. Hence, lncRNAs could play their roles through multiple regulation mechanisms in developmental neurotoxicity studies (Chen et al., 2016b)(Bohnsack et al., 2019). Moreover, the mechanisms of miRNA-based competing endogenous RNAs was also found in studies described on the AIDN section above (Zhang et al., 2019; Zhao and Ai, 2019), in which lncRNAs and certain mRNAs were both subject to the negative inhibition of miRNAs and hence regulated via a lncRNA/miRNA/mRNA pattern. The variety of possible mechanisms of lncRNA actions make studying them a particular challenge, but also help explain how lncRNAs are involved in such a wide range of biological processes.

There are also overlapping pathological changes and mechanisms across the stress-related developmental brain disorders described above. For instance, apoptosis was commonly observed in ethanol, sevoflurane, and viral-induced developing brains. Some dysregulated ncRNAs, e.g. miR-145 and miR-153 were dysregulated in both models of FASD and nicotine exposure. The notion of overlapping pathological changes and mechanisms by which developmental neurotoxicity takes place makes sense if we consider that there are specific vulnerable periods during normal brain development that once disrupted can cause severe and over-lasting damage. For instance, our group and others have extensively demonstrated that anesthetics induced neuroapoptosis in developing animal models, and we also observed a similar phenotype in human brain organoids exposed to ethanol (Arzua et al., 2020). Other groups have also found increased apoptosis in developing brains exposed to nicotine and models of Zika virus infection (Huang et al., 2007; Huang et al., 2016). It is likely that not only the loss of neuronal cells but also the disruption of the normal brain development processes shown in Figure 2, causes the symptoms seen in all of these conditions – from considerable structural damages to the overlapping soft neurological dysfunctions. Furthermore, it is plausible that there are master ncRNA regulators that are easily affected by external elements and these ncRNAs might be involved in the common pathological changes such as apoptosis. MiR-145, for instance, is dysregulated in three different conditions (ethanol, sevoflurane, and viral disruptions). Nevertheless, such overlap might indicate not only similar molecular mechanisms but also key genes and molecules that can be pharmaceutically targeted in broad protective measures in multiple environmental factor-induced developmental injuries

It is also important to consider how various brain regions might be affected differently. To the best of our knowledge, the hippocampus seems to be a focus of a lot of the studies reviewed, but changes were also seen in the prefrontal cortex and motor cortex. One possible explanation is simply that the hippocampus is a fairly conserved region across species, with direct links to the phenotypes observed in the conditions we focused on – memory and cognitive impairments. The molecular mechanisms of these brain region-specific studies remain to be further dissected. We expect to see differences in expression patterns since ncRNAs have been shown to play incredibly specific roles depending on when and where they are located.

We expect more and more lncRNA, miRNA, and other ncRNA-related findings will emerge shortly, especially considering the advance of technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing that are becoming more accessible and more capable to capture the transcriptomics of brain cell-specific ncRNAs (Gawronski and Kim, 2017). Additionally, newer techniques, in silico approaches, and computational prediction tools will allow for an enhanced understanding of the molecular machinery and signaling networks behind the dysfunctions (Chen et al., 2018; Fan et al., 2019; Yi et al., 2018). Furthermore, an even newer field of ncRNAs is also emerging, with the ever so growing evidence that circRNAs participate and regulate several different biological functions (Ji et al., 2019; Kristensen et al., 2019; Memczak et al., 2013; Qu et al., 2015). Indeed, Paudel et al have recently described sex differences in the expression profiles of circRNAs in a mouse model of PAE (Paudel et al., 2020). As with the other types of ncRNAs discussed, newer and exciting studies are advancing our technical abilities to detect and characterize circRNAs as well (Ji et al., 2019; Kristensen et al., 2019).

There are exciting future studies that are directly ahead of the ones summarized here. During the development of these developmental neurodegenerations, multiple genes are acutely dysregulated, with severe and lasting consequences that do not yet have defined therapies, but almost all show potential for different types of palliative care. That means that identifying early potential biomarkers associated with these developmental neurodegenerations is essential to start supportive prevention or treatment as early as possible. Moreover, in the search for effective therapies, we believe that further studies in downstream effectors are needed. Even without the presence of cell death, the phenotypes of the different types of neurotoxicity included here all have similar components such as mitochondrial dysfunctions, changes in neurotrophic factors, and increases in oxidative stress. It is possible that pharmacologically targeting ncRNA signaling can improve these components, thereby ameliorating some of the symptoms associated with the neurological dysfunctions.

Collectively, the field of ncRNAs is helping answer old scientific questions regarding developmental neurotoxicity, while still opening up new exciting research directions. What we present in this review is not only that there is a considerable body of literature dedicated to the changes in ncRNAs during development as affected by different substances, but also that there is an exciting potential for this field, especially as it moves towards studying and developing ncRNA-based therapeutic targets and neuroprotective strategies as well as ncRNA-based potential biomarkers for developmental brain injury.

Fig. 2.

Overview of the major steps in the development of the human central nervous system. Brain development starts with the formation of the neural tube (neurulation), from the gestational week (GW) 3 through 8. After that, a period of rapid proliferation begins, first with neurogenesis followed by a process of neuronal migration. The processes of myelination, gliogenesis, and the formation of new synapses (synaptogenesis) start before birth and continue later in life. Apoptosis starts soon after neurogenesis and goes on until the beginning of infancy, while pruning happens in two waves, first on the first two years of life, and second during adolescence.

Highlights

  • Brain development is a dynamic, prolonged, and complex process.

  • Disruption of developmental events by either internal genetic factors or external environmental stress can cause brain developmental disorders.

  • Emerging evidence suggests non-coding RNAs are pivotal molecules that are involved in brain development in health and disease.

  • Non-coding RNA signaling can offer possible pharmacological targets for therapeutic/neuroprotective interventions, as well as biomarkers for brain developmental disorders.
