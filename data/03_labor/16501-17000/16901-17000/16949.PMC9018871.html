Psychosocial Factors Related to Cardiovascular Disease Risk in Young African American Women: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Objective:

African American women are exposed to multiple adverse psychosocial factors, including racism, discrimination, poverty, neighborhood stress, anxiety, and depression. The impact of these psychosocial factors on cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in women during early adulthood is limited. This review aims to summarize and synthesize the recent literature on psychosocial factors related to CVD risk in young African American women.

Methods:

We conducted a comprehensive search of the literature in PubMed, APA PsycINFO, and CINAHL. We systematically reviewed the literature for studies examining associations between psychosocial factors (e.g., racism, discrimination, neighborhood stress, anxiety) and CVD risk factors (e.g., body mass index [BMI], blood pressure, diabetes) in African American women age 19–24 years. Eligible studies measured at least one psychosocial factor, a CVD risk factor, and included young adult African American women (age 19–24) or reported sex-stratified analyses.

Results:

We identified nine studies that met our inclusion criteria: six cross-sectional and three longitudinal studies. Of these, eight studies reported that psychosocial factors (i.e., perceived stress, racial discrimination, internalized racism, depression) are related to higher BMI and blood pressure. The majority of studies were conducted among college students or had a small sample size (<200). The quality of six studies was rated as excellent; the remainder were good and fair.

Conclusions:

Findings from this review suggest that exposure to adverse psychosocial factors may be related to increased CVD risk in early adulthood (age 19–24) in African American women. However, larger prospective analyses are necessary to examine these associations.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD), including coronary heart disease, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease, is the leading cause of death among nearly all racial or ethnic groups in the United States (US). African American women are disproportionately affected by CVD, with approximately 50,000 deaths annually (Virani et al., 2020). In 2013–2016 the prevalence of CVD was 57% for African American women age ≥20 years compared with 43% for Latinas and non-Hispanic White women, and 37% for non-Hispanic Asian American women (Neergheen et al., 2019). Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) suggests that non-Hispanic Black women are less likely to meet any criteria for ideal cardiovascular health than non-Hispanic White or Asian women in the US, including nutrition or physical activity guidelines (Benjamin et al., 2019). Moreover, recent evidence suggests an earlier onset of CVD among African Americans and increased severity potentially due to higher rates of traditional CVD risk factors such as obesity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia (Carnethon et al., 2017).

Among young adults (age 19–24), racial/ethnic disparities in CVD risk factors are even more apparent. The Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) Study found that African American women were twice as likely to have hypertension as non-Hispanic White women (Kishi et al., 2015). Similarly, African American women in the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health (mean age = 28.9 years) were more likely to be overweight, have diabetes, and report hypertension medication use than non-Hispanic White women, Latinas, or Asian/Pacific Islanders (Clark et al., 2014). In addition to traditional CVD risk factors (e.g., diet, weight, physical activity, smoking, diabetes, cholesterol, and blood pressure), psychosocial factors may be critical to consider in CVD prevention (Everson-Rose & Lewis, 2005). In this report we systematically assessed the quality of evidence relating psychosocial factors to CVD risk in young adult African American women.

Psychosocial factors are attributes or influences that affect an individual’s mind or behavior (Gellman & Turner, 2013). A broad range of psychosocial factors, including depression, anxiety, acute life stress, discrimination, and low social support have been associated with CVD and related risk factors (Everson-Rose & Lewis, 2005). The role of psychosocial factors in the development of CVD is further highlighted in INTERHEART—a case-control study that showed a higher risk of myocardial infarction among participants, particularly women, that experience depression, perceived stress, and anxiety (Rosengren et al., 2004). A recent systematic review found a positive correlation between stress-related measures (i.e., perceived stress, social strain, chronic stress burden, chronic neighborhood and individual stressors and stressful life events) and CVD risk later in life (Stewart et al., 2018). Potential mechanisms linking psychosocial factors to CVD risk may include neurobiological pathways (e.g., inflammation) and lifestyle (Robertson et al., 2015). For example, chronic high-stress levels have been linked with poor health behaviors such as smoking, physical inactivity, and unhealthy diet (Jackson et al., 2010). However, the majority of these studies have focused on midlife and older women.

The historical hardships African Americans have endured, including slavery, racial segregation, racism, and racial discrimination, continue to contribute to today’s socioeconomic and health disparities (Williams et al., 2016). Structural racism perpetuates socioeconomic and health disparities in African American women through the developments of laws, policies, practices, and norms that impact access to education, employment, housing, and other basic social needs for positive health outcomes. The intergenerational impact of these factors is evident among young adults. For example, African American women are more likely to earn poverty-level wages than non-Hispanic White men and women (Economic Policy Institute). Furthermore, African American adults are 20% more likely than non-Hispanic White adults to report psychological distress (Mental Health America, 2021). While some research has examined the relationship between psychosocial factors and CVD risk in older African American women (Barber, Hickson, Kawachi, et al., 2016; Lewis et al., 2006; Okhomina et al., 2018; Subramanyam et al., 2012), there is a lack of understanding of this association among young African American women (age 19–24). Yet, during early adulthood African American women face unique stressors related to the transition from adolescence into adulthood (e.g., marriage, cohabitation, parenthood, school completion, employment) (Frech, 2014). The purpose of this systematic review is to summarize and assess the quality of evidence on psychosocial factors related to CVD risk in African American women during early adulthood.

Methods

A systematic review was performed to examine the current state of the evidence on psychosocial factors related to CVD risk in young adult (age 19–24) African American women. We used the Preferred Reporting of Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to direct our review (Moher et al., 2009). The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database was searched to ensure the research topic was unique and that there were no duplicate studies in progress or published to date. This study was accepted in PROSPERO with the registration number: CRD4202019758. This study was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval.

Search Strategy

The following electronic databases were comprehensively searched from November 2019 to July 2021: PubMed, APA PsychInfo (EBSCOhost), and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL). The searches were created and finalized with the assistance of a health science librarian trained in systematic reviews. The databases were searched using subject headings and key terms, including ‘psychological stress’, ‘depression’, ‘anxiety’, ‘social discrimination,’ and ‘racism’ combined with ‘cardiovascular disease’, ‘metabolic syndrome’, ‘atherosclerosis’, ‘carotid-intima media thickness’, ‘hypertension’, ‘sedentary behavior’, and ‘obese.’ The results were limited to human research studies published in English. We excluded literature reviews, editorials, expert opinions, case reports, and animal studies. Young adulthood was defined as ages 19–24 years to circumvent capturing studies predominantly focused on adolescence. Adolescence was defined as ages 10–19 years old, consistent with the World Health Organization definition of adolescence (World Health Organization, 2021). Studies that included cardiovascular events such as heart disease and vascular disorders were excluded from this review, as the outcomes of interest are CVD risk factors. Additionally, CVD events in this age group may be due to an underlying condition and not directly related to psychosocial factors. A detailed outline of the search strategy is provided in Supplemental Table 2. A hand search of the reference lists of retrieved articles was also conducted to retrieve any additional pertinent articles missed by the electronic search. All references were imported into EndNote, where duplicates were removed. Next, all references were placed into the Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for screening and data extraction.

Selection Criteria and Data Extraction

Two reviewers (LKH and YIC) independently screened all study titles and abstracts in Covidence for eligibility. If potentially eligible, the full text of the article was further examined for inclusion criteria. Studies that were selected for inclusion had the following characteristics: 1) included a sample of African American women aged 19 to 24 years; 2) reported data on at least one psychosocial factor (e.g., depression, anxiety, stress, racism, discrimination) for African American women; and 3) assessed any traditional CVD risk factors (i.e., diet, weight, body mass index, physical activity, smoking, diabetes, lipids, blood pressure) or subclinical CVD measures. Studies with a multi-ethnic sample were included if the majority (>50%) of participants were African American, or data were stratified by race/ethnicity. Studies were excluded if they did not report data specifically for African American women, if the participants had a CVD event, or if data were not available for the age group of interest (age 19–24 years). Differences in eligibility assessments were resolved by discussion. If consensus could not be reached, disagreement between the two reviewers was resolved by a third reviewer (DCB).

Data from included articles were extracted by two coauthors (LKH, YIC) independently. Any disagreement was resolved by discussion. The following information was extracted from each study: author, year of publication, study aim, design, sample size and characteristics (age, sex [percent female], race/ethnicity [percent African American]), psychosocial factor, CVD risk factor assessment, and key findings.

Quality Assessment

Two reviewers (LKH, YIC) appraised the quality of the included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), an 8-item checklist that assesses study risk of bias for observational studies using three domains: the selection of the study groups; the comparability of the groups; and ascertainment of the exposure and outcome variables of interest (Wells et al., 2013). Several experts have established that the NOS has good content validity and inter-rater reliability (Lo et al., 2014). Each domain can be awarded a maximum of one point with the exception of the “compatibility” domain, which can be awarded a maximum of 2 points (for a NOS maximum of 9 points). An adapted version of the NOS was used for cross-sectional studies. The maximum points allocation varied slightly; the “selection” domain can be awarded a maximum of 5 points, the “comparability” can be awarded a max of 2 points, and the “outcome” can be awarded a max of 3 points (for a NOS maximum of 10 points). A quality assessment/least risk of bias score was calculated based on the number of quality items satisfied. Studies with greater than 75% criteria were rated as excellent, studies with greater than 60% but less than 75% criteria were rated good, and studies with less than 60% criteria were rated fair.

Results

Study Selection

The literature search yielded 1,199 potentially relevant articles for review. After excluding 363 duplicate records from the electronic databases, we screened 836 titles and abstracts for eligibility. Of these, 770 studies were excluded for three main reasons: wrong outcome, wrong exposure, and wrong patient population. This resulted in the retrieval of 66 articles for full-text review. Upon full-text review, 58 articles were excluded: 42 articles were excluded because the mean age of participants was >24 years when cardiovascular risk was assessed (wrong patient population), nine articles did not evaluate CVD risk factors or subclinical CVD (wrong outcome), five articles did not specifically report CVD risk for African American females, and one article did not assess for psychosocial factors (wrong exposure). In total, nine studies were included in our systematic review. Figure 1 provides detailed results of the literature search and study selection process.

The study characteristics are presented in Table 1. All of the studies included in this review were conducted in the United States. The majority of articles were cross-sectional in design (n=6); the remaining were longitudinal studies (n=3). The mean age of participants ranged from 19 to 22 years. Sample size ranged from 20 to 775 African American women. Five studies included samples of both men and women (Curtis et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2016; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012; Shomaker & Goodman, 2015; Wright et al., 2018). Overall, four studies reported data exclusively on African American women (Cooper et al., 2014; Diggins et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2016; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012; Wright et al., 2018), and five studies stratified results by race or gender. The majority of studies (n=7) were conducted among college students, of which four attended a historically Black college or university (Diggins et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2016; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012; Wright et al., 2018).

Measures of Psychosocial Stress

Studies in this review assessed various psychosocial factors (Table 1). Two studies examined perceived stress (Diggins et al., 2015; Wright et al., 2018), four focused on discrimination or racism (Cooper et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2016; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012; Reid et al., 2016), and four collected data on depressive symptoms or anxiety (Curtis et al., 2016; Franko et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2016; Shomaker & Goodman, 2015). These psychosocial factors were measured using validated questionnaires and scales such as the Perceived Stress Scale (Cohen et al., 1983), the Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression Scale (Roberts et al., 1990), and Everyday Discrimination Scale (Williams et al., 1997). In addition, three studies examined coping or resiliency factors such as racial identity, having an Africentric worldview, prayer, and mindfulness (Cooper et al., 2014; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012; Wright et al., 2018).

CVD Risk Outcomes

The primary outcome in five of the studies was BMI (Table 1). One article assessed weight gain (Reid et al., 2016). Four studies reported on blood pressure, and one study reported fasting insulin. Also, two articles examined waist to hip ratio and waist to hip circumference. Outcomes were measured during in-person visits using a standard protocol of height and weight, blood pressure or fasting insulin. One study measured heart rate variability (Cooper et al., 2014). Of the included studies, one ascertained the outcome by self-report (Diggins et al., 2015) and one by record linkage (Reid et al., 2016).

Psychosocial Factors as Predictors of CVD risk

As reported in Table 2, seven studies found a significant association between a psychosocial factor and CVD risk factor. The four studies reporting on BMI found that the following psychosocial factors were related to higher BMI among young African American women: perceived stress, contextualized stress, internalized racism and depression or depressive symptoms (Curtis et al., 2016; Franko et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2016; Shomaker & Goodman, 2015). In one study (Reid et al., 2016), higher reports of everyday discrimination were associated with a 1.71times (95% CI, 1.03–2.85) higher odds of excessive weight gain. However, associations did not differ between African American and Latina women (Reid et al., 2016). Early life adversity (e.g. childhood exposure to violence, crime, or a diagnosis of illness in the family) was weakly (r=0.20) correlated with a greater waist-to-hip ratio (Curtis et al., 2016). Studies comparing depressive symptoms and CVD risk in African American women found that more depressive symptoms were associated with a higher incidence of obesity and decreased insulin levels (Franko et al., 2005; Shomaker & Goodman, 2015). In terms of blood pressure, Wright et al., 2018, found a weak (rs= −0.075) negative correlation between perceived stress and diastolic blood pressure. Depression and internalized racism were related to higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure (F=3.87 and 3.31, respectively) (Lee et al., 2016). Several protective factors were independently associated with lower CVD risk: prayer (Cooper et al., 2014), mindfulness (Wright et al., 2018), and positive racial identity (Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012).

Methodological Quality of Studies

Table 3 summarizes the methodological quality of studies using the NOS. The mean quality scores were 8.75 (Franko et al., 2005; Reid et al., 2016; Shomaker & Goodman, 2015) for cohort studies and 7 for cross-sectional studies (Cooper et al., 2014; Curtis et al., 2016; Diggins et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2016; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012; Wright et al., 2018), respectively. Six studies were rated as excellent (Cooper et al., 2014; Curtis et al., 2016; Franko et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2016; Reid et al., 2016; Shomaker & Goodman, 2015) and the remainder were rated as good (n= 1)(Wright et al., 2018) and fair (n=2) (Diggins et al., 2015; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012). The most common risk of bias in the cross-sectional studies was the selection of the study groups; none of the cross-sectional studies met all criteria for this domain. Using the adapted NOS quality assessment tool, another common risk of bias was a non-justified sample size and lack of information on non-respondents (Cooper et al., 2014; Curtis et al., 2016; Diggins et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2016; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012; Wright et al., 2018). Most studies had an appropriate comparability of groups based on design or analysis, with the exception of two (Diggins et al., 2015; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012). The lowest risk of bias was the selection, and reporting of outcome measures, with only one study using self-report (Diggins et al., 2015).

Discussion

Main Findings

This systematic review summarizes findings and critically evaluates the quality of 9 studies that examined the association between psychosocial factors and CVD risk in young African American women. Overall, the current evidence suggests there is a positive correlation between various psychosocial factors and CVD risk among African American women aged 19 to 24 years. Common psychosocial factors evaluated in the literature included perceived stress, discrimination, and racism. In terms of CVD risk factors, the most common outcomes were BMI and blood pressure. Studies were overall of excellent quality, with cohort studies being of higher quality than cross-sectional analyses.

Findings from the current systematic literature review suggest that among young African American women, perceived stress, racism, discrimination, and depression are significantly associated with BMI (Diggins et al., 2015; Franko et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2016; Reid et al., 2016). Several studies reported that African American women who ever experienced discrimination or internalized racism had a higher odds of weight gain and elevated BMI (Lee et al., 2016; Reid et al., 2016). Likewise, reporting depressive symptoms was associated with higher BMI and a greater likelihood of obesity even after adjusting for sex, race, age, education, height and weight (Curtis et al., 2016; Franko et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2016; Shomaker & Goodman, 2015). A potential explanation for the association between these psychosocial factors and BMI is their adverse effects on health behaviors such as diet and physical activity (Benjamin et al., 2018; Hayman et al., 2015). In a recent qualitative study, Black adolescents described that experiences of discrimination based on race, sex, and weight contributed to health-compromising behaviors such as sedentariness and unhealthy food choices (Jernigan, 2020). Similar trends were noted for BMI and waist-to-hip ratio among participants who reported experiencing early life adversity (i.e., adverse childhood exposure to crime, violence, and family illness) (Curtis et al., 2016). In 2018, among US adults age ≥20 years, the age-adjusted prevalence of obesity was 55% in non-Hispanic Black women compared to 38% in non-Hispanic White women (Virani et al., 2020). Early adulthood is a critical period when an individual develops health determining behaviors such as physical activity and dietary patterns (Jahangard et al., 2019), which are associated with obesity and subsequent CVD. However, only one study in this review reported dietary behaviors (Diggins et al., 2015). Identifying earlier risk factors for CVD, particularly among African American women, who are disproportionately affected by CVD (Murray et al., 2013), is necessary.

This review also found that racial discrimination, individual racism, and depression are related to higher blood pressure in young African American women (Lee et al., 2016; Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012). Although there is evidence that racial discrimination may be unrelated to changes in diastolic blood pressure, a positive correlation between racial discrimination and systolic blood pressure was found (Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012). These findings are consistent with studies among midlife African American women, which have reported that everyday discrimination, perceived stress, and experiences of racism are associated with elevated blood pressure (Barksdale et al., 2009). In addition, one study found that the association between racial discrimination and blood pressure differed by racial identity and whether participants had an Africentric worldview (Neblett Jr & Carter, 2012). A high Africentric worldview was characterized as nonmaterialistic, communal, and holistic orientation to people. A high Africentric worldview had a protective effect in associations between racial discrimination and diastolic blood pressure. However, these studies were cross-sectional and did not examine blood pressure changes over time. Longitudinal analyses are necessary to examine whether discrimination is related to elevated blood pressure in young African American women.

This systematic review found evidence that several positive psychosocial factors may reduce CVD risk. Two studies observed that mindfulness and prayer were related to lower blood pressure (Cooper et al., 2014; Wright et al., 2018). However, findings of the effect of these positive psychosocial factors on CVD risk are limited and have been inconsistent. A recent systematic review revealed that John Henryism, a measure of high-effort coping (James et al., 1992), is related to CVD and premature death in midlife African American women (Felix et al., 2019). Prolonged high-effort coping can result in premature aging, poor health behaviors, and health deterioration (Geronimus et al., 2006). The effects of high-effort coping were not examined in any of the studies included in this review. Future studies should examine the relationship between high-effort coping and CVD risks in younger women.

Several important pathways were not examined in the literature and may explain the associations between psychosocial factors and CVD risk in young African American women. While studies in this review adjusted for several relevant covariates, including age, education, income, and general health status, the majority did not collect information on dietary and caloric intake, physical activity, medication history, and self-efficacy measures (n=7). Previous studies have assessed the behavioral and physiologic interactions between psychosocial factors (e.g., perceived stress, discrimination, anxiety) and CVD risk (Robertson et al., 2015). Studies suggest that acute or chronic stress exposure can create a series of maladaptive responses in the sympathetic nervous system and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical axis, leading to the suppression of insulin, elevated blood pressure, and enhanced inflammation (Lagraauw et al., 2015). A recent study found that African Americans aged 17–19 years who experienced high levels of racial discrimination had elevated cytokine levels by age 22 years (Brody et al., 2015). These findings highlight the role of pro-inflammatory cytokines in chronic stress (Cunningham et al., 2012; Mwendwa et al., 2013), and the need to investigate inflammatory responses as a potential explanation for the association between psychosocial factors and CVD risk in younger African American women.

It is also important to note that studies in this review did not use an intersectional approach to understand the psychosocial factors driving increased CVD burden among young African American women. Research using an intersectional approach examine how the hierarchies of race, gender, and class mutually create structures of oppression (McCall, 2009). Stress related to experiences of racism, sexism, sexual identity orientation, and rurality have been related to CVD risk in African American women (Caceres et al., 2018; Kalinowski et al., 2019; Taylor et al., 2002). However, the multidimensionality of African American women’s experiences with race, ethnicity, gender, sexual identity, sexual orientation, and geographic location is generally understudied. Further, the way in which these factors interact and their adverse impact on CVD risk has not been assessed. Future work is necessary using an intersectional approach.

Methodological Quality of Studies

While the overall quality of studies in this review was excellent, there were two main areas of weakness or risk of bias: sample size and inadequate or absent description of the characteristics of the responders and non-responders. The sample size in the included studies ranged from 20 to 775 African American women. Also, four of the nine studies were conducted at historically Black colleges or universities. These limitations can introduce selection bias and limit the statistical conclusions and generalizability of findings. Additionally, the stressors of being a college student such as academic performance, financial concerns, pressures to succeed, could have confounding effects on the outcomes (Beiter et al., 2015). Two studies reported correlations (Curtis et al., 2016; Wright et al., 2018), which do not examine the independent associations between psychosocial factors and CVD risk. A major strength of the included studies was the ascertainment of the outcome. Only one study collected outcome data by self-report (Diggins et al., 2015), and the remainder of studies assessed the outcome by record linkage, standard protocol of anthropometric measures, or by trained personnel using calibrated equipment. Additionally, eight out of nine studies used validated questionnaires as measurement tools for stress exposure.

Our review identified several gaps in the current literature. First, there is limited research examining the relationship between psychosocial factors (e.g., perceived stress, racism, discrimination, anxiety, depression) and CVD risk during early adulthood (age 19–24 years) in African American women. Studies relating psychosocial factors to CVD risk in African American women have largely focused on midlife (Barber, Hickson, Kawachi, et al., 2016; Okhomina et al., 2018; Subramanyam et al., 2012). However, before age 30, approximately 14% of African American women have diabetes and overweight, indicating the need for earlier intervention (Clark et al., 2014). Next, the majority of studies in this systematic review report on BMI and blood pressure. There has been little research examining the association between psychosocial stress and other CVD risk factors such as nutrition, physical activity, metabolic syndrome, vascular stiffness, and carotid intima-media thickness. However, research among older women has shown that racial discrimination, perceived stress, and insufficient social support is positively related to CVD risk (Hernandez & Pressler, 2014). It is important to note that research on neighborhood stress, poverty, work environment, sexism, sexual identity, and social stress and its implications for cardiovascular health is also limited. Studies in older women suggest that these stressors influence CVD risk in African American women (Barber, Hickson, Kawachi, et al., 2016; Barber, Hickson, Wang, et al., 2016; Edwards et al., 2012) indicating a need to evaluate the influence in younger women. Thus, future studies using an intersectional approach and relating various sources of stress (i.e., the built environment, systemic and internalized racism, poverty, sexism, childhood trauma) to CVD risk in young adulthood are necessary.

Strengths and Limitation of Review

This study contributes to the existing literature by systematically reviewing the available evidence examining associations between psychosocial factors and CVD risk in young African American women (age 19–24 years). However, several limitations of the present review should be noted. Included studies were restricted to English-language articles published in peer-reviewed journals, which may result in publication bias. Also, the majority (n=7) of studies were completed on college campuses, which may limit generalizability. The small sample size in each study also limited the ability to perform any sub-groups analyses. Finally, there was heterogeneity in the study sample, psychosocial factors, and CVD risk outcome measures, which prevented the use of meta-analytic methods. One strength of this review is that we thoroughly searched three electronic databases (PubMed, APA PsychInfo (EBSCOhost), and CINAHL). Only studies with ≥50% African American women in the study sample, or that provided stratified data for African American women were included; therefore, more rigorous studies were included and reduced the probability of missing eligible studies. We used the PRISMA flow diagram and confirmed that our research topic was unique and registered our protocol on the PROSPERO database.

Conclusions

Findings from the current systematic review suggests that adverse psychosocial factors (i.e., perceived stress, racism, discrimination, and depression) may be associated with CVD risk factors (i.e., BMI, blood pressure) in young African American women. However, there is insufficient evidence to examine associations between a particular psychosocial factor and CVD risk factors. Additional research is necessary to determine the effect of psychosocial factors on cardiovascular health in young African American women. Longitudinal studies are necessary to gain a better understanding of the role of psychosocial factors in CVD risk progression and to identify potential target for future interventions in African American women. Studies should also consider psychosocial factors across multiple levels (e.g., individual, interpersonal, community, societal).
