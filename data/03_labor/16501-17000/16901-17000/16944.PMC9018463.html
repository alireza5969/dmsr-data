State of the science in inflammation and stroke recovery: a systematic review

Abstract

Stroke is a major cause of mortality worldwide, and survivors often have major life-changing disabilities. Annually in the United States, an estimated 795,000 people experience a new or recurrent stroke. All types of stroke involve an inflammatory reaction that follows the initial phase of incidence. However, investigations into any links between inflammatory markers and recovery processes in the context of post-stroke rehabilitation are lacking. In this systematic review, we searched the literature in PubMed, SCOPUS, and CINAHL databases to gather information on inflammatory biomarkers related to stroke and their association with rehabilitation outcomes, according to PRISMA guidelines. Eleven articles (n=1,773 stroke patients) were selected. Immune markers (interleukin 6 [IL-6], C-reactive protein, IL-1α, tumor necrosis factor α, soluble intercellular adhesion molecule 1) and functional status assessments (Modified Rankin Score, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, Functional Independence Measure, etc.) were the primary measures used in the reviewed studies. We found preliminary evidence for the evaluation of inflammatory biomarkers post-stroke, including the role of inflammation in functional recovery and the influence of rehabilitation on inflammation. This is the first systematic review of the topic. The review identifies several gaps in the literature that are critical for understanding the potential use of inflammatory markers to improve post-stroke outcomes.

Introduction

There are over 4 million survivors of stroke living in the United States, and up to 50% experience chronic disability [1]. Disabilities after stroke are numerous and include cognitive and communication issues, reduced ability to walk, and motor deficits [2]. Stroke survivors are also at high risk for a second stroke [3], pulmonary or urinary tract infections [4], and depression [5]. Stroke is a global healthcare and financial burden with associated costs of $34 billion annually in the United States alone [6].

The only US Food and Drug Administration-approved treatments are intravenous thrombolysis and thrombectomy, both with lower than 20% eligibility rates and common serious side effects. Because of the short treatment window for stroke therapy, together with the requirement for neuroimaging to rule out intracerebral hemorrhage, tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) is used for about 7% of acute ischemic stroke patients in the United States [7]. t-PA therapy carries a risk of fatal intracranial hemorrhage and is contraindicated in hemorrhagic stroke [8–10].

This situation has motivated researchers to investigate therapeutic targets beyond restoring blood flow, such as reducing inflammation. Unfortunately, nearly all investigational anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective drugs have failed in clinical trials, despite showing promise in preclinical studies, and few candidates reached phase III trials [11]. In many cases, this failure has been attributed to inadequate assessment of functional and/or long-term outcomes and the use of young healthy animals in preclinical studies that are not representative of clinical cohorts. Inflammation plays a key role in the progression and secondary injury mechanisms after stroke [12]. Early and strenuous intervention with targeted rehabilitation efforts post-stroke is associated with improved functional recovery [13], but many clinicians are concerned that strenuous rehabilitation could increase inflammation, even though it reduced inflammation in a preclinical model [14]. Understanding this association during stroke recovery will help in the development and/or refinement of neuroprotective approaches. Recent consensus-based recommendations encourage the incorporation of stroke recovery biomarkers into rehabilitation research in order to explore the potential of using inflammatory markers to help clinicians diagnose and determine stroke outcomes [15–16].

This is the first systematic review to provide a synopsis of inflammatory biomarkers related to stroke and the recovery process. We incorporated clinical data from studies of acute ischemic stroke that analyzed the role and presence of inflammation contributing to functional recovery. We selected specific inflammatory biomarkers that were reported at different times during patient recovery. Furthermore, we analyzed which inflammatory biomarkers were related to patient rehabilitation outcomes. Our goal was to provide a state-of-the-science update for researchers and clinicians to use and a resource to assist in investigating and developing new stroke therapeutics.

Methods

Literature search and selection criteria

This review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines [17]. Articles were identified by searching PubMed, SCOPUS, and CINAHL databases. A systematic search of PubMed, SCOPUS and CINAHL was used to indicate clinical studies addressing the role of inflammatory biomarkers in relation to functional recovery in stroke patients. The initial search involved the terms “stroke”, “inflammation”, and “rehabilitation”. The search was limited to human clinical studies published in English and was supplemented by reviewing additional references from included studies. The references of selected articles were also analyzed for relevance. The database search was conducted on October 6, 2020. We included studies that 1) reported the role of inflammatory biomarkers in stroke patients, 2), 3) evaluated inflammatory biomarkers in stroke patients, and 4) evaluated functional recovery post-stroke.

Results

Data extraction

In total, 117 articles were selected for initial review (Figure). The process of article selection for final review resulted in 11 articles and is summarized in the Figure. For the 11 studies included in this review, the primary baseline characteristics are described in Table 1, and study outcomes are in Table 2. Baseline characteristics were extracted from 11 articles and included patient number, age, sex, current stroke diagnosis, and concurrent conditions, including diabetes, hypertension, depression, hyperlipidemia, or atrial fibrillation. All 11 articles reported smoking status, drinking status, time since stroke, inflammatory markers interleukin 6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), IL-1α, tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), and body mass index (BMI), A subset of articles also reported the US National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, cholesterol levels, and white blood cell count, weight, obesity, soluble intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (sICAM-1) level, Modified Rankin Score (mRS), plasma glucose, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure. Patient outcome measurements were extracted from all articles and included serum levels of the inflammatory biomarkers IL-6 (pg/ml), CRP (mg/dl), IL-1 α (pg/ml), TNF-α (pg/ml), and sICAM-1 (ng/ml). Data for the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE), NIHSS, mRS, and Barthel Index were available in a subset of articles to characterize functional recovery post stroke, and the Functional Independence Measure (FIM) was available in 2 articles to evaluate functional status.

Quality assessment

Appendix A.1 describes all of the studies included in the systematic review. The methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed by Downs and Black valid and reliable checklist [18]. Ten articles were characterized as “good” quality, and one [19] had “fair” quality, described in Appendix A.2.

Patient demographics

This review describes 1,773 patients (mean [SD] age 67.5 [8.7] years; 46% male) with a diagnosis of acute stroke. The most common concurrent conditions mentioned across all studies included diabetes (407 [29%] patients, 3 articles with no data), hypertension (918 [64%] patients, 3 articles with no data), depression (172 [18%] patients, 9 articles with no data), hyperlipidemia (55 [32%] patients, 9 articles with no data), and atrial fibrillation (298 [29%] patients, 8 articles with no data). To treat these concurrent conditions, several patients were taking medications including aspirin (134 [64%]), statins (68 [49%]), beta blockers (8 [33%]), and angiotensinconverting enzyme inhibitors (65 [40%]). The mean (SD) weight was 76.8 (7.4) kg; 14 (33%) patients had a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. The mean time since the first stroke was 20.9 days (range 1 to 74.9). The mean NIHSS and mRS scores (3.9 [3.0] and 3.3 [1.7]) indicated stroke severity and functional impairment in the sample.

Inflammatory biomarkers, functional recovery, and relation between inflammatory markers and functional recovery

CRP level was the most common inflammatory marker, discussed in 8 studies, with IL-6 (pg/ml) the second most discussed marker, in 5 studies. The mean baseline levels for inflammatory biomarkers across the 11 studies were for IL-6, 8.8 (4.7) pg/ml; CRP, 1.4 (1.0) mg/dl; IL-1 α, 2.8 (0.0) pg/ml; TNF-α, 62.3 (89.0) pg/ml; and sICAM-1, 387.7 (167.3) ng/ml.

Only 4 studies provided repeated measures of inflammatory markers:

Manolescu et al.[8] found a mean IL-6 level of 19.2 (1.7) pg/ml upon admission to inpatient rehabilitation, which significantly increased to 28.9 (10.1) pg/ml at patient discharge, with a mean hospitalization of 13 (1.0) days (p<0.05). Aquilani et al.[20] found a mean IL-6 level at admission to inpatient rehabilitation of 19.24 (23.01) pg/ml versus a mean level at patient discharge of 10.4 (7.8) pg/ml, with a mean hospitalization of 45 (7) days. Ryan et al. [21] found a mean IL-6 level of 10.9 (11.1) pg/ml in stroke patients pre-resistive training versus a mean level of 12.0 (12.6) pg/ml after 12 weeks of training. Yeh et al.[19] found a mean sICAM-1 level pre–robot-assisted therapy of 275.3 (80.4) ng/mL versus a mean level of 258.5 (75.0) ng/mL after therapy.

The most common functional recovery measure, reported in 4 of the 11 studies, was the NIHSS score. The mRS score was used in only 4 studies. Other recovery measures included the FIM or the Barthel Index. Four studies used the MMSE to assess patient mental state upon hospital admission and to define cognitive recovery; for symptoms of post-stroke depression, the Hamilton Depression Scale was used in 1 study. Patient outcomes were assessed at various times, most commonly at patient admission or discharge.

Inflammatory markers and functional recovery findings

Rothenburg et al.[22] studied 48 ischemic stroke patients within their first month post-stroke (mean NIHSS score 6.8 [4.0] and mean MMSE score 26.4 [3.8]) and found a relation between inflammation and cognitive impairment post-stroke. Specifically, the authors found significant correlation of MMSE score with levels of IL-6 (r = −0.33, p=0.025) and CRP (r = −0.46, p=0.002) and that they were significantly intercorrelated (r=0.48, p=0.001). When patient risk factors (diabetes, smoking, obesity, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia) were included in the analyses, serum CRP level remained significantly associated with MMSE score (βCRP = −0.46, p=0.002). Similarly, after adjustment for age, infarct side, and level of education, CRP level was the only significant predictor of MMSE score (F1,44 = 11.3, p=0.002). MMSE scores ranged from 13 to 30; 21% of the sample had evidence of cognitive impairment (MMSE score ≤ 24), but the sample size for this exploratory study was limited and may have influenced the detection of associations between cognitive impairment, stroke severity, and levels of inflammatory markers, specifically IL-6 and interferon δ.

Meng et al.[9] assessed levels of inflammatory biomarkers the morning after admission for patients with and without post-stroke depression (PSD). Mean levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were significantly higher in the PSD than non-PSD group (IL-6: 4.6 [1.0] vs 3.4 [0.9], TNF-α: 224.4 [44.1] vs 164.9 [43.7]). Also, nerve growth factor levels were lower for patients with than without PSD (mean 6.5 vs 8.1 ng/L). In general, patients with PSD more frequently than non-PSD patients had higher NIHSS scores (median, 3 vs 1), as well as lower MMSE scores (median, 25 vs 29). The median (interquartile range) NIHSS of 1.0 (0–2) in the non-PSD group and 3 (1–6) in the PSD group did not include people with more severe stroke and limits the generalizability of the results.

Regarding stroke patient recovery, Manolescu et al.[8] found a decreasing trend for Lp(a) level but an increasing trend for levels of IL-6, IL-8, and sICAM-1 at discharge from inpatient rehabilitation. Levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and Lp(a) inflammatory markers were significantly increased in stroke patients versus healthy controls at admission to inpatient rehabilitation. Improvement in functional recovery, measured by the Barthel index, was correlated with decreased levels of IL-1α TNF-α, and LP(a). Only 18 patients with stroke were included in this study and levels of inflammatory markers may be confounded by age and comorbidities of the study participants.

Aquilani et al.[20] studied inflammatory markers at admission to and discharge from inpatient rehabilitation in patients admitted within 90 days post-stroke. Upon admission, the study sample showed elevated IL-6 and CRP levels. Overall, 55% of the 94 stroke patients showed an inflammatory status related to significantly lower FIM scores. At admission, functional disability and IL-6 level were not related, although a significant association was found between functional disability, measured by FIM, and the liver shift of protein synthesis. Serum albumin level was an independent predictor of functional independence at admission and was inversely associated with IL-6 level. During the rehabilitation stage of stroke, IL-6 and albumin levels maintained their inverse relation. The gain in functional independence (FIM) averaged 17.8 points for stroke patients with inflammation and 15.6 points for those without inflammation. At discharge, the inflammation rate, although reduced, was still present in 60% of the patients with inflammation and was related to poor functional status (p<0.03). FIM score was positively correlated with blood hemoglobin, serum albumin, plasma histidine, tryptophan, and tyrosine levels. The study separately analyzed inflammatory markers for the subset (32%) of patients with a diagnosis of dysphagia and found the prevalence of inflammation higher in individuals with than without dysphagia (73% vs 43%) and related to lower FIM scores for patients with high inflammation and dysphagia (35.3 points lower). Patients with and without dysphagia showed improved FIM scores during rehabilitation, by 14 and 17 points, respectively. Patients with than without dysphagia more frequently exhibited infection during rehabilitation (66% vs. 37%).

Smith et al.[23] reported an association of IL-6 peak plasma level with CT-detected infarct volume (0.26, p=0.19) and stroke severity (0.37, p=0.03) at 5 to 7 days post-stroke, and functional recovery outcomes, mRS and Barthel Index. Specifically, peak plasma IL-6 level was significantly correlated with Barthel Index at 3 months (r = −0.074), mRS score at 12 months (r=0.77), and Barthel Index at 12 months (r = −0.77). Strong correlations were found for CRP level and white blood cell count and functional recovery outcomes, NIHSS, mRS, and Barthel Index, at all times, 1 week, 3 months and 12 months. This was the only article to compare CRP level with functional recovery (mRS) from 5 to 7 days post-stroke up to 1 year. Here, the authors noted a mean CRP level of 0.54 mg/dl in the NIHSS cohort at 5 to 7 days post-stroke, 0.63 mg/dl at 3 months post-stroke, and 0.65 mg/dl at 12 months post-stroke. However, the sample size was limited, with 34 participants available for week-1 data and 19 for 12-month data.

Winbeck et al.[24] also demonstrated an association between serial CRP level during the acute phase of stroke with functional outcomes. The authors analyzed 3 CRP level at 3 times. CRP 1 level (mean 0.86 [0.33] mg/dl) was measured immediately after patient admission within 12 hr of symptom onset, CRP 2 level (1.22 [0.67] mg/dl) was measured 24 hr after stroke symptom onset, and CRP 3 level (1.8 [1.0] mg/dl) was measured 24 hr after the second measurement. The initial CRP level determined within 12 hr of symptom onset failed to predict new fatal or nonfatal cerebrovascular events or functional outcome after follow-up at 1 year. CRP level assessed at 12–24 hr after ischemic stroke predicted endpoint events and an unfavorable outcome after stroke (odds ratio, 3.5). Furthermore, patients with low CRP level at 24 hr after symptom onset had poor functional recovery as measured by the Barthel Index at 1 year. The authors concluded that the combination of initial diffusion weighted imaging lesion volume and CRP measurement at 24 hr after symptom onset may aid in identifying patients who are predisposed to activation of the inflammatory process due to even small infarcts.

Geng et al.[7] found poor functional status (mRS score 3 to 6) predicted by CRP level within the first 24 hr of admission (odds ratio, 4.89) and direct bilirubin level (odds ratio, 1.79) at discharge, on average 2 weeks after admission. Age and rural residence were also associated with poor functional status, which may also be related to other causes of inflammation.

Rabadi et al.[25] investigated the association between CRP level at inpatient rehabilitation admission and change in functional status at discharge. Functional measures at admission and not CRP level predicted improvement in function at discharge. Elevated CRP level at admission was related to discharge to an institution (vs home) (66% vs 44%, p<0.03) as well as low functional status, motricity index and FIM and increased frequency of infection at admission (57% vs 29%, p<0.005).

Winovich et al.[26] found cognitive decline in men post-stroke associated with levels of CRP (p =0.01 adjusted for interaction) and IL-6 (p =0.04 adjusted for interaction). The authors also found IL-6 level associated with post-stroke survival for individuals < 80 years old. One limitation of the study was the amount of missing data for activities of daily living (ADL). When ADL data were imputed and characterized as ADL decline or no decline, increased CRP and IL-6 levels were significant risk factors for poor functional outcome.

Yeh et al.[19] demonstrated that 4 weeks of rehabilitation reduced level of the oxidative stress biomarker (malondialdehyde [MDA], z=2.54, p<0.05), so rehabilitation alleviated oxidative stress in these patients. The authors found a significant correlation between pre-MDA and post-Wolf Motor Function Test time (ρ = 0.462, p<0.05). After rehabilitation, patients showed a significant improvement in functional outcomes as indicated by the mRS (z-score = 3.45, p=0.001) and Stroke Impact Scale score (z-score = 2.98, p=0.003).

Ryan et al.[21] found that levels of inflammatory markers did not increase after 12 weeks of resistive training in a pilot study of 20 subacute patients post-stroke. CRP level decreased 12% but not significantly. Of note, baseline inflammatory values for this patient population were within or near normal limits for the inflammatory markers IL-6 (10.9 [11.1] ρg/mL), IL-8 (13.6 [15.3] ρg/mL), CRP (6.5 [8.5] mg/mL), and TNF-α (16.3 [5.9] ρg/mL), which may limit the generalizability to patients with greater baseline inflammation.

There was insufficient longitudinal data across any of the 11 studies to perform outcome analyses at multiple times.

Discussion

In this review, we sought to understand changes in inflammatory biomarkers post-stroke, the association between these changes and functional outcomes, and the impact of rehabilitation on inflammation post-stroke. Eleven studies met inclusion criteria for this review, and overall, they suggest preliminary evidence for the evaluation of inflammatory biomarkers post-stroke including the role of inflammation in functional recovery and the effect of rehabilitation on inflammation. There is more substantial evidence to support the use of inflammatory biomarkers to predict post-stroke mortality.

Inflammatory biomarkers post-stroke

There is limited and conflicting evidence on the time course of inflammation post-stroke. Following stroke, IL-6 is released by activated microglia within the central nervous system [27], and this release is mirrored by increased peripheral IL-6 level [28]. Rothenburg et al.[22] found no association of levels of CRP and IL-6 with time post-stoke, ranging from 5 days to 1 month post-stroke. Manolescu et al.[8] noted an increase in IL-6 level from admission to discharge from inpatient rehabilitation, whereas Aquilani et al.[20] found a reduction in IL-6 level from admission to discharge from inpatient rehabilitation. Ryan et al.[21] found no change in IL-6 level from pre- to post-resistive training. Winbeck et al.[24] found CRP level from 12–24 hr post-stroke but not before 12 hr or from 24–48 hr associated with mortality and functional recovery. In Rabadi et al.[25], patients with greater time since stroke had higher levels of CRP than other patients; however, they also had longer acute hospitalizations and, presumably, more severe strokes. If inflammatory markers are used as a prognostic factor or an indicator for a specific intervention, one must first have a good understanding of the natural progression of inflammation post-stroke. Furthermore, an understanding of the degree to which inflammation is related to the stroke event versus being indicative of comorbidities, age, and medication will be important in determining the role of inflammatory markers in patient care post-stroke.

Association between inflammatory markers and functional status

There is emerging evidence that inflammation is related to functional status. Meng et al.[9], Manolescu et al.[8], Aquilani et al.[20], Smith et al.[23], Winbeck et al.[24], Geng et al.[7], Rabadi et al.[25], and Winovich et al.[26] all reported associations between inflammation and functional status (mRS, Barthel Index, FIM, dysphagia, etc.). Rothenburg et al.[22] and Winovich et al.[26] found relations between inflammation (IL-6 and CRP levels) and cognitive impairment post-stroke. Winovich et al.[26] and Winbeck et al.[24] found an association between inflammation and stroke survival. Meng et al.[9] found IL-6 and TNF-α levels associated with PSD.

Impact of rehabilitation on inflammation

Four of the 11 studies measured inflammation pre- and post-rehabilitation. These studies generally found decreased levels of inflammatory markers after rehabilitation. Manolescu et al.[8] found decreased levels of IL-1α, TNF-α, and LP(a) related to functional improvement (Barthel Index); however, this study had a small sample size (n=18) and provided no description of the rehabilitation. Aquilani et al.[20] found that patients both with and without inflammation showed improved their functional status (FIM) with rehabilitation (specific rehabilitation unspecified). Inflammation levels were generally decreased, but 60% of patients with inflammation on admission had inflammation at discharge, which was associated with poor functional status (FIM). Yeh et al.[19] found oxidative stress (MDA) decreased after 4 weeks of upper-limb intervention. Ryan et al.[21] found no change in inflammation after 12 weeks of lower-limb resistive training. Of note, stroke patients represent a heterogenous population, and therefore inclusion criteria and participant demographics should be carefully considered when evaluating changes in inflammatory markers. Additionally, the time post-stroke that a study is conducted is critical in that we expect functional differences and rehabilitation strategies to vary in the acute, subacute, and chronic stages.

Given the small number of available clinical studies, we lack sufficient information on the association of inflammation and recovery outcomes in stroke patients. From the results of this review, the inflammatory biomarkers CRP and IL-6 are indicators of this process. Patient comorbidities, stroke severity, and age also contribute to the acute stroke recovery trajectory and overall worse functional outcomes and are associated with a more pronounced elevation of levels of these biomarkers.

Clinical implications

This review indicates an emerging understanding of the role of inflammation in the natural progression of post-stroke recovery and in rehabilitation-aided recovery. Ultimately, such knowledge may help clinicians know when and how to optimize post-stroke rehabilitation. For example, rehabilitation professionals debate when to start rehabilitation [29]. Data on the progression of inflammation and inflammatory changes during rehabilitation would help clarify whether early rehabilitation is helpful or harmful. Clinicians also debate the intensity and type of rehabilitation. Understanding whether intense rehabilitation (e.g., rehabilitation that stresses the patient such as strenuous exercise or long duration exercise) has a meaningful negative impact on inflammation is important because clinicians largely avoid this rehabilitation due to health safety concerns.

Limitations

Most of the studies reviewed presented pilot data with small sample sizes. Several of these studies were performed at a single center, and future studies should test whether observations in these studies are generalizable to larger patient populations. The studies also varied greatly regarding stroke patient selection and recruitment, stroke baseline severity, time to intervention, and the measures used to assess functional recovery and inflammation. Although stroke severity was assessed and included as a covariate, the timing of administration of the NIHSS relative to the stroke and other assessments varied among participants. Most of the studies were not designed or powered to assess cerebrovascular risk factors. Additionally, in most of the studies, inflammatory biomarkers were measured only once at admission. Future studies are needed to determine whether other inflammatory biomarkers would be more useful than CRP level. Further research including longitudinal data is crucial to assess inflammatory biomarkers at several times during stroke recovery and rehabilitation. The results of this review suggest preliminary evidence that inflammation biomarkers are associated with functional status post-stroke; however further research is needed to define the nature and time course of the association during specific rehabilitation treatments, which vary in intensity. Additionally, studies need to standardize the functional status measures and inflammatory biomarkers that are reported to improve comparisons and generalizability.

Conclusion

The intention of this review was to determine the state of the science for specific inflammatory biomarkers in post-stroke patients at various times after stroke symptom onset and to determine any association between inflammation and functional recovery. The literature reveals emerging evidence for evaluating inflammatory biomarkers post-stroke, the role of inflammation in functional recovery, and the influence of rehabilitation on inflammation. We found more substantial evidence to support the use of inflammatory biomarkers to predict post-stroke mortality. This review supports the need for further investigation, using longitudinal data, of the association between inflammation and rehabilitation-related recovery to determine, and optimise, patient outcomes after stroke.
