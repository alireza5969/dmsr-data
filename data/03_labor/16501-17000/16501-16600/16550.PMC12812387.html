Hypoglycemia and Dysautonomia After Bariatric Surgery: a Systematic Review and Perspective

[http://orcid.org/0000-0002-7636-106X] Poppy Addison[1]

Abstract

Introduction

With the increasing performance of bariatric surgery, rare complications are becoming prevalent. We review the diagnosis and treatment of dysautonomia after bariatric surgery and the limited treatment options available. We summarize the suggested mechanisms and explain why a complete understanding of the etiology has yet to be determined.

Methods

In accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, a systematic review was performed.

Results

Of 448 studies identified in the literature search, 4 studies were reviewed, describing 87 patients diagnosed with dysautonomia. We present a patient who developed severe dysautonomia following conversion of sleeve gastrectomy to gastric bypass.

Conclusion

Treatment needs to focus on optimizing nutrition, avoiding hypoglycemia, and optimizing volume status.

Introduction

After Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), patients can experience a cluster of symptoms including near-syncope, syncope, seizures, episodic diaphoresis, and nausea/vomiting; these often range in severity and can represent a diagnostic dilemma for bariatric surgeons [1]. When symptoms are mild, they can be attributed to either early dumping syndrome and/or hypoglycemia occurring later in the postprandial period. Fortunately, the prevalence of severe neuroglycopenia leading to hospitalization for syncope, seizure-like events, and severe hypotension is rare, with registry studies indicating hospitalization is needed in fewer than 1% of post-bypass patients [2].

Initial case reports demonstrated nesidioblastosis on pancreatic pathology in patients with severe post-bariatric hypoglycemia, implicating a role for pancreatic islet dysfunction as the major mediator of hypoglycemia [3, 4]. This finding had a profound impact on the bariatric community, prompting the use of partial pancreatectomy for therapy. Unfortunately, the majority of patients treated with partial pancreatectomy develop recurrent hypoglycemia after surgery [2, 5]. Moreover, subsequent clinical investigations demonstrated normal fasting insulin secretion, but excessive glucose-dependent insulin secretion in the postprandial state, in turn related to markedly increased postprandial secretion of incretin hormones such as GLP1 [6]. Together, these findings have led to realization that the pancreas is largely an innocent bystander, and that incretin-dependent mechanisms resulting from intestinal remodeling are the predominant culprit. As a result, pancreatic resection should be avoided, except in rare cases with a coexisting insulinoma.

Most patients with post-bariatric hypoglycemia are treated as outpatients, including medical nutrition therapy focused on reduced intake of simple carbohydrates and optimization of overall nutrition, acarbose to limit carbohydrate absorption, and agents to reduce incretin and insulin secretion. Some patients do not fully respond and continue to have recurrent severe hypoglycemia and neuroglycopenia. In such patients, reversal of bypass surgery, conversion to pyloric-sparing operations to reduce the rate of gastric emptying2, or provision of nutrient delivery into the excluded stomach can be considered to reduce the excessive postprandial secretion of incretins and insulin.

Even in patients achieving some degree of success in reducing hypoglycemia, autonomic symptoms can emerge as a dominant clinical picture. Like hypoglycemia, these symptoms rarely develop early in the post-operative course. Furthermore, patients that develop these complex neurologic symptoms generally have excessive weight loss. The underlying etiology of these symptoms remains uncertain but could be related to hypoglycemia-induced autonomic failure, as described for type 1 diabetes [7], or related to an unknown nutritional deficiency. What is certainly clear is that merely decreasing the rate of gastric emptying or resection of pancreatic tissue will not reverse the complex neuropathic symptoms that can occur.

The purpose of this review is to bring awareness to the complex spectrum of patients that have severe hypoglycemia, neuroglycopenia, and dysautonomia. While these terms are heavily related, they are not interchangeable; dysautonomia specifically refers to a disordered autonomic nervous system and neuroglycopenia to symptoms of low glucose levels in the brain. Given that this combination is often refractory to treatment, patients with even mild symptoms, especially in those with excessive weight loss, should be evaluated for early intervention. Even mild symptoms can be a harbinger of bad things to come, and not representative of a great outcome. We describe a recent case which illustrates the clinical challenges of managing this constellation of symptoms.

Methods

To estimate the incidence of post-gastric bypass dysautonomia, a systematic literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus was performed in April 2021 using the following search strategy: “bariatric surgery” AND “dysautonomia” OR “autonomic dysfunction” OR “syncope” OR “hypotension” (MeSH terms in supplemental data). This search was supplemented by hand-reviewing the references of included studies to identify potentially relevant articles. The protocol was not prospectively registered in a database of systematic reviews. The results were reviewed by two independent authors for relevance and appropriateness, and any disagreements were reconciled by a third author. There was no assessment of bias. Studies were included if they were case reports or database studies in patients undergoing bariatric surgery who had experienced postoperative dysautonomia. Systematic reviews or meta-analyses and any studies where hypoglycemia was the primary outcome were excluded. Given that this review focuses on gastric bypass, any non-gastric bypass bariatric surgery or studies without specificity for which bariatric surgery was performed were excluded. If a study included patients who underwent gastric bypass in addition to other non-gastric bypass bariatric surgery, the study was included for the purposes of including those relevant patients. These strategies were used to identify the original patients to avoid inclusion of the same patient in both a case report and a systematic review and to only evaluate the outcome of interest rather than including patients who experienced syncope due to any other cause.

Results

Of 448 studies identified in the literature search, 18 studies met initial eligibility criteria for review (Fig. 1). Additional studies were excluded due to non-bypass intervention (6), primary outcome not dysautonomia (4), or systematic or narrative reviews (4), leaving 4 studies for inclusion (Table 1). Described in these studies are 87 patients who experienced dysautonomia after gastric bypass. Two studies present their data in combination with some patients who underwent sleeve gastrectomy. Given that there are few sleeve gastrectomy cases compared to the number of gastric bypass cases in these studies, we present approximate medical history, with the caveat that these numbers are not exact.

Two papers presented describe a single patient who experienced dysautonomia after gastric bypass, both diagnosed after tilt table testing [8]. One patient required a cardiac pacemaker [10], and one was treated with dietary changes and midodrine and fludrocortisone but with only 3 days of follow-up [8]. Billakanty et al. describe 14 patients who were referred for dysautonomic symptoms, ultimately with 13 diagnosed with dysautonomia with a tilt table test and responsive to medical therapy [9]. Zhang et al. present a retrospective review of a prospective registry of 4500 patients undergoing bariatric surgery, identifying 4% with confirmed dysautonomia [11].

Most patients described in these studies are women, who had experienced 50–60 kg postoperative weight loss. Percent weight loss and rapidity of weight loss were not frequently reported. Initial symptoms occurred about 5–7 months after surgery and included near-syncope or syncope, dizziness, nausea, and fatigue. Diagnostic work-up to evaluate for dehydration, anemia, adrenal insufficiency, or cardiac etiologies with electrocardiogram or echocardiogram were negative. Patients were largely diagnosed with dysautonomia based on a positive tilt table test, and most were treated with medical therapy, with one requiring a cardiac pacemaker. Symptoms were described as improving after treatment, but follow-up duration ranged from only 3 days to 5 years. Few studies highlighted the overlap of hypoglycemia and dysautonomia.

Case Report

A 51-year-old woman with a history of Class III obesity (BMI = 70 kg/m2) underwent a sleeve gastrectomy, achieving a weight loss of 90 kg; she required conversion to a gastric bypass for intractable GERD. Fourteen months following the conversion, she developed repeated episodes of syncope, describing the episodes as feeling lightheaded then losing consciousness. At this point, her weight had decreased from 180 to 70 kg (BMI = 27 kg/m2). She also developed a neurogenic bladder requiring implantation of a nerve stimulator and bradyarrhythmia requiring a permanent pacemaker. Following a series of falls, fainting episodes, and concussions, additional neurological evaluations were conducted. Initially, she was placed on several anti-seizure medications; however, no seizure focus was found. She had an episode of syncope and cardiac evaluation revealed only bradycardia. She was referred to endocrinology, and continuous glucose monitoring was initiated. Low sensor glucose (< 70 mg/dL) was found 60% of the time, with nadir sensor glucose in the 30s.

She was referred to our tertiary bariatric center for further management. At this time, she was on over 20 medications. We began a process of sequential weaning of non-essential medications. While being evaluated, she had additional syncopal episodes and was hospitalized. Additional symptoms included severe dysphagia and constipation. Her vitals and physical exam were normal, with fasting glucose 70 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c 5.4%., and appropriate fasting insulin of 5 uU/ml. Vitamins B1, B12, D, folate, and zinc levels were normal. Albumin was 4 g/dL. EGD showed a hiatal hernia but no obstruction. CT scan was essentially normal except for high stool load.

Our impression was that profound dysautonomia was contributing to not only the fainting spells but also other reported symptoms and might be also linked to hypoglycemic events. After a thorough discussion involving multiple family members, we offered to reconstruct more normal anatomy by returning the Roux limb to the proximal duodenum and inserting a feeding jejunostomy (Fig. 2). It was clearly explained that the goal of the surgery would be to improve nutritional status and reduce the likelihood of hypoglycemic episodes, but resolution of all symptoms would be unlikely. In the operating room, a retrocolic, antegastric Roux limb was identified (2D). The jejunum was divided 20 cm distal to the gastrojejunostomy and anastomosed to the antrum in the remnant stomach with running absorbable barbed suture (2E, 2F). The distal portion of the Roux limb was anastomosed as an interposition between the gastric pouch and remnant to reduce the risk of recurrent esophageal reflux with a full reconstruction of the stomach (2F). A methylene blue leak test was negative. The jejunojejunostomy was identified, and the biliopancreatic (BP) limb was divided (2F). The cut end of the Roux limb was anastomosed to the BP limb with a running barbed suture to restore intestinal continuity (2G). A jejunal feeding tube was placed proximally.

She tolerated the procedure well and was discharged 12 days postoperatively on tube feeds from 8 pm to 8 am. At the most recent follow-up at 6 months postoperatively, she has resumed some activities, has had no further episodes of syncope or falls, but still complains of dysphagia and constipation. She remains dependent on her feeding tube and is on a bowel regimen to reduce constipation. Follow-up endoscopy and CT scans show no evidence of obstruction. Despite these improvements, she still has significant difficulty eating with frequent vomiting and will require chronic alimentation from the feeding tube unless oral intake improves dramatically.

Discussion

Given the dramatic increase in the performance of bariatric surgery for severe obesity, the incidence of neurologic complications has increased. Although there have been few reports of orthostatic hypotension and dysautonomia, this complication has probably been under reported and poorly understood. Consistent with the experience of our patient, patients with this symptom complex are often passed from specialist to specialist as the underlying cause of these diverse symptoms may not be fully considered. Indeed, our review demonstrated a paucity of literature on dysautonomia after gastric bypass. However, based on the available data, it seems that patients present after a large initial weight loss about 6 months post-operatively. Initial management with dietary and lifestyle changes can be effective, but patients who that fail those modalities may require individualized treatment, as we have presented.

We do not understand mechanisms underlying the development of post-bariatric dysautonomia but can consider several possibilities linked to direct and indirect consequences of RYGB-induced rearrangement of nutrient flow, weight loss, and alterations in systemic metabolism. RYGB induces (1) complex changes in neural regulation of appetite and satiety [12]; (2) profound changes in multiple hormones including GLP 1, peptide YY, FGF19, ghrelin, and leptin; and (3) alterations in hepatic and bile acid metabolism. Moreover, postsurgical hormonal changes increase vagal activity, increase parasympathetic tome, decrease sympathetic tone, and increase heart rate variability, leading to impaired vasoconstriction and intravascular volume depletion that can collectively contribute to orthostatic hypotension [10, 9]. Yet to some extent, these changes occur in every RYGB patient. Why do so few develop the severe symptoms of autonomic dysfunction and outcomes experienced by our patient? Why do symptoms occur late in the postoperative state, after most patients reach their nadir weight loss, rather than concurrent with the early hormonal changes?

While we do not yet know the answers to these key questions, our literature review indicates that most of the patients reported thus far had experienced massive weight loss between 60 and 90 kg, suggesting that there could be a component of malnutrition or unrecognized micronutrient deficiency contributing to autonomic neuropathy. Moreover, patients who experience repetitive severe hypoglycemia may develop hypoglycemia-associated autonomic failure (HAAF), a condition initially described in individuals with type 1 diabetes who develop hypoglycemia unawareness and reduced counterregulatory responses to hypoglycemia [7]. Multiple aspects of the post-surgical environment may also directly modulate key effectors of the autonomic nervous system. When creating the gastric pouch, the linear stapler can interrupt branches of the anterior and posterior vagus nerves. The impact of vagal innervation on gut hormones and the brain-gut-liver metabolic axis is currently gaining research attention. Chemical vagotomy with atropine results in lower ghrelin levels [13, 14] and lower postprandial pancreatic polypeptide release [15]. Surgical vagotomy reduces responsiveness to exogenous ghrelin, without expected increases in food intake [16]. Likewise, alterations in ghrelin and GLP1 after gastric bypass modulate vagal activity and cellular excitability in opposing ways. Ghrelin hyperpolarizes vagal cells, decreasing their activity, while GLP-1 increases intracellular cAMP concentration, increasing excitability [17]. The changes in gut hormone secretion are dynamic across the postoperative time course; as weight loss ensues, ghrelin levels rise to higher than those found preoperatively [18]. However, increased vagal tone persists in patients for at least a year after gastric bypass [19], regardless of the amount of weight loss [20]. Whether these factors differ in patients with dysautonomia is completely unknown. Clearly, additional research will be required to define mechanisms responsible for post-bypass autonomic dysfunction. Possible etiologies include nutritional or vitamin/mineral deficiency, vagal injury, hypoglycemia, or another currently unconsidered cause. To some extent, these changes also occur in duodenal switch or in one-anastomosis gastric bypass. There are no reports yet of patients experiencing the full cascade of hypoglycemia and dysautonomia, and given that a single anastomosis duodenal switch (SADI) or one anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) are newer procedures, it is unclear if there is a difference in outcomes or if the long-term outcomes are not yet known. However, we do not yet know whether dysautonomia will also be observed after these procedures. As these become more common, and patients begin to experience long-term complications, it will be important to consider this syndrome in order to define relative prevalence in patients undergoing these distinct surgical procedures.

Primary care physicians and bariatric surgeons can screen their long-term patients with a thorough review of symptoms that includes near-syncope, episodic diaphoresis, recurrent nausea/vomiting, disordered GI motility, and disordered urodynamics and obtaining a focused history. The initial management for symptoms of dysautonomia in post-bariatric patients described in the literature is varied, but includes diet modifications, volume expansion with increased sodium intake or fludrocortisone, blood pressure elevation with midodrine or methylphenidate, and cardiac pacemaker placement. In our estimation, all treat only the symptoms and not the underlying issue. While we presented only one patient in this report, we are consulted about multiple similar patients on an annual basis. Our most vital recommendation is that patients with true syncope, seizures, or other neuroglycopenia should undergo aggressive diagnostic evaluation and treatment. Even with less severe symptoms, the possibility of hypoglycemia should be fully explored by an experienced endocrinology team, even in a patient without classical symptoms of hypoglycemia, as recurrent hypoglycemia can cause hypoglycemia unawareness. Evaluation of hypoglycemia should include detailed medical and hormonal evaluation with specific assessment of orthostatic hypotension and peripheral neuropathy. Blinded continuous glucose monitoring with concurrent symptom and food/activity diaries can help determine patterns of glucose in relation to symptoms, meals, and activity. All patients should be counseled to modify their diet to include more frequent smaller meals with low glycemic index. If hypoglycemia is documented but inadequately controlled with diet, medications can be added sequentially as needed to reduce the frequency and severity of hypoglycemia and improve safety (e.g., acarbose, other medications to reduce incretin and insulin secretion [6]) (Fig. 3). Persistence of severe hypoglycemia may warrant placement of a feeding tube into the remnant stomach for delivery of most nutritional needs and consideration of reversal of surgical anatomy.

If laboratory evidence of hypoglycemia is not found, the differential diagnosis should be broadened to encompass additional cardiovascular and autonomic disorders. Prolonged assessment of cardiac rhythm and tilt table testing may be required. Patients with evidence of orthostasis should be advised to wear lower extremity compression hose, increase salt and water intake, and potentially consider therapy with fludrocortisone or midodrine. Early placement of a feeding tube in the gastric remnant, if possible, should be considered to improve nutrition and correct any unappreciated micronutrient deficiencies. If autonomic symptoms resolve with insertion of a feeding tube, we are far more optimistic about the impact of surgical revision.

In the patient described here, we elected to place a jejunal feeding tube at the same time as revisional surgery, as the patient did not have a remnant due to prior sleeve gastrectomy and noninvasive insertion was not feasible. We additionally felt that reversal would maximize any chance that she would not be dependent on enteral nutrition. To date, we have been successful in reducing her triggering events, but she remains dependent on a bladder stimulator and continues to experience dysphagia and severe constipation.

Inherent in every systematic review, this study has several important limitations. Most notably, there is inconsistent reporting of methods of diagnosis and follow-up after treatment. We recommend that future reviews of patients with dysautonomia clearly describe how patients were diagnosed and for how long they were followed after treatment to evaluate the robustness of their treatment and response. Given that we attempted to describe the incidence and current diagnosis and treatment strategies, we were unable to report on the study quality. As more patients are diagnosed, future studies could consider comparing treatments to identify an optimal strategy.

Conclusion

After gastric bypass, patients may experience a complex cluster of symptoms that requires careful diagnostic evaluation to differentiate among dumping syndrome, insulinoma, post-bariatric hypoglycemia, and dysautonomia. In patients with dysautonomia, we propose a treatment strategy and suggest placement of feeding tubes prior to embarking on definitive reversal if patients fail medical management. This review identified a need for further investigation into this rare complication to clearly define the diagnosis and best treatment modalities.

Key Points

  1. Hypoglycemia should be assessed in patients with syncope or autonomic symptoms.

  2. Medical therapy should be considered for orthostasis but can be ineffective.

  3. Advanced cases may not benefit from surgical intervention.

  4. Successful treatment of hypoglycemia may not reverse symptoms of dysautonomia.
