Abstract

The current systematic review examines the behavioral effects of TMS on reading. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to targeted nodes of the brain’s reading network has been shown to impact reading. Extracted data included (a) study characteristics, (b) methodology, (c) targeted nodes, (d) control paradigm, (e) type of reading task, (f) adverse effects, and (g) main findings. Data was classified by type of reading task: 1) phonological processing, 2) semantic judgment, 3) lexical decision, 4) whole word reading, and 5) visual or text characteristics. Seventy records from 46 studies (n = 844) were identified. Results indicate that TMS modulates semantic judgments when focused in the anterior aspects of the reading circuit, phonological processes after stimulation within the dorsal circuit, and impacts single word recognition and contextual reading when administered to the ventral circuit. Findings suggest that changes in specific behavioral aspects of reading following TMS may contribute to identification of foci for use as part of reading interventions.

Reading is a critical skill for one’s quality of life, vocation, and health. A set of core neurocognitive processes must be precisely integrated for the development of proficient reading. Efficient identification of single words requires precise integration of phonological, semantic, and visual information (Cain et al., 2004; Rapp et al., 2007). Phonological processes are processes by which the phonemes of a specific language are associated with particular letters or combinations of letters (or symbols), whereas semantic processes link the visual stimulus of the word with its associated verbal label and meaning. Single word reading also requires various visual attention and processing skills which allow for the detection and discrimination of particular visual shapes in the text that may indicate specific letters, their combinations, or words. Proficient reading also requires efficient text level processes, such as casual inferencing, which are also critical for reading comprehension. Together, these word level processes, integrated with higher-level text comprehension processes, contribute to the development of a coherent representation of the text and result in fluent reading and comprehension (for complete review see, Castles et al., 2018). Given the multiple neurocognitive systems involved in reading, it is not surprising that successful reading requires the recruitment and efficient integration of multiple neural systems.

Proficient reading is typically supported by a well-defined, left-lateralized ‘reading network’ in the brain. This network consists of three distinct, but interconnected, neural circuits that work together to facilitate reading (Fig. 1; Pugh et al., 2000). The dorsal circuit consists of the inferior parietal lobule (IPL), including the angular gyrus and the supramarginal gyrus (SMG), and posterior portions of the superior temporal gyrus (STG; Wernicke’s Area). This circuit supports the sub-lexical phonological decoding processes of single words (Cohen et al., 2000, 2002). The ventral circuit extends from the left occipitotemporal cortex into the inferior and middle temporal gyrus (MTG) and is thought to support recognition of visual text and orthographic patterns that ultimately enable automatic word recognition (Schlaggar & McCandliss, 2007; Shaywitz et al., 2002). The anterior circuit is primarily focused around Broca’s area in the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and is involved in more complex components of reading such as those involved in silent reading or naming and text processing (Pugh et al, 2000; Shankweiler et al., 2008; Vigneau et al., 2006). There is growing evidence that these neural circuits that underlie reading require significant environmental or instructional inputs in order to form this high-order brain-based reading network.

Different nodes within these three circuits of the reading network have been shown to be differentially involved in various component processes of reading depending on the task demands (for a review, see Cattinelli et al. 2013; Martin et al., 2015; Turkeltaub et al., 2002). Children and adults who do not learn to read proficiently, despite quality reading instruction and empirically-based interventions, frequently have specific neurobiological impairments that impact the formation of a well-integrated and efficient reading network. Reading impairments may result from dysfunction in one or more of the neural circuits within the reading network, with theories regarding the cause ranging from problems with network or subcomponent hypoarousal or poor connectivity (Pugh et al., 2008) to the effects of hyperarousal or noise (Hancock et al., 2017).

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that allows for transient stimulation of the brain that produces temporary changes in cortical excitability in a specifically targeted brain region (Hallett, 2007; Rossini et al., 2015). During TMS, a brief magnetic pulse is generated by an electrical current in a coil situated above the subject’s head (Walsh & Rushworth, 1999). This current then induces a magnetic field below the coil that passes into the cortex and induces electrical changes in the targeted cortical region. TMS can have an excitatory or an inhibitory effect, which is capable of facilitating or suppressing behavioral responses, depending on the rate of stimulation. TMS has been used to induce changes in motor responses (Rossini et al., 2015) as well as cognitive task performance (Veniero et al., 2019), and has been successfully applied as a treatment for a number of neurological and psychiatric disorders (George et al., 2013; Morales-Sanchez et al., 2019; Perera et al., 2016). Non-invasive brain stimulation methods, such as TMS, have the potential to create temporary, systematic changes in core brain functions within the reading network circuits in order to further investigate the underlying neural correlates of reading and reading impairment.

In general, TMS methods are typically classified into two distinct paradigms: “online” interference approaches and “offline” modulatory approaches. “Online” approaches involve delivering stimulation to a given brain region actively involved in a behavior, in order to measure the immediate consequences of this stimulation on the ongoing behavior. Online approaches can include single-pulse TMS protocols (spTMS), paired-pulse TMS protocols (ppTMS), or repetitive TMS (rTMS) protocols. In spTMS, a single magnetic pulse is applied to the targeted brain region, which elicits a transient effect in underlying cortical tissue. Similarly, in ppTMS, two single pulses are delivered sequentially. ppTMS is similar to spTMS in that the delivery of two pulses sequentially at a set interval leads to immediately observable changes in the ongoing behavior. Changes in motor task performance can be induced via varying paired-pulse sequences (Derosiere et al., 2020; Lefaucheur, 2019), as can changes in cognitive task performance (Mimura et al., 2021). In most cases, a short interval between pulses (i.e., a few milliseconds) produces an inhibitory effect on cognitive or motor task performance, whereas a longer inter-stimulus interval has a facilitatory effect (Valls-Solé et al., 1992). Both spTMS and ppTMS to various brain regions have been shown to transiently alter behavioral and cognitive response times on a variety of tasks (Veniero et al., 2019). However, spTMS and ppTMS have brief and transient effects on cortical excitability, and therefore produce no lasting effects on task performance. Online rTMS consists of the application of brief trains of patterned pulses to a given brain region during ongoing task performance. The rTMS briefly disrupts neural activity in the given brain region, relative to the duration of stimulation, and transiently changes task performance.

In contrast, offline TMS paradigms typically consist of longer trains of rTMS applied prior to behavioral task execution, in order to discern the effects of changes in cortical excitability on task performance. Effects of offline rTMS on task performance vary depending on the stimulation protocol used (i.e., frequency and duration). Low-frequency rTMS (0.2 Hz to1 Hz) frequently produces an inhibitory effect, suppressing behavioral response, while high-frequency rTMS (≥ 5 Hz) is typically excitatory, producing a faciliatory effect of behavioral output. Various rTMS paradigms have been shown to result in effects on cortical activity that last beyond the stimulation period. In some cases, the effects of a single session of rTMS (either facilitation or inhibition of the behavior) may last up to 60 min (Klomjai et al., 2015; Hallet et al., 2020). Repeated sessions of rTMS can lead to lasting changes in cognitive and affective processing, and has therefore been successfully applied as a treatment for psychiatric symptoms (for a review see Rossini, 2015; Hallet, 2020). This is hypothesized to occur through mechanisms resembling long-term potentiation or depression (Huang et al., 2005; Pell et al., 2011; Tokay et al., 2009) in the underlying cortical tissue.

A specific rTMS paradigm known as theta-burst stimulation (TBS) consists of patterned bursts of 3 pulses at 50 Hz given at 5 Hz (Hallett, 2020; Huang et al., 2005). Like traditional rTMS, TBS produces more prolonged effects on cortical excitability which can lead to changes in behavior that are believed to be driven by changes in long-term potentiation or depression, depending on the stimulation protocol administered. Delivery of TBS continuously for a length of 40 s is known as continuous TBS (cTBS), and typically produces inhibitory effects linked to long-term depression. Delivery of TBS at intermittent intervals (typically every 10 s) over the course of 190 s is known as intermittent TBS (iTBS), and typically produces excitatory or facilitatory effects on behavior thought to be linked to long-term potentiation (for a review, see Hallet et al., 2020). TBS has been shown to produce changes in behavior, with extended effects, comparable to those of rTMS following much shorter durations of stimulation (Lefaucheur, 2019).

TMS and Reading

Recent evidence suggests that TMS administered to different targeted brain regions (i.e., nodes) within the reading network may have specific, yet differential impacts on different aspects of reading behavior. The component reading skills impacted may depend on the specific node of the reading network that is being targeted for modulation. For example, stimulation of a node of the dorsal circuit has been shown to selectively facilitate phonological processing (Stoeckel et al., 2009) while stimulation of the ventral circuit is shown to impact processing of semantic information (Hoffman et al., 2012). However, to date, no comprehensive review of this literature has been conducted, making it difficult to draw conclusions regarding the potential effects of TMS to different regions on different reading related skills and tasks. In order to integrate and evaluate existing findings, we performed a systematic review of the current evidence for the effects of TMS on reading and its component skills.

Method

Literature Review

This review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis guidelines and was prospectively registered with PROSPERO CRD42018112413. We systematically searched the electronic databases PubMed, PsychINFO, and Web of Science for studies published between 1990 and September 2018. Combinations of the following keywords were entered: neurostimulation, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), reading, dyslexia, alexia, and word recognition. References from included studies rendered during the first search were hand searched to identify additional publications that met inclusion criteria.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Several criteria had to be met for a study to be included in this synthesis. During initial review, publications were excluded if they were: (a) not empirical, (b) did not include human subjects, (c) did not report TMS administration to a cortical region, (d) did not include a behavioral measure of reading or component reading process (i.e., phonological processing), or (e) published in a language other than English.

Included articles were each independently reviewed by two members of the research team to determine if they met inclusionary criteria. Inclusion required studies to report: (a) measures of reading skills assessed in subjects’ native or primary language, (b) at least one measure of pre/post TMS reading performance, (c) group data or comparisons, and (d) unique data not previously reported in an already included sample. Publications under dispute were resolved through discussion between reviewers with a third reviewer consulted when necessary. This process yielded 46 independent studies for inclusion in the final synthesis.

Data Collection

Data was extracted by one of the primary reviewers and was crossed checked by the other. A data extraction spreadsheet was used to collect data from each study regarding: (a) characteristics of the study population, (b) stimulation methodology, (c) brain regions targeted, (d) control paradigm used (i.e., active control site or sham stimulation), (e) type of reading task used, (f) reported adverse effects, and (g) main findings. Main findings were coded for change in response time (RT) or accuracy when reported, with most studies reporting one or the other.

Quality Assessment

The quality of included studies was assessed using the nine items from the critical assessment tool for systematic review as outlined by Hawker et al. (2002). For each of the included studies, a quality assessment score was calculated by rating each of the nine items from 1 (very poor) to 4 (good). Each study was independently reviewed by two members of the research team with scores compared between the two raters. Studies under dispute were resolved through discussion between reviewers. The summed score provided an index of empirical rigor for each study (9 very poor; 36 good), with high quality studies receiving a score of 32 or greater (Hawker et al, 2002).

Reading Task Classification

Studies were classified into one of five groups based on the primary component of the reading process being assessed in conjunction with TMS stimulation: 1) phonological processing, 2) semantic judgment, 3) lexical decision, 4) whole word or text reading, or 5) visual or text characteristics. Phonological processing tasks were characterized as primarily focusing on the phonological elements or sounds of a word. This included tasks where the participant had to identify if two words sounded the same, decide if two words rhyme, pronounce pseudowords, or segment words into sound or syllable components. Semantic judgment tasks consisted of those which focused on the meaning of a word, such as deciding if a given word represented one of two semantic categories (i.e., tool versus animal) or if two words had similar meaning. Lexical decision tasks consisted of those requiring participants to identify whether a presented stimulus was a valid real word or a pseudoword. Whole word or text reading tasks were those that required reading in a manner that required explicit identification or comprehension of the text. These studies required participants to read words, phrases, or sentences and respond in a way indicative of comprehension of the stimuli. Tasks that focused on the visual or spatial aspects of text such as font style, letter case, or letter pattern recognition were identified as visual or text characteristics tasks. Some studies included more than one task and therefore were represented by records in multiple task groups.

Results

Study Selection

The database search provided 614 studies to be screened for inclusion. Based on a review of titles and abstracts, 538 studies were excluded. Screening of citations and references provided an additional 17 studies for review. In total, 93 articles were identified and further reviewed for inclusion. Full text review of these publications identified 46 studies that met full inclusion criteria with a total of 72 records included in this qualitative synthesis (Fig. 2).

Quality Assessment

Included studies were, on average, of high quality (mean = 34.32). Only two included studies (Hsiao et al., 2006; Laycock et al., 2009) were rated as fair with total summed scores of 30.

Study Characteristics (Table 1)

Sample Characteristics

A total of 844 participants (male = 394, age range = 15–51) were involved in the included records. Only one record (Costanzo et al., 2013) included a sample of participants with diagnoses of DD. One record (Woollams et al., 2017) included a sample defined as low versus high semantic reliance readers. Two records (Cattaneo et al., 2009; Skarratt & Lavidor, 2006) did not report reader status of their sample. All others consisted of neurologically healthy samples with typical reading skills.

Reported Adverse Effects

Overall, TMS was well tolerated with most studies reporting no adverse effects. Four records did report discomfort or mild adverse effects of TMS. One study reported the withdrawal of three participants (out of total n = 23) after introduction to stimulation (Acheseon & Haggort, 2013). One participant (out of n = 12) was excluded due to hand twitching that interfered with task performance (Sliwinska et al., 2015). Two records (Laycock et al., 2009; Whitney et al., 2011) reported participant discomfort at a priori study designated stimulation levels (n = 9 out of 28), with the protocol continued with stimulator output percentage reduced to individual subject comfort level. Finally, a small facial twitch due to stimulation of the temporalis muscle was described by some participants as “unusual but not uncomfortable” (Sliwinska et al., 2012).

TMS Methodology

Records were reviewed to identify three primary TMS stimulation paradigms (Table 2). Ten records utilized an online spTMS paradigm, seven records implemented an online ppTMS paradigm with inter-stimulus interval varying by study, and 38 records utilized either an offline (n = 20) or online (n = 18) rTMS paradigms. The 20 offline rTMS protocol records consisted of rTMS paired with pre- and post-stimulation behavioral assessments. Seven of those records utilized a standard offline “virtual lesion” protocol (Pascual-Leone et al., 1998) consisting of rTMS delivered at 1 Hz for 10 min (600 pulses). Nine additional records used study specific variants of the virtual lesion protocol. The final four records used the rTMS continuous theta burst protocol as outlined by Huang et al. (2005). The eighteen online rTMS protocol records utilized stimulation which was delivered in coordination with reading stimuli onset, with a 10 Hz protocol the most commonly used (12 records) with stimulation durations ranging from 300 to 1500 ms.

Intensity of stimulation also varied considerably by study. The majority of records used individually defined intensity based on a percentage (80 – 150%) of individualized resting motor threshold (MT) which was defined as the lowest intensity necessary to produce a visible muscle twitch or contraction in the relaxed right hand (i.e., intercostal muscle) 50% of the times when stimulated with a single pulse to the left motor cortex. Four records used an MT variant in which the hand muscle is active or contracted. Active MT typically requires less intensity to elicit a motor response and is thus commonly used in conjunction with more intense stimulation protocols, or when stimulating a region likely to induce facial nerve stimulation. Tomasino et al. (2008) used a “sub-threshold” MT by obtaining a resting MT and then lowering intensity to just below subjects’ sensory threshold. In some cases (e.g., Wheat et al., 2013), an a priori study MT percentage was established but was then adjusted to provide the maximum stimulation intensity while maintaining participant comfort. Hsiao et al. (2006) used a phosphene-based threshold in which intensity was based on the minimum amount of stimulation required to reliably elicit a phosphene while stimulating the visual cortex. The remaining sixteen records defined stimulation intensity based on maximum stimulator output (40 to 70%).

All studies used coil types deemed appropriate for stimulation paradigm and targeted brain regions. A figure-of-eight coil was most commonly used, with diameters ranging from 40 – 90 mm. One study (Ziegler et al., 2018) used a MCF-B65 figure-of-eight coil to stimulate the anterior insula. Although a relatively deep cortical structure, stimulation parameters used in this study were believed to be sufficient for targeting this brain region with this specific coil based on the previous literature (Duncan et al., 2010). Two studies (Braet & Humphreys, 2006a, 2006b) utilized a 70 mm double circular coil for stimulation of parietal or occipital regions. Two additional studies (Kohler et al., 2004; Laycock et al., 2009) stimulated larger cortical regions using a 90 mm round coil.

Control Paradigm

A total of 44 records compared results of TMS to areas of interest relevant to reading against one or more control conditions or paradigms designed to account for the sensory or placebo effects associated with TMS (see Table 2). Twenty-three records used active stimulation to a control brain region not believed to be involved in reading, with the vertex being used most frequently (n = 11 records). Sixteen records used a sham stimulation protocol to either the primary region of interest or a control region. Sham stimulation is used to mimic the auditory or sensory effects of active TMS without actual stimulation of the brain (Duecker & Sack, 2015). While most utilized a purpose-built sham coil that is designed to attenuate the magnetic field, five records instituted a sham protocol which consisted of an active coil that was positioned in such a way that the magnetic field was not directed towards the brain (i.e., coil at a 90-degree angle with scalp or foam pad placed between coil and head). The remaining 12 records did not include a control or sham stimulation paradigm. However, in most cases active TMS was delivered in conjunction with a behavioral control task which did not involve reading processes (i.e., picture naming) and was not believed to be directly associated with the targeted brain region.

Task-based Synthesis

Phonological Processing (see Appendix 1 for List of Studies)

Dorsal Circuit

Thirteen records reported phonological processing task performance after TMS, with the primary targeted areas falling within temporoparietal regions associated with the dorsal circuit or its right hemisphere homolog. SMG was the most commonly targeted node within the dorsal circuit, with results varying depending on the specific targeted area within this region. Different individually targeted subregions within the anterior SMG (varying by 1–2 cm) either slowed reaction time (RT) by 6% or speeded it by roughly 4% following ppTMS, depending on which of three subregions were targeted (Sliwinska et al., 2012). Similarly, slowing of RT occurred after high frequency rTMS (10 Hz) to the left or right SMG compared to baseline or control trials (Gough et al., 2005; Pattamadilok et al., 2010, 2015; Sliwinska et al., 2012, 2015), while one record (Stoeckel et al., 2009) reported that spTMS to left SMG speeded RT by 16 ms. Sliwinska et al. (2012, 2015) also found that following high frequency rTMS (10 Hz) to different subregions within the left SMG, RT was slowed, while stimulation to the neighboring angular gyrus speeded RT (Sliwinska et al., 2015). Costanzo et al., (2012, 2013) found no effects on pseudoword reading after 5 Hz rTMS to left or right STG (EEG 10–20 P5/P6), however stimulation of right or left IPL (P3/P4) resulted in fewer errors in both typical and dyslexic readers.

Ventral and Anterior Circuits

Four additional records assessed phonological processing after TMS to brain regions outside of temporoparietal regions (dorsal circuit). Similar to effects observed after stimulation of the dorsal circuit, TMS to nodes of the ventral and anterior circuits had differential effects depending on region targeted. With regard to the ventral circuit, slower RTs for words but not pseudowords were observed after ppTMS to the posterior middle temporal gyrus (pMTG), however, ppTMS of the ventral occipitotemporal cortex (bilaterally) significantly slowed reading of both pseudowords and irregular real words (Pattamadilok et al., 2015). In the anterior circuit, one record (Gough et al., 2005) targeted functionally defined posterior portions of the IFG and found no significant impact of online, high frequency rTMS on task accuracy but there was a significant slowing of RT.

Non-network Regions

One record targeted a brain region not directly associated with the reading network (e.g., M1). Papeo et al. (2009) found that spTMS to M1 produced a significant slowing of phonological processing but had no effect on accuracy during syllabic segmentation of action-verbs.

Semantic Judgment (see Appendix 2 for List of Studies)

Dorsal Circuit

Eight records reported the effect of stimulation to nodes within the dorsal circuit on semantic processing. Inhibitory paradigms, such as spTMS or ppTMS, slowed RT during a semantic processing task, as might be expected (Cattaneo et al., 2010; Jackson et al., 2015; Jackson et al., 2015; Pattamadilok et al., 2015; Sliwinska et al., 2012). Low frequency offline rTMS (1 Hz) to the left and right temporal pole (10 mm posterior along the MTG) slowed RT during synonym judgement (Pobric et al., 2009), while 0.9 Hz rTMS had no impact on RT or accuracy of semantic character judgement after stimulation of the left or right inferior temporal cortex, 1 cm rostral to the preoc-cipital incisura (Ueki et al., 2006). Inhibitory ppTMS to left SMG resulted in a net slowdown on a synonym judgment task, with no significant effect on task accuracy (Sliwinska et al., 2012). Following excitatory 10 Hz rTMS to the left SMG produced an anticipated faciliatory effect and speeded RT (Sliwinska et al., 2015; Stoeckel et al., 2009).

Conversely, both ppTMS and online high frequency rTMS to the left angular gyrus slowed RT by roughly 47 ms relative to control trials (Sliwinska et al., 2012). Single pulse and online 10 Hz rTMS administered to the left anterior intraparietal sulcus (on or near apex of pars triangularis) was shown to slow RT compared to no-TMS baseline (Devlin et al., 2003). Offline low frequency rTMS to the left posterior intraparietal sulcus (MNI −23, −73, 48) also slowed RT on a task requiring selection of a word based on semantic features (Whitney et al., 2012).

Ventral Circuit

Stimulation of targets located roughly within the bounds of the ventral circuit (or right hemisphere homologs) also impacted semantic processing, depending on site targeted and stimulation paradigm. Seven records (Hoffman et al., 2012; Jackson et al., 2015; Jackson et al., 2015; Pattamadilok et al., 2015; Pobric et al., 2009; Whitney et al., 2011, 2012) reported faster RT during a semantic task after TMS to left or right pMTG, temporal poles, or anterior temporal lobe. While low frequency rTMS to left pMTG slowed RT, it had no impact on task accuracy (Hoffman et al., 2012; Whitney et al., 2011, 2012). Alternatively, offline low frequency rTMS (0.9 Hz) to either left or right posterior inferior temporal cortex produced no significant speed or accuracy difference in pre or post stimulation performance on a Japanese character semantic judgment task (Ueki et al., 2006).

Anterior Circuit

Nodes within the anterior circuit of the reading network, including left IFG, were the most common stimulation targets. Nine records observed inhibitory effects of rTMS (i.e., slower RT) after stimulation of nodes within the anterior circuit of the reading network. Online high frequency rTMS to the left IFG was also shown to slow RT on semantic judgement tasks (Chouinard et al., 2009; Gough et al., 2005). Offline rTMS had a similar slowing effect on a low relatedness synonym judgement task following low frequency (1 Hz) stimulation to a target within the left IFG corresponding to the pars triangularis (Whitney et al., 2011, 2012) Of note, Gough et al. (2005) found that this was the only case when stimulation was delivered to anterior, but not posterior, portions of the left IFG.

Similarly, online high frequency rTMS to anterior portions of the slowed RT during a semantic categorization task by an average of 41 ms (Gough et al., 2005). Stimulation (7 Hz rTMS) to the left inferior prefrontal cortex IFG (defined by individual functional activation) was also shown to slow RT (Köhler et al., 2004). One record (Hoffman et al., 2010) reported slowed RT on a similar task requiring semantic judgment of abstract versus concrete words after low frequency rTMS (1 Hz) to left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (MNI −54, 24, 3). spTMS delivered to left IFG (mean Talairach −45, 21, 17) had no significant effect on performance (RT or accuracy) during an Italian semantic categorization task (Cattaneo et al., 2009).

Peri-network Regions

Two records showed an inhibitory impact on reading after TMS to brain regions outside of the primary reading circuits. One record (Hsiao et al., 2006) reported that online high frequency rTMS to left occipital cortex significantly slowed RT, compared to no-TMS, sham, or right hemisphere stimulation. Papeo et al. (2009) reported a slowed RT to non-action verbs after spTMS to M1 when categorizing words as action or non-action.

Lexical Decision (see Appendix 3 for List of Studies)

Dorsal Circuit

Fifteen records utilized lexical decision tasks for assessment of real word versus pseudoword reading. One record reported effects of online, high frequency rTMS on lexical decision making (20 Hz) after stimulation of a target within the dorsal circuit. 20 Hz stimulation to the left temporoparietal junction, or its right hemisphere homolog, slowed RT to words with emotional content (Rochas et al., 2014).

Ventral Circuit

Six records involved lexical decision-making during or after stimulation of regions associated with the ventral circuit. Online, high frequency (10 Hz) stimulation of the left (or right) vOTC was shown to slow RT to real word stimuli (Duncan et al., 2010; Pattamadilok et al., 2010, 2015). A significantly slower RT and increased error rates were also found during 8 Hz stimulation to vOTC bilaterally (Lavidor & Walsh, 2003; Lavidor et al., 2003). Two of these records (Lavidor et al., 2003; Pattamadilok et al., 2015) found that online rTMS to vOTC also increased RT to pseudowords as well as to real words.

One record (Zhu et al., 2015) found that cTBS to left pMTG differentially affected word recognition speed depending on time of stimulation delivery relative to stimulation onset, with shortened RT after stimulation delivered 600 ms post stimuli onset, but were slower after stimulation delivered 150 ms post stimuli onset. Another record reported slowed RT to words with disgust-related content following low frequency rTMS to the left anterior insula (Ziegler et al., 2018).

Anterior Circuit

One record reported effects of stimulation to an node within the anterior circuit of the reading network on lexical decision making. Zhu et al. (2015) found that cTBS to the anterior – ventral portion of the left IFG (Brodmann 47) slowed word recognition speed.

Peri-network Regions

Online, high frequency rTMS to the left occipital region slowed the time it took to identify words, depending on word length, but had no effect on the speed at which pseudowords were identified (Skarratt & Lavidor, 2006). Stimulation of motor regions of the brain not primarily considered to be part of the traditional reading network (i.e., M1 and premotor cortex) also slowed lexical decision-making processes. RT to pseudowords, but not real words, was slowed following ppTMS to the premotor region involved in writing (Pattamadilok et al., 2015). Lexical decision responses to hand-related action verbs were faster following cTBS to left versus right premotor cortex (Willems et al., 2011). Similarly, spTMS to leg or hand areas (M1) slowed lexical decision RT to action verbs corresponding to leg or hand actions respectively (Pulvermüller et al., 2005).

Whole Word or Text Reading (see Appendix 4 for List of Studies)

Dorsal Circuit

Fourteen records required participants to read whole words in a manner that demonstrated recognition or meaning of the stimuli, with nodes within the dorsal circuit being the primary target. Stimulation of nodes within the dorsal circuit, or its right hemisphere homolog, slowed reading of whole words (Costanzo et al., 2012, 2013; Nakamura et al., 2006; Wheat et al., 2013). More errors in text reading were reported following high frequency (5 Hz) stimulation of the right hemisphere STG (EEG 10–20 P6) in typically reading adults (Costanzo et al., 2012). However, the same rTMS paradigm to the same region resulted in fewer errors in adults with reading impairment (Costanzo et al., 2013). Online high frequency rTMS to left posterior parietal cortex (7 cm posterior, 3 cm lateral to the vertex) slowed reading speed for word arrays (Leff et al., 2001). Cazzoli et al. (2014) found no impact on reading speed following cTBS to the right hemisphere homolog (EEG 10–20 P4).

Ventral Circuit

Three records reported the effects of ventral circuit stimulation on whole word reading. Word reading errors were increased in high semantic reliance readers following 1 Hz rTMS to the left anterior MTG (10 mm posterior from tip of temporal lobe), but only for those words with atypical spelling (Woollams et al., 2017). Offline low frequency (0.9 Hz) rTMS to the left or right posterior inferior temporal cortex did not impact RT during reading aloud of Kanji characters in native Japanese speakers (Ueki et al., 2006) and cTBS to the posterior left MTG (MNI −52, −50, −8)had no effect on reading of ambiguous sentences.

Anterior Circuit

Stimulation of nodes within the anterior circuit slowed reading of regular words. Five records showed slower whole word reading after stimulation to posterior regions of the left IFG. A single pulse to posterior left IFG (pars opercularis) delivered at 225 – 3000 ms post stimulation onset slowed RT during word reading (Wheat et al., 2013). High frequency (10 Hz) online stimulation to a similar target region slowed processing of both regular and irregular words (Carreiras et al., 2012; Chouinard et al., 2009). Continuous TBS to a smiliar posterior region of the left IFG also slowed reading of ambiguous sentences (Acheseon & Haggort, 2013). Peri-network Regions

Two records examined the contribution of motor regions to whole word reading. Faster RTs to action words during motor imagery were observed following spTMS to M1 (Tomasino et al., 2008), while online high frequency rTMS to right frontal eye fields slowed the time it took for the first saccadic eye movement to occur (Leff et al., 2001).

Dorsal Circuit

Seven records involved tasks that assessed visual or text aspects of reading with two of these records targeting nodes within the dorsal circuit. Online high frequency (20 Hz) rTMS to the right parietal cortex (EEG 10–20 P4) increased errors for mixed-case (i.e., mIxEd) relative to single-case as compared to V1 or sham stimulation (Braet & Humphreys, 2006b). RT to mixed cased stimuli was slowed following stimulation of the right parietal cortex compared to a control site (Braet & Humphreys, 2006a). RT to mixed case words, when words were presented with low visual contrast, was also slowed during 20 Hz rTMS to the right parietal cortex (Braet & Humphreys, 2006b).

Ventral Circuit

One record reported effects of stimulation to a node within the ventral circuit on processing of visual or text characteristics. Single pulse stimulation to the left occipitotemporal cortex (~ 25 mm posterior to the lateral edge of transverse temporal gyrus) was shown to slow RT to mirror-image recognition of letter strings, relative to right hemisphere or vertex stimulation (Nakamura et al., 2014).

Anterior Circuit

No records reported effects of anterior circuit stimulation on processing of visual or text characteristics.

Peri-network Regions

Stimulation to nodes within the visual cortex was shown to negatively impact processing of visual or text characteristics. Repetitive stimulation of V1 (online 20 Hz) slowed RT on the reading of low-contrast single-case words relative to high-contrast (Braet & Humphreys, 2006b). Paired pulse stimulation to V1/V2 and left V5/MT + reduced accuracy during a forced-choice match to target task (Laycock et al., 2009). Additionally, ppTMS to left MT + reduced accuracy during motion trials with presentation of stimuli manipulated by line contour, luminance, and motion (Rauschecker et al., 2011).

Discussion

This systematic review examined effects of TMS on the component processes involved in reading, with the dorsal (25 records) and ventral circuits (18 records) of the reading network being the most frequently targeted regions. We found that TMS impacted performances on several types of reading and reading-related tasks, including those involving phonological decoding, semantic and lexical decision making, and whole word reading. Overall, findings from this review suggest that neuromodulation of various nodes of the neural reading network differentially modulates the speed and accuracy of reading-related skills.

As expected, stimulation to different nodes of the reading network resulted in modulation of different reading-related skills. Phonological processing was most impacted after stimulation to nodes within the dorsal component of the reading circuit, but the valence of the effect was heavily dependent on the specific brain region targeted (i.e., faciliatory after stimulation to angular gyrus versus inhibitory after stimulation to SMG). Changes in speed on tasks related to semantic aspects of reading were most reliably seen after stimulation of the anterior circuit. For word recognition and context reading, changes in speed were most commonly found after perturbation of the ventral circuit. Visual processes related to reading of text were slowed by inhibitory stimulation to the reading network, as well as to regions of the visual cortex. While most records utilized inhibitory stimulation paradigms that reliably slowed reading and reading-related processes, those that used excitatory stimulation typically saw expected improvement (i.e., faster RT) in component reading skills, depending on brain region targeted. As anticipated, facilitatory stimulation of the dorsal and ventral circuits, particularly the left SMG, angular gyrus, and STG, was shown to produce improved performance (i.e., reduced RT) during reading tasks that include various types of word or letter stimuli, consistent with the role of these regions in phonological decoding and related reading processes.

These results largely support the differential contributions of the distinct, yet interconnected, circuits of the reading network. Moreover, differential effects could be found at different locations within the processing stream within the same circuit. For instance, numerous studies showed a slowing of phonological processing following high frequency stimulation to the left SMG while one study (Sliwinska et al., 2015) showed that stimulation of the angular gyrus speeded RT on a phonological decoding task. This may suggest that faciliatory stimulation enhances integration of visual and phonological information when the targeted brain region is associated with earlier processes which then have positive downstream effects on phonologically oriented reading tasks. Conversely, increasing cortical excitability in a more downstream brain region within the same circuit may negatively impact processing in an early stage and in turn, delay completion of the task.

Potential mechanisms by which these differential changes may occur could relate to the inhibition of hyperarousal in the network or excitation of hypoaroused nodes within the network. When considering functional differences in these regions between typical and impaired readers, one of the most relevant findings concerns differences in neural noise, or variability of cortical excitability, in regional and network-level activity, between these groups. This “neural noise” hypothesis suggests that hypoarousal or hyperarousal in, or between, certain nodes of the reading network may lead to reading difficulties (Hancock et al., 2017; Pugh et al., 2008). It is possible that deficits observed in impaired readers could be the result of undesirable levels of excitably in critical brain regions involved in efficient reading, with neu-romodulatory results such as those observed in Sliwinska et al. (2015) supporting such conceptualization. Additionally, while inhibitory TMS paradigms may impede reading performance in typical readers, these effects may have the potential to improve reading in atypical readers by suppressing excess noise in these networks.

It is important to note that only two of the reviewed studies included adults with confirmed reading impairments. In most studies, authors reported suppressive effects of inhibitory TMS on RT occurring in typical readers, but the such behavioral effects to any given region may differ in impaired readers. For example, in Costanzo et al. (2012), reduced performance was found after high frequency rTMS to the right STG in typical readers, whereas improved performance (speeded RT) was found after the same stimulation paradigm to this same site in impaired readers (Costanzo et al., 2013). A large body of neuroimaging evidence has also indicated differences in functional activation and connectivity of the reading network in typical readers compared to those with reading impairments (for a review, see Pugh et al., 2008; Richlan et al. (2012). If this is the case, then faciliatory high-frequency rTMS applied to a cortical region with appropriate levels of arousal in a typical reader may suppress reading performance. However, if this same stimulation protocol is applied to a cortical region that is hypoaroused in an impaired reader, this may induce a more optimal level of excitably in the network which facilitates reading performances. This hypothesis may be further considered by the impact of right hemisphere homolog stimulation in typical readers (i.e., Cazzoli et al., 2014; Costanzo et al., 2012; Rochas et al., 2014), and the functional imaging studies that suggest an overactive right hemisphere often seen in those with reading impairment (Pugh et al., 2000). It is debated as to whether the right hemisphere is compensatory or involved in the underlying deficit. In order to further explore this question, future studies are needed comparing the effects of TMS to a variety of nodes of the reading network and their right hemisphere homologs in both typical and impaired readers, comparing the impact of stimulation across nodes within an individual reader’s network.

One important thing to consider in the interpretation of the reviewed literature is that most studies presented either RT or accuracy data. While several reported changes in RT following stimulation, only two (Costanzo et al., 2012, 2013) reported changes in accuracy. It is in some ways not surprising that many of these studies did not show changes in accuracy, as these studies largely consisted of adults with typically developed reading skills, performing tasks with high accuracy percentages (~ 80–90%). The studies that did show changes in accuracy (Costanzo et al., 2012, 2013) were among the few to make use of standardized reading assessments that do not show high accuracy rates or ceiling effects in typical adults. Further research with more complex reading-related tasks is necessary in order to determine whether TMS to reading network regions is capable of impacting accuracy.

While the clinical significance of changes in word reading accuracy following TMS are clear, the significance of changes in RT (especially in cases where TMS resulted in slower RT) is not as intuitive. Although slowed RT on reading-related tasks may not be an ideal intervention outcome, slowed RT following TMS supports the ability of TMS to modulate activity in the reading network and create “virtual lesions” that allow researchers to infer the roles of different brain regions in reading behavior. Such research in readers of differing skill levels could help to elucidate ways in which such activity, and the impact of TMS, differs in those with reading disabilities.

An interesting finding was that there were studies reviewed that showed an effect following TMS to brain regions not considered part of the classic reading network. For example, spTMS to M1 decreased accuracy to action-related verbs (Papeo et al., 2009), and spTMS to M1 modulated RT to reading related tasks using action-verbs in multiple studies (Pulvermuller et al., 2005; Tomasino et al., 2008; Willems et al., 2011). In general, improvement on reading-related tasks following stimulation to M1 was only shown on tasks in which motor regions such as M1 are involved in the reading related task, such as those involving thinking about motor task related words (i.e., thinking about the words ‘tossing a ball’). Similarly, rTMS to the insula modulated RT during a reading task involving reading words eliciting disgust, which is considered to be processed by the insula (Ziegler et al., 2018). In general, TMS to regions outside of the classic reading network modulated performance for reading related tasks which also had particular characteristics that involved behaviors processed by these peripheral regions, such as perceiving action words (M1) or disgust-related content (insula). Although further investigation is needed, it is informative to gain a better understanding of the impact of peri-network regions on reading behavior. This may provide additional knowledge regarding the impact of connected networks, such as those involved in motor planning, attentional control, or semantic networks, on reading. Although some of these regions might impact more core brain networks (i.e., attention or arousal) that can impact task performances unrelated to reading, they may also serve as the basis for contrasting methodologies. Better theoretical or methodological and neuroanatomical explanations for their involvement are required to increase understanding of these results.

It is also possible that these results may also reflect downstream effects of stimulation, with stimulation to any region involved in a task effectively modulating the connected network(s) involved in the task, including the connected reading-related regions. There is evidence to suggest that the effects of TMS are not limited only to the cortical area of stimulation, and that stimulation to a given region may affect functional connectivity of the network(s) and peripheral activation of secondary regions (Fox et al., 2012). Future studies should consider measuring functional activation and connectivity throughout entire task-related networks prior to and following TMS, in order to further evaluate this hypothesis.

The current literature limits our ability to draw more specific conclusions regarding the use of TMS for modulation of the reading network. First, it is important to note that within the current literature, there were many different targeted brain regions, many of which were grossly localized despite using a coil type that provides for precise targeting of brain region. There were also several studies that identified the same brain region but defined them in different ways. For instance, Costanzo et al. (2012, 2013) identified their targets (IPL and STG) based on EEG 10–20 coordinates while Nakamura et al. (2006) used individualized neuroanatomical landmarks to identified the same targeted brain regions. Both studies reported results that supported the role of the left IPL in phonological processing but reported inconsistent, but not necessarily contradictory, roles of the left STG in whole word reading. More standardized methodology for the specification of stimulation targets could help increase comparability across studies, and interpretability and reproducibility.

A related factor, target node localization protocols, also varied across studies, with 31 studies using neuronavigation, while 12 used task-based localization. Neuronavigation ensures more precise and accurate coil placement during stimulation, while individual task-based functional coordinates provide the most accurate assessment of target node location. Reliability of the stimulation site, and interpretation of results, is open to question without the use of these forms of target localization. Similarly, specificity of stimulation targets could be brought into question in those studies that used round or double circular coils, designed to target larger or deeper regions of cortical tissue (Braet & Humphreys, 2006a, b; Kohler et al., 2004; Laycock et al., 2009). In one case (Ziegler et al., 2018), a figure-of-eight coil was used to stimulate the anterior insula, a relatively deep cortical structure. While previous research has demonstrated success in stimulation of this region using this type of coil, both the location of the structure and the required intensity of stimulation would also result in the stimulation of multiple surrounding structures, limiting specificity of stimulation. These confounds are of particular relevance given evidence that even incremental differences in targeted site locations within the same brain region can have inverse effects on reading behaviors (Sliwinska et al., 2012).

The types of stimulation paradigms used across studies were also highly variable (see Table 2) which resulted in few opportunities to identify consistent patterns of results across studies. Even when using the same type of stimulation, parameters or intensities (i.e., individualized MT versus MSO) varied considerably. Numerous studies utilized a consistent MSO percentage across all subjects. While this maintains a consistent intensity, the effect on the targeted brain region may vary across subjects due to individual cortical excitability not accounted for. This is less of a concern with those studies that used individualized MT. However, those studies that used MT but adjusted intensity based on participant sensory threshold or comfort level (i.e.,Tomasino et al., 2008; Wheat et al., 2013) create concerns about the consistency of stimulation delivered across subjects. These varying methods could impact the amount of delivered energy to the targeted brain region and thus modify results, again negatively impacting comparability and generalizability across studies.

A major limitation of the current literature reflects a short coming of the field as a whole. No records reviewed discussed the impact of pre-TMS or baseline individual differences on the behavioral outcomes following TMS. Factors such as medications being taken or the amount of sleep prior to stimulation have been shown to impact cortical excitability and thus TMS outcomes (Rossi et al., 2021). Individual differences such as age, handedness, or cortical thickness have also been shown to be moderating factors of TMS effects (Boes et al., 2018; Davidson & Tremblay, 2013). Particularly relevant to the current literature review, are individual differences in reading abilities and the integrity of the reading network and how they may interact with the effects of TMS. Future research should provide more details regarding these subject factors and more systematically address the role of these factors in their findings.

As previously mentioned, only two studies (Costanzo et al., 2013; Woollams et al., 2017) included subjects with reading impairments. Underrepresentation of atypical readers in this review limits our understanding of how TMS may modulate reading ability in these individuals with potentially atypical reading networks. Results from these two studies are promising, but replication and comparisons with typical readers are needed in order to draw conclusions about the potential future role of TMS in interventions. It would also be beneficial to further investigate the durability of TMS effects on reading processes beyond the expected stimulation duration, as this would be crucial in understanding possible long-term benefits of stimulation as an adjunct to behavioral reading remediations. Costanzo et al. (2012, 2013) used a ‘return to baseline’ task to assess the immediate durability of TMS effects and found that all participants (both typical and impaired readers) returned to baseline after 100 min post stimulation. No studies did longer-term (i.e., one or more days post-TMS) follow-up of the effects of TMS on the reading network or its underlying processes. The effects of repeated stimulation sessions, similar to current successful treatment protocols utilized for depression or stroke, could also provide critical information about the potential use of TMS as an adjunctive component of state-of-the-art remediations for chronic reading impairments.

Conclusion

In this review, we identified TMS protocols that result in improved or reduced performances in reading and reading-related skills. Our findings provide further confirmation of the key nodes within the primary reading network and generally supported the current neurobiological model for their differential roles in the complex behavior of reading. Stimulation methods and target neuroanatomy varied greatly among reported TMS protocols; therefore, it is difficult to identify any specific TMS protocol that would be most effective for targeting specific reading processes at this time. Further studies evaluating the effectiveness of different applications of TMS in both typical and impaired readers should provide the basis for improved understanding of the neural correlates of reading and reading impairment.
