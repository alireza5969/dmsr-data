Abstract

Linkage to care (LTC) and initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) are key components in the longitudinal care cascade for people living with HIV (PLHIV). Many strategies to optimize these stages of HIV care have been implemented, though there is a paucity of analyses comparing the outcomes of these efforts in low- and middle-income countries. We conducted a systematic review of studies assessing interventions along all stages of the HIV care continuum published between 2008 and 2020. A comprehensive search strategy reviewed five electronic databases to capture studies assessing HIV testing, LTC, ART initiation, ART adherence, and viral suppression. Of the 388 articles that met the inclusion criteria, 78 described interventions for improving LTC/ART initiation. Efforts focused on empowering patients through integrative approaches generally yielded more substantive results compared to provider-initiated non-adaptive LTC interventions or cash incentives. Specifically, tailoring care and incorporating ART initiation into existing infrastructures, such as maternal clinics, had a high impact across settings. Moreover, strategies such as home-based HIV counseling and testing (HBHCT) appear to be most effective when implemented in tandem with other approaches including motivational counseling and point-of-care CD4 testing.

INTRODUCTION:

Testing and diagnosis, linkage to care (LTC), antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation, and maintenance of therapy are key stages of the HIV care cascade, which are critical to addressing the ongoing HIV epidemic (1). However, the high levels of patients lost to follow up at each stage of the HIV care cascade make it difficult to consistently provide quality care in a longitudinal manner across health systems. In particular, significant rates of patient attrition have been reported in cascade stages prior to ART initiation (1, 2). In 2014, UNAIDS launched an ambitious “90–90-90” goal of diagnosing, treating, and virally suppressing 90% of all people living with HIV (PLHIV) globally by 2020, and an equivalent 95–95-95 target for 2030 (3). However, as of 2019, 67% of the nearly 38 million PLHIV globally were receiving antiretroviral therapy (4). While the coverage is higher in eastern and southern Africa with 72% of PLHIV on ART, only 58% of PLHIV in western and central Africa are estimated to be on ART. Because people living in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) across Sub-Saharan Africa account for two-thirds of the global burden of HIV, increasing the proportion of PLHIV who are linked to treatment and initiated on ART in these countries has been shown to be an important step in tackling these HIV epidemics. Specifically, ensuring that people living with HIV have access to treatment improves quality of life and decreases mortality with derivative benefits of reducing onward sexual, parenteral, or vertical HIV transmission (5).

LTC and ART initiation are both central to the effectiveness of an HIV response and are among the most challenging steps in the HIV care continuum (6, 7). These two steps require careful integration of and navigation through intersecting individual, behavioral, and system-level factors, which often vary significantly across time and space (6, 8). Several studies have evaluated the barriers to and facilitators of LTC/ART initiation in LMICs in Sub-Saharan Africa (9). One 2012 systematic review highlighted the provision of referral letters, transportation vouchers, and supportive counseling as facilitators of LTC, while stigma, fear of drug toxicity, transportation costs, distance to facility, long waiting times at clinic, and shortage of healthcare workers were cited as major barriers to linkage to and continuity of care (10).

Strategies to overcome these barriers, as well as interventions and approaches to simplify, streamline, and optimize the LTC and ART initiation processes have been implemented and studied in numerous settings. However, summative analyses of interventions according to impact and prioritization for scale-up across LMICs remains limited, especially in recent years. In response, we undertook this systematic review to comprehensively examine literature presenting LTC and ART initiation strategies conducted in countries across Sub-Saharan Africa, and to highlight interventions reporting particularly promising results.

METHODS

We performed a systematic review of studies examining interventions along the HIV care cascade published between 2008 and 2020. A search strategy was developed including concepts for HIV/AIDS, ART adherence, LTC, healthcare delivery, intervention studies, and Sub-Saharan African countries, designed to capture studies of interventions on any stage of the HIV care continuum. Search strategy concepts were developed in collaboration with a professional librarian (Katie Lobner) and the Johns Hopkins University Ending Aids in West Africa (EAWA) study team (EH, GT, CEL) in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A query of selected search terms was completed in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Wed of Science, and the Cochrane Library. Studies were categorized into one of the stages of the HIV care cascade (prevention, testing/diagnosis, LTC/ART initiation, or adherence) and each stage was treated as an independent, focused systematic review. An overview of all articles included in this review is available in Appendix Table I.

Study Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Our objective was to evaluate studies that reported on results and implementation of interventions to improve patient LTC or initiation of ART in countries across Sub-Saharan Africa. Interventions that were included focused primarily on same-day ART initiation, CD4 and viral load testing at time of diagnosis, and linkage support systems. Only studies published between January 1, 2008 and July 1, 2020 were included. Figure 1 summarizes the PRISMA diagram for systematic review stages, including number of studies excluded and reasons for exclusion. Studies were excluded if any of the following criteria were met: the study did not report results (e.g. study protocols, feasibility studies), PLHIV were not the focus of the study, the study did not occur in Sub-Saharan Africa, the intervention was not on the care cascade, the study did not report primary data collection (e.g. a review paper), or the study was a simulation and was not implemented in the field. Studies that reported descriptions of intervention implementation but no intervention results, or only qualitative results, were also excluded.

Data abstraction and synthesis for emergent themes

Literature search results underwent de-duplication, title and abstract screening, full-text review, and data abstraction by two reviewers, including authors GT and EH. Data abstracted from the studies included: country of study, intervention target population, intervention delivery strategy, type of intervention implemented, and a summary of key findings and conclusions discussed by authors. Authors NO and AB completed further evaluation of articles by synthesizing descriptions of the interventions and conclusions about the outcome(s) of the interventions.

Evaluation of Study Quality

Authors NO and AB independently assessed the quality of each study (Appendix Table 2) using a set of predetermined criteria based on the GRADE framework (11), which has been used in a similar manner by other research groups (12). The criteria of interest focused on assessing each study’s design, sample size/magnitude, and applicability (low, moderate, or high for each domain) as well as an overall quality score using the same metrics.

Conceptual Framework

We categorized interventions into four levels based on a modified socio-ecological model (Figure 2), which focuses on understanding patients and providers as unique individuals who live in unique social and cultural contexts within broader health systems, all of which affect LTC and ART initiation outcomes (13). The four interconnected levels include provider-level interventions at the time of diagnosis, patient-level interventions at the time of diagnosis, motivational/supportive interventions, and health system interventions. Provider-level interventions are those delivered by providers at the time of HIV diagnosis that represent a shift in the standard of care. These interventions include point-of-care CD4 testing (POC CD4 testing), same day/accelerated ART initiation, universal test and treat, and provider-initiated testing and counseling (PITC). Patient-level interventions, which include home-based HIV counselling and testing (HCT) and mobile testing, aim at expanding the reach of HIV testing and counseling services by meeting patients in their homes and communities. Motivational/supportive interventions involve efforts to promote LTC by addressing behavioral barriers to care and/or providing various forms of motivational and social support. Three such interventions were identified: counseling, reminder calls and text messages, and incentives. Interventions in the health system category aim to improve LTC through the reduction of structural barriers to care, as well as the strengthening of health infrastructure and healthcare practices. Such interventions include integration of HIV services into antenatal care as well as other health services, task shifting, use of an electronic medical record system, training traditional healers and educational outreach programs to improve provider practice.

RESULTS

Study Characteristics

From 7,940 unique titles identified, 811 articles described the implementation and evaluation of an HIV care cascade intervention and underwent full text review. Of these, 388 met the inclusion criteria, and where multiple articles reported on the same study, only study articles including intervention results were included in the final synthesis, resulting in 78 articles that focus on the LTC and ART initiation phase of the HIV care cascade in Sub-Saharan Africa. These articles include the following 13 countries: Eswatini (formerly Swaziland), Kenya, Liberia, Malawi, Mozambique, Nigeria, Rwanda, Sierra Leone, South Africa, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia and Zimbabwe. Of the 78 studies, studies in South Africa are featured most prominently, recurring as the setting in 34 articles, followed by Uganda (14), Kenya (9) and Mozambique (6). Study design types included randomized controlled trials, retrospective cohort studies, and prospective cohort studies, among others. A total of 45 studies included single component interventions, while 33 studies included multiple component interventions. Adult populations were examined in 40 studies, while 20 other studies included both adult and pediatric/adolescent populations. Among the remaining studies, two focused on children, nine focused on pregnant women or mother/infant pairs, three focused on female sex workers and one study each focused exclusively on the following populations: women experiencing intimate partner violence (IPV); adult males; youth; and people who use drugs. The intervention population reported in studies below are adults living with HIV in the overall population, unless otherwise stated.

Provider-level interventions at the time of diagnosis

Point-of-care CD4 testing

Offering CD4 testing at the time of HIV diagnosis was the primary intervention or a component of multiple interventions in 17 studies. Of these, 13 observed an increase in participants in the sample reporting LTC and/or ART initiation, and in one, study patients described POC CD4 testing as the most useful intervention in facilitating LTC (14). Among the most impactful of these studies, characterized by the highest proportion of sample participants initiating ART, were two which focused on preventing mother to child transmission (described in more detail in the sections on Same-day/accelerated ART initiation and Integrating services into antenatal care respectively), (15, 16) as well as the study which focused on infants (17). A cluster randomized trial in Mozambique which evaluated the effect of POC early infant HIV testing on ART initiation rates found that the 89.7% of infants in the POC arm initiated ART within 60 days of sample collection compared to 12.8% of infants in the standard of care arm (aRR 7.34; p < 0.001). Three studies showed no significant effect of POC CD4 testing alone on LTC or ART initiation (18–20). Larson et al found that CD4 testing using a point-of-care, but not rapid, technology and without other health system changes had minor impacts on the uptake of HIV care and treatment (20).

Universal test and treat

Following the WHO’s 2015 policy which removed all limitations on ART eligibility requirements, universal test and treat programs were implemented in 11 studies, with mixed results. Universal test and treat had the greatest impact in the TAPS study in South Africa in which 94% (n = 139/148) of eligible female sex workers (FSW) in the study initiated ART (21). In a longitudinal study of the implementation of the package of CDC- and WHO-recommended linkage services in Tanzania, enrolment in HIV care and ART initiation among all children and adults diagnosed with HIV were highest in the years following the enactment of the test and treat policy (22). There were two cluster randomized trials with relatively small sample sizes (n = 514 and n = 278) that reported increased ART initiation and LTC among patients in the intervention arm who were offered immediate ART (23, 24). A larger cluster randomized trial (n = 12,894) found no significant difference between ART initiation within 3 months using the universal test and treat model compared to using the guidelines which recommend a CD4 count < 350 cells/mm3 (89% vs. 92.8%) (25). A nested case-control study in Zambia and South Africa highlighted stigma- and health system-related barriers to timely linkage-to-ART within a universal test and treat program including “not feeling ready” (aOR 2.75; 95% CI: 1.89 to 4.01; p < 0.001), preferring to wait until sick (aOR 2.00; 95% CI: 1.27 to 3.14; p = 0.02), finding clinics overcrowded (aOR 1.51; 95% CI: 1.08 to 2.12; p = 0.006), finding opening hours inconvenient (aOR: 1.63; 95% CI: 1.06 to 2.51; p = 0.02) and expressing strong feelings of shame about having HIV (ptrend = 0.007) (26).

Same-day/accelerated ART initiation

Prior to the implementation of universal test and treat programs, accelerated ART initiation was implemented in several settings. Accelerated ART initiation involves starting eligible patients (CD4 cell count < 350 cells/uL) on ART as soon as possible following HIV diagnosis. Accelerated ART initiation was implemented as a complementary intervention to POC CD4 testing in four studies and as a unique intervention in one study where a clinical algorithm was used to assess eligibility. The most notable study, the Rapid Initiation of ART in Pregnancy (RAP) program in South Africa, which investigated same-day ART initiation in ART-eligible pregnant women, resulted in 97% of mothers initiating therapy during pregnancy (15). Two studies that included accelerated ART initiation for eligible patients as a part of a combination intervention strategy reported increased LTC and retention in care at 12 months among patients who received the intervention compared to those who received the standard of care (27, 28). The effects of individual interventions, however, could not be assessed.

Provider-initiated testing and counselling (PITC)

Although recommended by the WHO in all countries experiencing an HIV epidemic, PITC had a negative impact on LTC in both outpatient and in-patient settings in studies evaluated in this systematic review. In a study of six Rwandan facilities, LTC among patients decreased after the introduction of PITC in out-patient departments (aOR 2.0; 95% CI: 1.0–4.2; p = 0.051) (29). Non-acceptance of HIV diagnosis, stigma and healthcare service problems were cited as barriers to LTC. Only one study evaluated the effect of providing HCT to in-patients; a randomized trial among medical inpatients in Mulago Hospital in Uganda found that 55.8% of patients who received in-patient HCT attended an HIV clinic compared to 73.6% of those who were referred for out-patient HCT 1-week post-discharge (χ2 = 4.58; p = 0.032) (30).

Patient-level interventions at the time of diagnosis

Home-based HIV counseling and testing (HBHCT)

Study participants were identified through HBHCT in 13 studies. HBHCT was assessed as the primary intervention in only three studies, (31, 32) one of which compared HBHCT to health facility HCT. In this study, LTC was lower in the HBHCT group: 64%, compared to the public sector health facilities group: 76.1% (difference 12.1%; 95% CI 7.8% to 16.5%; p < 0.001) (31). Likewise, within the SEARCH test and treat trial in Kenya and Uganda, those who were diagnosed by home-based testing were about 25% less likely to link to care within a month of testing, compared to those who received their diagnosis through community health campaigns (aRR 0.78, 95% CI: 0.70 – 0.86) (33). When coupled with other strategies to facilitate LTC, such as POC CD4 testing, accelerated ART initiation and counseling sessions, HBHCT led to better outcomes than HBHCT alone (18, 25, 34–37).

Mobile testing

None of the studies that compared mobile testing as an exclusive intervention to clinic or facility-based testing showed a higher percentage in LTC. In one study, recipients of mobile testing were less likely to undergo CD4 testing (33% vs. 83%) than individuals tested in clinic, and they were also less likely to link to care (10% vs. 72%; p < 0.001, OR = 0.05, CI: 0.03 – 0.07) (38). However, studies that supplemented mobile testing with other strategies to enhance LTC had more promising results. In one study, 71% of patients who linked to care after a mobile testing diagnosis attributed the mobile unit’s referral to facilitating their linkage (39). Another study found that patients who were offered a POC CD4 test at the mobile testing unit had a higher relative risk of completing their referral visit than those who were not offered the POC test (RR 1.23; 95% CI: 1.00–1.57) (40). Other strategies including counseling sessions (19) and follow-up phone calls (33, 41, 42) increased LTC following HIV diagnosis at a mobile testing unit. Aiming to reach FSW through community-based HCT was assessed in one study, which found that FSW in the intervention group were more likely to have initiated ART (100% vs. 71.5%; p = 0.04) (43).

Motivational/supportive interventions

Counseling

Counseling was one of the most frequently reported intervention strategies across studies. Various forms of counseling, including peer counseling and provider-led counseling were assessed. Peer counseling among people living with HIV (PLHIV) was evaluated in five studies, which predominantly showed increased LTC. Peer counseling was particularly effective in the study that focused on youth: ART initiation was at 80% among youth who had peer mentors compared to those who did not (45%) (44). The only study that showed no significant effect of peer counseling on the primary outcome measure was a cluster randomized trial among FSW in Zimbabwe. In this study of a targeted combination including peer education and support, 86% of FSW in the intervention group-initiated ART compared to 83% in the usual-care group (45).

Counseling patients who were ineligible for ART during the pre-ART period improved LTC, but not ART initiation. Participants who received pre-ART counseling in a randomized controlled trial in Uganda were 80% more likely to link to care (defined as visiting a health center for clinical check-up) within five months (RR 1.8; 95% CI: 1.4 – 2.1) (46). However, participants in a prospective cohort study in Uganda who completed pre-ART adherence counseling had longer delays from ART eligibility to initiation (median 49 vs. 14 days, p < 0.01) (47).

Counseling also bolstered the effects of other interventions such as POC CD4 testing and HBHCT. In two of the studies that assessed POC CD4 testing as a single intervention, there was no significant difference from the control group, however a significant increase in LTC was observed when POC CD4 testing was coupled with counseling (18, 19). Counseling provided following HBHCT also led to an increase in LTC in a cluster randomized trial in Uganda (OR = 2.18; 95% CI: 1.26–3.78; p = 0.008) (37). In two studies where participants were interviewed following receipt of a combination intervention package, counseling was cited as a major facilitator in LTC (14, 15).

Technology-based intervention (calls and texts)

The use of text messages and/or phone calls to communicate with patients was implemented as the predominant intervention in four studies and was met with mixed results, owing partly to the differences in definitions of LTC or primary outcome measures. A study in South Africa, which implemented a call center to facilitate LTC following HIV diagnosis in a mobile testing unit found that a majority of individuals (51%) were linked to care (defined as obtaining CD4 testing to determine ART eligibility) with a mean time to linkage of 31 days (41). In contrast, a randomized controlled trial in South Africa, with a more stringent primary outcome measure (completion of at least 3 months of ART or 6 months of TB treatment for coinfected participants) found that 39% of outcome-eligible participants who had received phone calls and text messages over 4 months reached the primary outcome compared to 42% in the routine care group (RR 0.93; 95% CI: 0.80 to 1.08) (48). Likewise, implementation of a mobile health application to relay HIV laboratory results, support messages and appointment reminders did not lead to a significant increase in LTC among the broader adult population (61% in the control group vs. 64.1% in the intervention group; p = 0.551). However, the effect of this intervention was significant among youth: LTC was approximately 20% higher for youth with the app (31.9% control vs. 53% intervention; p = 0.009) (49). Implementation of text messages and/or phone calls for follow-up or appointment reminders consistently led to increased LTC when combined with other interventions such as counseling and (22, 33, 34, 42, 50–52) POC CD4 testing with accelerated ART initiation, (14, 22, 27, 28) however the distinct effects of the interventions were not assessed.

Incentives

Studies that investigated the independent effects of financial incentives (both cash and non-cash) consistently found that they did not significantly affect LTC. The least effective study of the use of cash incentives was a randomized controlled trial in South Africa, which showed that there were no significant differences in LTC (aoR 0.70; 95% CI: 0.26 – 1.91) and initiation of ART (aOR 0.67; 95% CI: 0.26 – 1.78) between the intervention group that had received a conditional cash incentive and the control group (53). Non-cash financial incentives such as pre-paid mobile airtime and transport reimbursement were also studied. A cluster-randomized trial in Mozambique showed that offering pre-paid mobile airtime did not significantly affect LTC within 1 month after diagnosis (RR = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.81 – 1.16) (27). In another study in Mozambique where pre-paid mobile airtime was offered as part of a combination intervention, participants reported the incentive as the least useful intervention for linkage (14). Another non-cash financial incentive, transport reimbursement, combined with POC CD4 testing did not improve LTC when compared to the standard of care HR: 1.1 (95% CI: 0.88 to 1.3, p = 0.5) (19).

Health system

Integration of Preventing Mother to Child Transmission (PMTCT) services (HCT/ART initiation) into antenatal care (ANC)

Designing interventions specific for pregnant women living with HIV, through integration of HCT and ART services into ANC was the most effective intervention across studies. It consistently resulted in an increased likelihood of ART initiation among intervention groups compared to those using non-integrated services. One notable study, a cluster-randomized controlled trial of 12 health-care facilities in Nigeria, found that mothers who accessed care in facilities with an integrated PMTCT care package were more likely to initiate ART (97% vs. 39%; aRR 3.3; 95% CI: 1.4 – 7.8) (16). Another study in South Africa evaluated three models of ART initiation in pregnancy: ART services integrated into ANC, ART services located proximal to ANC or services located distal to ANC. Women who visited the ANC with integrated services were significantly more likely to begin ART during pregnancy (RR 1.33; 95% CI: 1.09 – 1.64) compared to those who visited ANC with ART services located distally. No differences were observed between ART services located proximally and those located distally, highlighting the value of total integration of services (54).

Integration of other health services

There were three studies that examined the effects of integration of HIV and tuberculosis (TB) services on ART initiation. While a small cross-sectional study in Uganda (n = 298) found no significant difference in the proportion of people who initiated ART during integrated periods compared to during non-integrated periods (47.7% vs. 33.8%; aOR 1.34; 95% CI = 0.40 – 4.47) (55), a larger retrospective cohort study in Eswatini (n = 24,772), which included HIV/TB services decentralization and integration into primary care clinics as a part of a rapid ART expansion combination program, found an 8.4 fold increase in the number of people active on ART (56).

Only one study investigated the effects of integration of HIV services into an opioid treatment program on ART initiation: The Integrated Methadone and Anti-Retroviral Therapy (IMAT) strategy in Tanzania led to a doubling of the 90-day ART initiation rate (41% pre-IMAT to 87% post-IMAT; p < 0.001) (57).

Task shifting

Task shifting involves the delegation of various aspects of care to health professionals other than doctors, such as nurses and community health workers. Task shifting was described as a unique intervention or a part of a multiple component intervention package in 11 studies. The most promising results from a study which assessed task shifting as a single intervention were from the Streamlining Tasks and Roles to Expand Treatment and Care for HIV (STRETCH) intervention, which involved nurse-led ART. In this cluster randomized controlled trial conducted in South Africa, viral load suppression rates were equivalent to doctor-led ART and a non-significant difference in mortality (58). With the shortage of doctors in many facilities, nurses can be leveraged to expand HIV care and ART initiation. The results of two studies that evaluated the effect of the Nurse Initiated Management of ART (NIMART) program on ART initiation support the implementation of task shifting. These studies found that the NIMART program led to significant increases in ART initiation (59, 60).

Use of an electronic medical record (EMR) system

There were two studies that evaluated the effects of an EMR system as an individual intervention on HIV care. Introduction of an EMR in rural clinics in Kenya was associated with better compliance to HIV guidelines for pre-ART care, which includes CD4 testing (61). More than half (53.4%) of patients in the EMR group had at least one CD4 test compared to 38.8% in the paper group (p < 0.001, aOR 1.59; 95% CI 1.49–1.69). The median time from enrollment into HIV care to first CD4 test was 1.40 months (IQR 0.47–4.87) for paper vs. 0.93 months (IQR 0.43 – 3.37) for EMR. Another study of the use of an EMR system in HIV care, conducted in Rwanda, observed a significant reduction of 32.4% in CD4 counts that did not reach clinicians during consultation (p = 0.002; χ2 = 9.146) (62). In addition, automated reports from the EMR system identified 178 children born to HIV positive mothers who had not been tested. Of these children, 15 were found to be HIV positive and all received comprehensive care (62).

Training traditional healers

A pre-post educational intervention study in three rural districts in Mozambique showed a significant increase in HIV knowledge among traditional healers (67% score pre-intervention and 81% knowledge score post-intervention), which increased referral rates. There was a 35% (p = 0.046) increase in median monthly referral rate (0.25 patients pre-intervention compared to 0.34 patients post-intervention) (63). However, only 3.5% of referred patients were tested for HIV at a clinic.

Improving provider practice through educational outreach or campaigns

Efforts to improve provider practice were investigated in two studies and yielded mixed results. Implementation of a provider educational outreach program to increase recruitment into an ART program from primary care was not effective (53% vs. 50%; 1.19, 0.51 to 2.77) (64). However, another study of a quality improvement campaign among health workers improved ART initiation rates when the target population was pregnant women: the number of pregnant women initiated on ART increased from 21 per month (95% CI: 2 to 40) at baseline to 124 per month (95% CI: 108 to 140) after the intervention (p < 0.001). This amounts to a 4-fold increase in ART initiation by pregnant women when compared to the control district where no improvements were seen (65).

DISCUSSION

Given the biomedical advancements in HIV treatment, there has been an increased focus on long-term support of PLHIV by minimizing barriers to care and establishing supportive partnerships with patients and their families. The results presented here are consistent with these themes and highlight three interrelated trends: first, population-specific demographics matter and should inform the implementation of interventions; second, some strategies are not effective in isolation but gain synergistic impact when combined with other approaches; third, the most effective strategies prioritize building supportive communities and establishing meaningful relationships, as opposed to interventions that provide non-adaptive or universal incentives or reminders.

The importance of considering which patients are most likely to benefit from a given intervention is best demonstrated by the differential effects of similar intervention strategies among pregnant women as compared to the broader population. Among the most impactful studies looking at the efficacy of point-of-care testing in promoting LTC and ART initiation were two that focused on PMTCT (15, 16). Similarly, the most impactful study analyzing accelerated ART initiation was a study in South Africa which found that most pregnant women initiated ART in the context of antenatal care services (15). These outcomes may represent the opportunity of women receiving healthcare services for pregnancy to concurrently obtain HIV testing, counseling, and ART initiation, while possibly circumventing the barriers to HIV care experienced outside the context of prenatal care. Additionally, ART initiation among pregnant women may also be motivated by the intention to prevent vertical HIV transmission during delivery. These findings also highlight the challenges of LTC and ART initiation interventions using a universal non-specific approach. Instead, intentional planning to understand who is likely to benefit from a given implementation strategy — and how to design supplemental interventions for those who will not — is crucial (66, 67).

Substantial motivations also exist for gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (MSM), as well as FSW who have identified service needs that often go unmet, resulting in increased risk for acquisition and transmission of HIV (68). A community-based ART distribution program in Tanzania aiming to reach FSW resulted in 100% ART initiation in the intervention group (43). Data characterizing interventions focused on the HIV care continuum among key populations at disproportional risk for HIV, including MSM, FSW, people who use drugs, prisoners, and transgender persons, are scarce, highlighting the need for more interventional programs geared toward these groups. Adopting differentiated service delivery (DSD) models to tailor interventions according to the needs, preferences, and circumstances specific to identified populations is important moving forward (69). For example, peer support programs were especially effective among young people, who constitute another key demographic. Though younger age groups are diverse and cannot be expected to respond uniformly to any single intervention strategy, these findings suggest that thoughtfully tailoring programs increases the likelihood of generating meaningful change.

Broad systems-level efforts to improve ART accessibility may be sufficient to successfully initiate and retain pregnant mothers in treatment programs, but may not be as efficacious with all PLHIV in a given society or community. In some circumstances, combining strategies that are independently insufficient provides a meaningful way to re-purpose existing resources and frameworks to achieve more effective outcomes. For example, home-based HCT and mobile testing increased the number of patients who got tested for HIV, but they did not improve LTC when used in isolation. Similarly, educational interventions aimed at training traditional healers resulted in increased referral rates, but yielded minimal changes in subsequent numbers of individuals receiving HIV testing and treatment. Moreover, implementation of text messages and/or phone calls for follow-up or appointment reminders consistently led to increased LTC when combined with other interventions such as counseling, POC CD4 testing and accelerated ART initiation. Rather than generating novel stand-alone interventions, combining existing strategies that may not independently be consequential could provide a realistic and beneficial step forward in LMICs. Specifically, strategies such as POC testing, same-day ART initiation, home testing, mobile testing, and financial incentives may be most effective if implemented in concert with other interventions such as follow-up phone calls, support groups and counseling sessions (16, 20, 34, 38, 53, 70). Combinations of strategies that lack significant effectiveness in isolation should be oriented towards building supportive communities and establishing balanced relationships, both among patients and with their healthcare providers.

These findings also suggest that the quality of patient-provider interactions may matter more than which member of the health care team is providing counseling and care. For this reason, task-shifting interventions proved favorable and are not disadvantageous in LMIC settings with fewer physicians. Two studies evaluated the effect of the Nurse Initiated Management on ART initiation (NIMART) and found that these interventions led to significant increases in ART initiation by nurses (59, 60). Task shifting to nurses also led to an increase in the number of patients who initiated ART (51). Strategies that respect patients’ time and effort in the context of patients navigating difficult diagnoses, complex long-term drug regimens and complex health systems are notable. For example, integrative approaches that bundled ART with other healthcare services (such as treatment for TB or opioid use disorder) showed some promise.

This systematic review is subject to several limitations. Primarily, the studies reviewed here were completed in countries across Sub-Saharan Africa potentially limiting the generalizability of these findings to other regions with different cultural, social and health systems. Similarly, challenges that diminished the effectiveness of interventions in the 13 countries from which we analyzed literature may not be as prevalent elsewhere. It is therefore critical to distinguish between productive interventions and efficacious implementation, which are closely related but not inseparable. For example, a program or intervention with modest effects could be optimized if implemented in a longitudinally sustainable manner. In contrast, a beneficial intervention could be diminished by poor implementation, including chronic underfunding or understaffing (71). Direct comparison among studies was sometimes confounded by heterogenous definitions of LTC as a primary outcome measure. LTC was often defined as visiting a health center post diagnosis, obtaining a CD4 cell count or initiating ART. Nonetheless, consistent patterns and trends common in intervention outcomes across the settings explored provide valuable insights for consideration when deciding on LTC and ART initiation strategies, regardless of locale.

Taken together, several themes emerge from these findings. Above all, optimizing communication with patients by conveying empathy, providing a sense of belonging, and exploring the meaning of their diagnosis can meaningfully contribute to improved LTC and ART initiation. In contrast, provider-initiated non-adaptive LTC interventions as well as cash incentives did not prove to be as substantive in improving retention and initiation of ART. As with any therapeutic relationship, patients’ investment in their own care and wellbeing is essential. Ultimately, balanced and sustained partnerships that integrate client preferences, and offer meaningful support for people living with HIV are crucial in the implementation of sustainable and effective programs to initiate and support antiretroviral therapy.
