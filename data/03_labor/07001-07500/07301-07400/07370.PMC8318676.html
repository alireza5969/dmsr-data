Feasibility of mHealth interventions for depressive symptoms in Latin America: a systematic review

Abstract

Depression is a prevalent disorder and leading cause of disability in Latin America, where the mental health treatment gap is still above 50%. We sought to synthesise and assess the quality of the evidence on the feasibility of mHealth-based interventions for depression in Latin America. We conducted a literature search of studies published in 2007 and after using four electronic databases. We included peer-reviewed articles, in English, Spanish or Portuguese, that evaluated interventions for depressive symptoms. Two authors independently extracted data using forms developed a priori. We assessed appropriateness of reporting utilising the CONSORT checklist for feasibility trials. Eight manuscripts were included for full data extraction. Appropriate reporting varied greatly. Most (n = 6, 75%) of studies were conducted in primary care settings and sought to deliver psychoeducation or behaviour change interventions for depressive symptoms. We found great heterogeneity in the assessment of feasibility. Two studies used comparator conditions. mHealth research for depression in Latin America is scarce. Included studies showed some feasibility despite methodological inconsistencies. Given the dire need for evidence-based mental health interventions in this region, governments and stakeholders must continue promoting and funding research tailored to cultural and population characteristics with subsequent pragmatic clinical trials.

Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2010, mental and substance use disorders are the leading causes of disability in Latin America (LA). Among them, depression and anxiety are the most prevalent disorders in the region, being overall the 6th and 13th, respectively, leading causes of DALYs (disability-adjusted life years). As a consequence, mental disorders are the main cause of health loss in LA when comparing to any other region of the world (Whiteford & Baxter, 2013). Among the factors contributing to these statistics are the insufficient specialised human and infrastructure resources, lack of widespread public mental health policy, and the striking disparities in access to health care, along with a slow transition to community care models, resulting in a concerning treatment gap of 74% (Kohn et al., 2018; Minoletti et al., 2012). Moreover, workforce shortages and budget constraints in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) represent an important obstacle for providing quality mental health care (Kakuma et al., 2011; Patel et al., 2016). LA countries have the lowest median number (8.8 per 100,000 persons) of mental health workers when compared to all other regions across the Americas, mostly comprised by psychologists, psychiatrists, and psychiatric nurses (Pan American Health Organization, 2018). This creates challenges and opportunities for delivering mental health services in LA, where most of mental health services are government-funded.

At the same time, we have seen an exponential growth and penetration of mobile communication networks and adoption of wireless devices adoption over the last 20 years, with consistent increased usage among all age and income groups (Pew Research Center, 2019). LA’s emerging economies have been consistent with this trend. By 2018, around 67% of LA population had an active mobile subscription, and this is expected to grow to 73% in next 5 years (GSMA Intelligence, 2019). Given this ubiquity of mobile communication devices, mental health researchers have explored the potential of these tools for multiple purposes such as disorders screening, symptoms monitoring, medication adherence, or delivery of psychological interventions (Bakker et al., 2016). The World Health Organisation uses the term mHealth to group these diverse interventions delivered through wireless connected mobile devices, defining it as ‘the use of mobile and wireless technologies to support the achievement of health objectives.’ (Bradway et al., 2017) These technologies can involve short voice or text messaging services (SMS), smartphone applications (apps) exchanging data over general packet radio service (GPRS), 3G, 4G and 5G systems, global positioning systems (GPS), and Bluetooth technologies such as those employed by wearables (e.g. smartwatches, smartbands, etc) (World Health Organization & World Health Organization, 2011).

The World Health Organisation guidelines for implementation of health digital technologies in LMICs recommend the usage of these technologies in a way that complements traditional delivery of mental health services (World Health Organization, 2019). Many studies have demonstrated the feasibility and efficacy of mHealth platforms in facilitating access to, and extending cost-efficient mental health care interventions in developed economies (Firth, Torous, Nicholas, Carney, Pratap, et al., 2017; Firth, Torous, Nicholas, Carney, Pratap, Rosenbaum, et al., 2017). However, developing, testing, and implementing these technologies in resource-poor settings requires the study of these solutions in said resource-poor settings in order to understand how culture, technology access and other factors influence their feasibility, acceptability, and comparative effectiveness (Menezess et al., 2015). Despite their urgent need of cost-effective mental health care solutions, LA countries lag behind in the development and implementation of mHealth technologies (Arjadi et al., 2015; Naslund et al., 2017). Given the particular socioeconomic and cultural characteristics of LA countries as well as their specific mental health care needs, it is of interest to evaluate the feasibility and availability of mobile mental health platforms in the region, especially regarding the most prevalent disorders such as anxiety and depression. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review with three main goals (1) to identify peer-reviewed articles that examined the feasibility of mHealth-based interventions focussed on improving symptoms of prevalent disorders in LA, such as depressive and anxiety symptoms, (2) to describe the characteristics of these studies, including the reporting quality of the study, and (3) to describe how study feasibility was measured across studies in order to gain perspective on the current state of the field and identify new directions for future research.

Methods

We report this review in accordance with the requirements of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Statement (PRISMA) (Supplementary Appendix 1). Our review protocol is registered in the PROPERO database (no. CRD42020166553), and we make it available here as a supplement (Supplementary Appendix 2).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included references published in peer-reviewed journals of medicine, public health, or social sciences, in English, Spanish, or Portuguese. On a case-by-case basis, we allowed references from conference proceedings, if these required full manuscript submission and peer-review before acceptance. Included references had to focus on an intervention delivered through mHealth (including mobile apps, wearable devices, or short text messages –SMS-). Moreover, interventions had to have either a primary or a secondary focus on improving depressive or anxiety symptoms in the context of a specific mental disorder (including depressive, bipolar, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, trauma- and stress-related, or schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders) or another health condition. Manuscripts had to be from studies conducted in Latin America (including South and Central America, the Caribbean, and Mexico). Finally, we excluded manuscripts if they were theses, dissertations, or opinion pieces.

Search process

A health sciences librarian (RM) performed searches using the Ovid MEDLINE (published database from 1946 onward), EMBASE.com (published database from 1947 onward), SciELO Citation Index (published database from 2002 onward), and LILACS (published database from 1982 onward) databases. We developed different searches for each database using both controlled vocabulary and natural language. The concepts searched were mental disorders, mHealth interventions, and Latin America. Given the databases LILACS and SciELO primarily contain literature in the geographic region of interest, we searched these two databases using mental disorders and mHealth strings only. References were downloaded on 28 February 2020. After importing them into a bibliography managing software (Bramer et al., 2016), duplicate references were removed. We provide the entire list of terms, descriptors, and search strings as a supplement (Supplementary Appendix 3).

Study selection and data extraction

We completed all screening and data extraction procedures with DistillerSR (n.d.). Using this platform, we created structured forms to systematically guide the entire screening and data extraction process. Two researchers (CEV and DHM) independently screened all titles and abstracts and assessed full manuscript texts to determine eligibility. Interrater reliability was almost perfect (Cohen’s Kappa 0.98). Because we wanted to minimise risk of reviewer bias, we held consensus meetings between the coders and the rest of the team (HRM, MGT, LCC) to resolve differences. These meetings were sufficient to resolve all conflicts, and adjudication procedures were not needed.

Our extraction forms included six groups of data: (1) study logistics (authors, year of publication, geographic setting, research methodology, research design, and funding source), (2) study population (number of participants, recruitment methods, gender, age, education, employment status, household income, comorbidities), and (3) Intervention delivery (intervention name, type of device used, operational system, study length, intervention description, theoretical framework, comparison group, engagement measures used). Moreover, data extraction forms also included (4) clinical outcomes (main and secondary clinical outcomes, instrument used to measure, frequency of measurement), (5) main findings and limitations, and (6) appropriateness of reporting.

Quality of study reporting

We assessed quality of reporting for all included manuscripts. Given that all included manuscripts fell into a single study category, we used the CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility trials statement and checklist (Eldridge et al., 2016; Thabane et al., 2016b). This CONSORT statement consists of 40-item check-list, which can be used to assess all sections of feasibility research manuscripts. We assigned values of 0–1 to each item, depending on whether checkmark was present. Therefore, the total score 40 means the manuscript fully met CONSORT standards of appropriate reporting. We provide our assessment as a supplement (Supplementary Appendix 4).

Results

Study identification

We identified 988 citations. Of these, 241 citations were from Ovid Medline, 465 were from EMBASE, 145 from LILACS, and 137 citations were from SCIELO. There were 226 duplicates, and after removing these, we had 762 references for initial screening (Figure 1). After initial title and abstract screening, we excluded 714 references from the review. In this part of the process, we reviewed six systematic or scoping reviews that focussed on lower and middle-income countries and manually identified 2 references that seemed potentially eligible for this project. Therefore, we fully reviewed fifty manuscripts and assessed their eligibility. Of these, we excluded thirty-two that did not focus on a mHealth-delivered intervention, five that were not conducted in Latin America, and five that did not seek to improve depressive nor anxiety symptoms. Eight research manuscripts were included for full review and synthesis.

Study characteristics and quality of reporting

Of the eight manuscripts included, seven (87.5%) assessed feasibility of an mHealth intervention (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018; Grunauer et al., 2014; Janevic et al., 2016; Menezes et al., 2019; Narvaez et al., 2016; Piette et al., 2012; Whittemore et al., 2020), and one (12.5%) assessed ‘viability’ of mHealth interventions or assessments (Vieira de Lima et al., 2019) (Table 1). All of the included studies had a primary or secondary goal of improving depressive symptoms. Studies were conducted in Ecuador (Grunauer et al., 2014), Honduras/Mexico (Piette et al., 2012), Bolivia (Janevic et al., 2016), Brazil/Peru (Menezes et al., 2019), Colombia (Narvaez et al., 2016), Peru (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018), Brazil (Vieira de Lima et al., 2019), and Mexico (Whittemore et al., 2020). Study duration was variable across all eight studies, ranging from 6 weeks to 6 months; two manuscripts did not report study duration (Grunauer et al., 2014; Narvaez et al., 2016). Four studies (50%) used flip-phones (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018; Janevic et al., 2016; Whittemore et al., 2020), three (37.5%) used smartphones (Menezes et al., 2019; Narvaez et al., 2016; Vieira de Lima et al., 2019), and two (25%) used tablets (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018; Grunauer et al., 2014).

Six (75%) studies were conducted among patients attending primary care facilities (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018; Grunauer et al., 2014; Janevic et al., 2016; Menezes et al., 2019; Piette et al., 2012; Whittemore et al., 2020), one (12.5%) at infectious disease outpatient clinics (Vieira de Lima et al., 2019), and one (12.5%) among faculty members at an urban university campus (Narvaez et al., 2016). Among studies that reported participants’ age, median ranged between 45 and 58 years old in five (50%) studies (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018; Janevic et al., 2016; Menezes et al., 2019; Piette et al., 2012; Whittemore et al., 2020). In one study, participants median age was 30.6 (Vieira de Lima et al., 2019). Two (25%) studies did not report participants’ mean age (Grunauer et al., 2014; Narvaez et al., 2016).

Overall, reporting quality of results was variable. Out of a maximum of 40 possible points, CONSORT (Eldridge et al., 2016; Thabane et al., 2016b, 2016a) scores among included studies ranged from 15 to 28. While all included studies met the reporting standards in the title, abstract, and introduction sections, no study met all the criteria for adequate reporting of the methods, results, or discussion sections (for a detailed report, we refer the reader to Supplementary Appendix 4).

Intervention characteristics and main findings

Characteristics of the interventions varied across studies (Table 2). Two (25%) studies assessed the feasibility of using tablets (Grunauer et al., 2014) or a mobile app (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018) for screening of depressive symptoms; in one of these studies authors also compared the validity and reliability of three different depression scales for this purpose (Grunauer et al., 2014). One (12.5%) study sought to assess the usability of a mobile app for preventing depressive symptoms in the context of Burnout syndrome (Narvaez et al., 2016). Three (37.5%) studies sought to deliver educational messages or pictures about depression and other mental concerns using WhatsApp (Vieira de Lima et al., 2019) or SMS (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018; Whittemore et al., 2020). Another three (37.5%) studies assessed the feasibility of delivering behaviour change messages, behavioural activation sessions, or depression psychoeducation service via either automated phone calls (Piette et al., 2012), interactive voice response (Janevic et al., 2016), or a mobile app (Menezes et al., 2019). Among studies delivering a behavioural intervention, theoretical frameworks included theory of planned behaviour (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018), behaviour change/activation (Menezes et al., 2019; Piette et al., 2012), and psychoeducation (Janevic et al., 2016; Menezes et al., 2019; Whittemore et al., 2020).

In the studies included, main outcomes assessed also varied widely. While five (62.5%) studies included some measure of feasibility as main study outcome (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018; Grunauer et al., 2014; Janevic et al., 2016; Narvaez et al., 2016; Vieira de Lima et al., 2019), three studies used change overtime (e.g. depression, HBA1c, etc.) as main outcome (Menezes et al., 2019; Piette et al., 2012; Whittemore et al., 2020). Among studies in which feasibility indicators were not the main outcome, only one (Menezes et al., 2019) assessed feasibility measures as secondary study outcomes. Notably, with the exception of two (25%) manuscripts (Piette et al., 2012; Whittemore et al., 2020), studies included did not have a comparator condition.

Assessment of feasibility for delivering the interventions (whether these interventions had screening, prevention, or therapeutic purpose) via mHealth was variable across all studies. We summarised these findings in Table 2. Among studies focussed on delivering screening instruments, feasibility was assessed with completion rates of the assessments, either among patients (Grunauer et al., 2014) or primary care providers (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018).

Among studies using mHealth to deliver educational content about depression or other mental health concerns, feasibility was assessed with participant confirmation rates of receiving the SMS (Vieira de Lima et al., 2019) or picture messages (Whittemore et al., 2020) and qualitative assessments (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018). Studies delivering behaviour change or behavioural activation evaluated feasibility assessing satisfaction with care (Piette et al., 2012), satisfaction with program (Janevic et al., 2016), or behavioural activation session rates of completion (Menezes et al., 2019). Finally, one study used a validated scale to assess app usability and satisfaction (Narvaez et al., 2016).

Discussion

With this review, we sought to identify, describe, and assess the quality of peer-reviewed research that evaluated the feasibility of mHealth-based interventions for depression or anxiety in LA countries. Despite the increasingly widespread use of mobile technology in the region, we found a very low number of studies that evaluated the feasibility or preliminary efficacy of mHealth-based interventions targeting depression or anxiety symptoms. Most studies were conducted at primary health care settings and recruited older participants. We also found great variability in both how studies assessed intervention feasibility and reported study results. Below we discuss the implications of these findings.

We developed a comprehensive set of inclusion criteria and systematic online search approach (e.g. we included papers in which depressive symptoms were a secondary clinical outcome of interest). However, we only found few LA studies that examined the feasibility of utilising mHealth-based interventions to target depressive symptoms. Furthermore, we did not find mHealth interventions aiming to detect, diagnose or treat anxiety symptoms or disorders. Given the high prevalence of both depressive and anxiety disorders in LA (Whiteford & Baxter, 2013), it is surprising that only half of studies found in this review were either specifically screening or targeting one of these mental health symptoms. At least four of the studies found in this review were aimed at screening or help coping with comorbid depressive symptoms as a result of other illnesses such as diabetes, hypertension or HIV (Piette et al., 2012; Vieira de Lima et al., 2019; Whittemore et al., 2020). Compared to other regions with predominance of LMIC, such as Africa and Asia, the number of mHealth studies in LA remains minimal. Among the identified barriers for the use of mHealth platforms throughout LMIC are infrastructure, lack of equipment and technology gap (Kruse et al., 2019). Nonetheless, mobile networks penetration and smartphone adoption in LA reach 68% and 69% of the population respectively, in comparison to the lower the levels of Sub-Saharan Africa (45% and 45%) and even higher than Asia-Pacific (60 and 64%) (GSMA Intelligence, 2020).

Even though a small number of studies were included in this systematic review, it is worth noting the striking variability of terms, definitions, criteria, designs, and measures employed to evaluate mHealth platformś feasibility, usability, or acceptability. For instance, three (Grunauer et al., 2014; Janevic et al., 2016; Vieira de Lima et al., 2019) of the eight studies assessed rates of completed or received interventions from the total planned as their main feasibility measure. One of these studies adopted the term of viability to refer to feasibility. On the other hand, three (Menezes et al., 2019; Piette et al., 2012; Whittemore et al., 2020) assessed the feasibility of evaluation of clinical outcomes such as the depressive symptoms, blood pressure and glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c). Finally, only one (Narvaez et al., 2016) study used a standardised method to assess usability such as the System usability scale (SUS) whereas another (Diez-Canseco et al., 2018) used rates of screenings completed and exit qualitative assessments with both providers and patients. This heterogeneity is widely spread across the emerging mHealth field, and it is a relevant barrier for interpreting, validating, and generalising the results (Chandrashekar, 2018; Ng et al., 2019). The lack of uniform definitions, criteria and methodologies across mHealth studies extends also to randomised controlled trials (RCT) (Tønning et al., Faurholt-Jepsen et al., Faurholt-Jepsen et al., 2020), which were remarkably absent in LA despite also being in our initial search criteria. This might be a crucial factor hampering the advancement of the whole field, as it makes difficult for stakeholders, decision-makers and regulatory agencies to validate their effectiveness. There is still an urgent need to reach a consensus on common guidelines and definitions in the field as well as adequately designed, powered and conducted RCTs or pragmatic effectiveness trials (Hidalgo-Mazzei et al., 2020; Torous et al., 2019).

Among the definitions and measures requiring consensus, engagement and retention are the most used measures of feasibility. Several studies have demonstrated that one of the main issues of e-mental health interventions is lack of participants’ adherence and commitment given the lack of face-to-face contact with mental health care teams (Ng et al., 2019). Most interventions required a minimum amount of data or participation in order to provide accurate symptom monitoring or reach their therapeutic aims. Despite their relatively short follow-up periods, only two (Menezes et al., 2019; Piette et al., 2012) studies included in this review investigated this aspect. However, none of the studies included in this review reported how the intervention could have been integrated in a real-world clinical practice (e.g. communication with clinicians or health care team in case of relapse or worsening). Hence, there is a major need of pragmatic, real-world studies evaluating not only efficacy and retention of stand-alone and independent mHealth platforms, but also effectiveness and retention when implemented in community health care systems (Massoudi et al., 2019). These studies might be particularly important in LMIC countries in LA and other regions of the world that have made strides in growing provision of mental health services in primary care. In these settings, primary care doctors and clinicians could be greatly served with practical ways of monitoring their patients with depression (World Health Organization, 2016).

A promising pathway to remediate the lack of engagement with mHealth interventions that deservers further research is enhancing data collection through subjective tests with automatically generated data (i.e. passive sensing) from smartphoneś usage patterns (e.g. calls, SMSs, apps, social media, etc.). Moreover, passive sensing through sensors and wearables might particularly relevant in individuals with cognitive deficits (Van Til et al., 2020). This user characterisation using passively collected data from wireless devices has been denominated with different terms such as digital phenotyping or phonotyping (Rollman & Brent, 2019). There are already some encouraging results from studies conducted in developed countries demonstrating the relationship of illness activity in mood disorders and passive sensing from smartphones as well as wearables (Doryab et al., 2019; Faurholt-Jepsen et al., 2020; Scott et al., 2017). While one systematic review found no studies that used passive sensing for mental health outcomes in LMICs of other regions of the world (Naslund et al., 2017), one ongoing study in rural areas of Nepal is using accelerometers, GPS, Bluetooth, and micro-phones to compare behavioural patterns between 20 adolescent mothers with and 20 without depression (Poudyal et al., 2019). To our knowledge, no study involving passively collected data in relation with depression is currently being conducted in LA.

Passive sensing has two important advantages in that it allows continuous monitoring in real time with data collected in situ while enabling just-in-time interventions (Aung et al., 2017; Spruijt-Metz & Nilsen, 2014). Importantly, passive sensing is indirect and unobtrusive, that is without requiring individual’s direct involvement and without interrupting their daily lives. Smartphones and wearables possess a variety of sensors capable of measuring and capturing many behavioural and contextual variables. While there are several ethical and safety challenges to consider before it can be generalised (Hidalgo-Mazzei et al., 2018; Insel, 2018), passive sensing and mobile technology might be instrumental for bridging the existing mental health treatment gap, expanding the reach and capacity of the mental health systems in LMIC in Latin America and other regions of the world (Naslund et al., 2019).

Limitations

This systematic review has some limitations to consider: (1) Despite our extensive and comprehensive online search, which also included all possible terms in three languages, it is the possible we missed some publications listed in local databases or languages. (2) Due to the lack of a clear definition of mHealth, we might have missed some studies that used mobile devices, which employed other terms when reporting their results. Nonetheless, we adopted the most accepted and comprehensive definition of mHealth in line with the one proposed by the WHO. Moreover, we used a blinded parallel screening; two of the co-authors conducted eligibility and data extraction and all conflicts regarding definition of mHealth were discussed extensively to reach a consensus. (3) Due to the wide heterogeneity among studies’ design, methodology and reporting style, it was challenging to uniformly report similarities and bias among them, which might limit the broad generalisability of the conclusions regarding their quality. Nonetheless, we followed the latest CONSORT guidelines for reporting feasibility studies. (4) We did not have access to the platforms described in this review, instead we based our descriptions rely on the information provided in the manuscripts about their respective platforms. (5) Finally, like most systematic reviews, there is the inherent risk of not having included ongoing projects, which might not have been published during our search process and timeframe. However, our comprehensive search was updated until 3 months before reporting the results of our findings as pre-registered in our PROSPERO protocol.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of this systematic review show that research and implementation of mHealth platforms targeting the most prevalent and disabling conditions in LA, such as depression and anxiety, are still scarce. The few studies conducted so far have demonstrated their feasibility despite some inconsistencies inherent to the emerging field of the mHealth discipline. LA has high penetration and adoption of smartphones in LA and mHealth interventions have shown efficacy for monitoring and treating depression and anxiety in other countries. Thus, it is important for Latin America to continue growing and leveraging mHealth intervention as a cost-efficient delivery method to reach communities with urgent need of evidence-based mental health care. This ambitious aim is achievable if governments and stakeholders continue to recognise this unique opportunity by promoting and encouraging research projects for developing mHealth tools tailored to the regionś cultural characteristics, which should be validated through pragmatic clinical trials.
