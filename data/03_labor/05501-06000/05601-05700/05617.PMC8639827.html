Emotional Resilience of Older Adults during COVID-19: A Systematic Review of Studies of Stress and Well-Being

Abstract

Objectives:

To examine post-traumatic stress, depression, anxiety, and well-being in older adults under quarantine.

Methods:

A systematic review of CINAHL, Ovid EBM Reviews, Ovid Embase, Ovid Medline, Ovid PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science from 2000 to 2020 was conducted. Keywords included coronavirus, epidemic, quarantine, stress, mental health, and similar terms. Included studies enrolled participants under quarantine, quantitatively measured mental health or well-being, and characterized outcomes by age.

Results:

Of 894 initial results, 20 studies met the criteria and were included. Studies comprise 106,553 participants from eight countries, ages 6–100, two epidemics (COVID-19, SARS), and 27 assessment tools. One study found greater distress in older adults relative to younger adults, one found no significant differences, and 18 found lower negative outcomes in older participants in at least one metric.

Conclusions:

Older adults in this review generally have lower stress and less negative emotions under quarantine than younger adults. It is unknown how this compares to pre-pandemic mea¬sures. More representative and longitudinal studies are needed to measure the impact of quarantine on the mental health of older adults.

Clinical Implications:

As existing scales may not capture the full extent of pandemic psychological effects on older adults, clinicians must vigilantly monitor older adults’ mental health.

Introduction

The outbreak of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) caused by the novel severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) started in the Hubei Province of China in December 2019 and rapidly spread internationally (Dong, Du, & Gardner, 2020). On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared COVID-19 a global pandemic, after which a surge of countries implemented national preventive measures (Zheng et al., 2020). Strategies to curb the spread of the virus varied by country and region, however, household containment measures were utilized to a degree not seen in recent history. These included quarantine, defined as the separation and restriction of the movement of people who were exposed to a contagious disease, and isolation, which refers to the separation of sick people with a contagious disease from healthy individuals, though the terms are often used interchangeably. The most comparable recent outbreaks necessitating quarantine measures include Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) in 2003 (D. M. Bell, 2004) and Middle Eastern Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) in 2015 (Kim, Tandi, Choi, Moon, & Kim, 2017), both caused by other coronaviruses. However, quarantine was limited to the exposed and was not implemented on the long-term, multinational scale seen in the current COVID-19 pandemic, in which measures have included precautionary quarantine, broad stay-at-home orders, face mask requirements, transition to remote schooling and teleworking, restriction of mass gatherings, and others, directly affecting the entire population.

Older adults and those with medical comorbidities are at an increased risk of severe illness from the virus and are especially urged to adhere to preventive measures (CDC, 2020a), which emphasize keeping physical distance from people outside of one’s household (CDC, 2020b). For older adults, potential infection with SAR-CoV-2 poses a pronounced threat to their physical health and psychological well-being. It is to be expected that experiencing such a threat would be followed by an acute stress response and persistent post-traumatic stress symptoms. Studies of the psychological effects following the smaller-scale SARS and MERS outbreaks show increased anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress symptoms in affected individuals (Chua et al., 2004; Lee et al., 2007; Sim, Chong, Chan, & Soon, 2004). However, due to the more confined nature of the outbreaks, only a subset of the population (patients, health-care workers, and potential contacts) was impacted; therefore, evaluation of the effects of age is limited by the sample sizes and age distributions.

Older adults have been found to have overall lower rates of post-traumatic stress symptoms (Norris, 1992; K. Reynolds, Pietrzak, Mackenzie, Chou, & Sareen, 2016), including after natural disasters that affect a population equally (B. D. Bell, 1978). Some studies suggest a difference in symptom profile between post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in older and younger adults with higher hyperarousal, avoidance, and sleep problem scores in older adults, but lower intrusive subcategory scores and no differences in total mean symptoms (Goenjian et al., 1994). Moreover, somatic symptoms are more commonly reported in the older adults (Owens, Baker, Kasckow, Ciesla, & Mohamed, 2005). Considering that the COVID-19 virus puts older adults at particular risk of negative health outcomes, it could be hypothesized that post-traumatic stress symptoms in response to the COVID-19 pandemic and associated containment measures will be higher than in the relatively lower-risk younger population. However, considering the differences in presentation of PTSD in older adults, scales may vary in capturing the range of psychological effects.

It is difficult to assess the full extent of the repercussions when the pandemic is still ongoing, but it is possible to look at the effects of strict quarantine measures instituted at the beginning of the crisis, as well as to compare these findings to previous instances of relatively large-scale quarantine. We conducted a systematic review of literature to examine the mental health effects, particularly stress symptoms, of infectious disease outbreaks and associated quarantine in older individuals.

Methods

Search strategy

The literature was searched by a medical librarian for the concepts of post-traumatic stress and mental health in older individuals under pandemic quarantine. Search strategies were created using a combination of keywords and standardized index terms. Searches were run in June 2020 in CINAHL (1981+), Ovid EBM Reviews, Ovid Embase (1974+), Ovid Medline (1946+ including epub ahead of print, in-process & other non-indexed citations), Ovid PsycINFO (1806), Scopus (1970+) and Web of Science (1975+). Search strategies are provided in Supplement 1. Results were limited to the year 2000 onward. All results were exported to Endnote where obvious duplicates were removed. Reference lists of articles and reviews were searched for additional articles.

Studies were included if they (1) reported on primary research (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, clinical trials, etc.), (2) were published in peer-review journals (3) were written in English or Spanish, (4) included participants asked to enter quarantine outside of a hospital environment for at least 24 hours, (5) included data on the prevalence of mental illness or psychological well-being, and (6) included characterization of outcomes by age. The following types of articles were excluded: (1) case reports, case series, commentary, letters to the editor, perspectives, qualitative studies, abstracts, systematic reviews, (2) studies that reported only on specific subpopulations (e.g., health-care workers, students, etc.), and (3) studies that did not report symptoms by age range or symptoms correlation with age.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts of all studies were independently screened by two authors (ES, MIL) and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Full texts of all studies passing the initial screen were retrieved and reviewed by the primary reviewer (ES), with validation by a second reviewer (MIL, AH).

Data extraction

Quantitative data from studies deemed eligible on full-text review were extracted. Key information on the country of the study, dates of data collection, number of participants, age, treatment of the age variable, and utilized scales was extracted. Measures of association of psychopathology scores between age groups or measures of association of continuous age and scores were extracted, along with confidence intervals, standard errors, number of participants in each age group when present.

Risk of bias assessment

As all of the retrieved studies were prevalence studies, a tool for assessment of bias in prevalence studies was used to assess each included article (Hoy et al., 2012). The tool consisted 10 yes or no questions, with the count of negative answers totaled for a summary score of low, medium, or high risk of bias. Individual question values for each study are included in Supplement 2.

Statistical analysis

Cochrane Review Manager (Review Manager (RevMan), 2020) was used to create a forest plot of extracted odds ratios and 95% CIs. Random-effects methods were used due to the expected high heterogeneity of studies. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. Due to high statistical heterogeneity of included studies (I2 = 95%; p < .00001), broad range of scales utilized for symptom assessment, and differing subdivisions of age groups, meta-analysis of included studies was not performed, as interpretation of a combined analysis would be hindered by the diversity of reported metrics.

Results

After deduplication, 889 articles were identified using the electronic database search. An additional five articles were identified through review of article and systematic review reference lists (Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020 from; González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020 from; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020 from; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020 from Costantini et al., 2020; Qiu et al., 2020 from; Rossi et al., 2020), totaling 894 unique articles. Initial screen of titles and abstracts resulted in the exclusion of 847 based on the defined criteria. Full text of the remaining 43 articles was further assessed for the presence of quantitative data on the association between age and mental health measures, resulting in 20 included studies (Ahmed et al., 2020; Becerra-García, Giménez Ballesta, Sánchez-Gutiérrez, Barbeito Resa, & Calvo Calvo, 2020; Bruine De Bruin, 2021; Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Forte, Favieri, Tambelli, & Casagrande, 2020; Gómez-Salgado, Andrés-Villas, Domínguez-Salas, Díaz-Milanés, & Ruiz-Frutos, 2020; González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020; Ko, Yen, Yen, & Yang, 2006; Liu et al., 2012; Madani, Boutebal, & Bryant, 2020; Mazza et al., 2020; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Qiu et al., 2020; D. L. Reynolds et al., 2008; Rossi et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Zhang, Wang, Rauch, & Wei, 2020). The selection process is summarized in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram (Figure 1).

Study characteristics

Key characteristics of the 20 included studies are presented in Table 1. The total number of participants in all included studies was 106,553. Study sample sizes ranged from 151 to 52,730, with a median of 1,057 (interquartile range [IQR], 524.5–3,830) participants. Complete ranges were reported in 12 studies (Ahmed et al., 2020; Becerra-García et al., 2020; Bruine de Bruin, 2021; Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Forte et al., 2020; González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020; Madani et al., 2020; Mazza et al., 2020; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020), which spanned participants 6 to 100 years old. The lowest maximum age for a study was 59 (Wang et al., 2020). Of studies that subdivided participants into age groups, one included a group for participants 75 years and older (Smith et al., 2020), comprising 4.5% of that study’s total sample; one included a group for participants 70 and older, comprising 12.2% of that study’s total sample (Bruine de Bruin et al., 2021). A more common group cutoff was 51 years and older, comprising 8.8% of (Ahmed et al., 2020); 10.3% of (Forte et al., 2020); 11.1% of Ko et al., 2006. Additionally, (Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020) included a group for participants 61 years and older (8.0% of total sample) and (Madani et al., 2020) included a group of 55 and older (2.4% of total sample). Remaining studies either did not report group totals or analyzed age as a continuous variable. Total means and standard deviations of age were reported in 12 studies (Ahmed et al., 2020; Becerra-García et al., 2020; Bruine de Bruin, 2020; Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Forte et al., 2020; Gómez-Salgado et al., 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020; Mazza et al., 2020; Reynolds et al., 2008; Rossi et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020), of which the combined mean (SD) was 34.3 (11.7) years.

A total of seven studies (Ahmed et al., 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020; Ko et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2012; Qiu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020) were conducted in Asia (35%), nine studies (Becerra-García et al., 2020; Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Forte et al., 2020; Gómez-Salgado et al., 2020; González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Mazza et al., 2020; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2020) in Europe (45%), three studies (Bruine de Bruin, 2020; Reynolds et al., 2008; Tull et al., 2020;) in North America (15%), and one study (Madani et al., 2020) in Africa (5%). The majority (17; 85%) of the studies covered the COVID-19 pandemic, while three (15%) studies (Ko et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2012; Reynolds et al., 2008) analyzed the effects of SARS (one each from Canada, Taiwan, and China).

Collection dates for studies of the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic ranged from January 31, 2020, to April 26, 2020, studying symptoms during quarantine, at the time of data collection. Of the three studies of SARS, two studies assessed psychological symptoms 1–5 months following quarantine (Ko et al., 2006; Reynolds et al., 2008), and one study assessed post-traumatic stress symptoms 3 years following quarantine (Liu et al., 2012).

Risk of bias assessment

No studies were scored to be at high risk of bias, while 14 of 20 (70%) (Ahmed et al., 2020; Becerra-García et al., 2020; Forte et al., 2020; Gómez-Salgado et al., 2020; González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020; Madani et al., 2020; Mazza et al., 2020; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Qiu et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020) studies were at medium risk of bias, and six (30%) (Bruine de Bruin, 2020; Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Ko et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2012; Reynolds et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2020) studies were at low risk of bias. Negative values were most often due to lack of randomization in participant selection and consequent lack of representation of the population. All of the studies with moderate risk of bias used an open-web format for participant recruitment, distributing links through social media, various web platforms, and snowball recruitment by encouraging participants to share the survey link with others. Studies with low risk of bias included all three studies of the effects of SARS and three studies of the effects of COVID-19.

Psychological assessment tools

The studies used a total of 27 different assessment tools (Table 2). Two tools measured multiple outcomes: Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21) and Symptom Assessment 45 (Depression and Anxiety). In total, six (22.2%) of the scales assessed anxiety, five (18.5%) assessed depression, four (14.8%) assessed general wellbeing, two (7.4%) assessed sleep, one (3.7%) assessed loneliness, one assessed social support, and one assessed economic strain. The remainder of the scale outcomes represented various types of stress: four (14.8%) assessed distress, three (11.1%) assessed post-traumatic stress, two (7.4%) assessed stress generally, and one scale (3.7%) measured the diagnostic criteria for adjustment disorder. The most commonly used scales were the DASS-21 (Mazza et al., 2020; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020) and the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) (Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Forte et al., 2020; Reynolds et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2020), utilized in four (20%) studies each. Two studies created novel scales specific to distress due to COVID-19: one scale was used in an Algerian population (Madani et al., 2020), and the other scale, the COVID Peritraumatic Distress Index (CDPI), was used in its original study (Qiu et al., 2020) in a Chinese population and validated in an Italian population in one additional study (Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020). All reported odds ratios compare younger age groups relative to older age groups.

Age, stress, and post-traumatic stress

Of the included studies, nine studies (Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Gómez-Salgado et al., 2020; Mazza et al., 2020; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Qiu et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020) reported values for measures of peritraumatic stress, six studies (Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Forte et al., 2020; Gonzalez-Sanguino et al., 2020; Reynolds et al., 2008; Rossi et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020) reported values of post-traumatic stress, and one study (Rossi et al., 2020) reported values of criteria for adjustment disorder. For all scales in this section, higher values correspond to higher distress. Of those, logistic regression correlation coefficients or odds ratios were reported in five studies that measured peritraumatic symptoms (Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Gómez-Salgado et al., 2020; Madani et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020), two studies that measured post-traumatic stress symptoms (Forte et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020), and one study that measured adjustment disorder symptoms (Rossi et al., 2020) (Figure 2). Odds of peritraumatic distress were significantly higher in younger participants than in older participants in four out of the five studies. Odds of post-traumatic stress were also significantly higher in younger participants, ranging from 1.46 to 1.71 [95% CI, 1.14 to 2.42] relative to older participants. Odds of adjustment disorder were not found to be significantly different by age: 1.05 [95% CI, 0.75 to 1.47]. A total of three studies (Madani et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020) reported linear regression coefficients for peritraumatic stress and three studies (González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Reynolds et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2020) reported linear regression coefficients for posttraumatic stress (Supplement 3). Peritraumatic stress, assessed via the DASS-21 was found to be significantly negatively correlated with age overall in one study (Tull et al., 2020) (regression coefficient [B] = −0.14 [95% CI, −0.224 to −0.056]) and in participants 30.8–40.2 years old compared to participants 49.6–59 years old (B = 0.03, t = −0.15, p < .05) in the second study (Wang et al., 2020). Peritraumatic stress was assessed with a novel, self-designed scale of the effects of COVID-19 in one study (Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020), which also found a negative correlation of stress scores with increasing age (B = −0.081 [95% CI, −0.117 to 0.001]). The two studies (Reynolds et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2020) that assessed post-traumatic stress with the IES-R found no significant correlation of symptoms with age, and the study (González-Sanguino et al., 2020) that assessed post-traumatic stress with the Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist (PCLC-2) found significantly higher scores in the 18–39 year old age group compared to the 60–80-year-old age group (β = −0.291, p < .001) but not compared to the 40–59 year old age group (β = 0.054, p > .05). Additionally, the study that designed and piloted the CPDI (Qiu et al., 2020) reported an average score (M[SD]) of 14.83(13.41) in participants <18 years old, 27.76 (15.69) in participants 18–30 years old, and 27.49 (24.22) in participants >60 years old.

Age and depression

Of the included studies, 13 studies (Ahmed et al., 2020; Becerra-García et al., 2020; Bruine de Bruin, 2020; González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020; Ko et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2012; Mazza et al., 2020; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020) reported values for measures of depression, comprising 45,619 participants total. For all scales of depression, higher values correspond to higher depressive symptoms. Logistic regression correlation coefficients or odds ratios of depression in younger participants relative to older participants were reported in seven studies (Bruine de Bruin, 2020; Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020; Liu et al., 2012; Mazza et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2020). Odds of depression were higher in younger participants in all except one group comparison (participants 36–45 years old relative to participants greater than 46 years old) in one study (0.71 [95% CI, 0.20 to 2.52] (Liu et al., 2012). Additionally, odds ratio confidence intervals crossed 1.00 in comparisons of odds of depression in participants 18–24 years old with participants 25–34 years old (1.10 [95% CI, 0.58 to 2.08]) and 35–44 years old (1.45 [95% CI, 0.69 to 3.03]) in a second study (Smith et al., 2020), and in the overall comparison (1.01 [95% CI, 0.99 to 1.02]) in a third study (Mazza et al., 2020). Linear regression coefficients for the correlation of depression scores with age were reported in three studies (González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). Of those, one study (Wang et al., 2020) identified that depression scores did not significantly correlate with age, while the other two reported a significant (p < .001) negative correlation of depression scores with increased age. A total of two studies reported chi-squared test results of categorical symptoms severity only, of which one study (Ahmed et al., 2020) found a significant difference in depression scores between age groups (χ2 = 38.830, p < .001, effect size = 0.190), with higher scores in the 21–30 year old age group, while the other study (Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020) found no significant differences in scores between age groups (χ2 = 9.93, p = .270, effect size = 0.071). One study (Becerra-García et al., 2020) found no significant difference in depression scores between participants aged 18–35 years old and >36 years old with a two-tailed two-sample t-test.

Age and anxiety

Of the included studies, 12 studies (Ahmed et al., 2020; Becerra-García et al., 2020; Bruine de Bruin, 2020; Forte et al., 2020; González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020; Mazza et al., 2020; Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020) reported values for measures of anxiety, comprising 46,290 participants total. Higher scores corresponded to higher levels of anxiety in all reported scales. Logistic regression correlations or odds ratios of anxiety in younger participants relative to older participants were reported in five studies (Forte et al., 2020; Huang & Zhao, 2020; Mazza et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2020). Odds of anxiety were higher in younger participants in all comparisons, ranging from 1.03 to 12.50. Similar to the trend in odds of depression, odds ratio confidence intervals crossed 1.00 in comparisons of anxiety in participants 18–24 years old with participants 25–34 years old (1.20 [95% CI, 0.67 to 2.16]) and 35–44 years old (1.85 [95% CI, 0.95 to 3.60]) in one additional study (Smith et al., 2020), but odds of anxiety in participants 18–24 increased with older comparison groups. Linear regression coefficients for the correlation of anxiety scores with age were reported in three studies (González-Sanguino et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). Of those, one study (González-Sanguino et al., 2020) found significantly increased anxiety scores in participants 18–39 years old relative to participants 40–50 years old (β = −0.215, p < .001) and 60–80 years old (β = −0.777, p < .001), while the other two did not find a significant correlation of anxiety and age. A total of two studies reported chi-squared test results of categorical symptoms severity only, of which one study (Ahmed et al., 2020) found a significant difference in depression scores between age groups (χ2 = 39.484, p < .001, effect size =0.192), with higher scores in the 21–30 year old age group. The other study (Ozamiz-Etxebarria et al., 2020) also found a significant difference in depression scores between age groups (χ2 = 19.42, p = .013, effect size = 0.100), with lowest scores in participants >61 years old. One study (Becerra-García et al., 2020) found a significant difference in depression scores between participants aged 18–35 years old and >36 years old with a two-tailed two-sample t-test (t = 2.78; p = .006; d = 0.45).

Age, well-being, and additional measures

Though studies assessing stress, anxiety, and depression were the most common, three studies (Ahmed et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020) used three different scales to measure general psychological well-being. Two studies (Huang & Zhao, 2020; Rossi et al., 2020) utilized two different scales to assess sleep disruptions. Additionally, one study (Tull et al., 2020) assessed loneliness, social support, and economic strain. One study (Tull et al., 2020) reported the odds of low scores on the Warwick Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (WEMWBS), corresponding to lower mental wellbeing, in participants 18–24 years old compared to six older age groups and found significantly higher odds of low scores relative to participants aged 45–54 (OR = 3.23 [95% CI, 1.23 to 8.43]), 55–65 (OR = 5.26 [95% CI, 1.66 to 16.67]), 65–75 (OR = 33.33 [95% CI, 5.56 to 200.02]), and >75 (OR = 12.50 [95% CI, 1.96, 79.68]). WEMWBS scores were found to differ significantly by age group in another study (Ahmed et al., 2020), in which the proportion of scores in the lower range decreased with age and the proportion of scores in the higher range increased with age, while scores in the middle range were similar. In one study (Zhang et al., 2020), higher scores on the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), corresponding to greater satisfaction, were found to be significantly correlated with increased age (OR = 1.02 [95% CI, 1.01 to 1.03]), while no correlation was found on the Short Form 12 (SF12) Mental Composite Score (OR = 1.03 [95% CI, 0.93 to 1.14]). No correlation with age was found for either the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) (Huang & Zhao, 2020) or the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) (Rossi et al., 2020). The study (Tull et al., 2020) utilizing the Family Economic Strain Scale (FESS), UCLA Loneliness Scale (ULS), and the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) found that loneliness significantly decreased with age (B = −0.01 [95% CI, −0.016 to 0.004], p = .03), but it found no correlation in the other two scales.

Discussion

This review of 20 studies that assessed the differences in psychological response to household containment measures by age found that metrics of stress, post-traumatic stress, depression, anxiety, and general mental well-being show better outcomes in older participants in almost all studies. This is consistent with studies showing lower stress response and lower negative emotions with age in pre-pandemic studies (Carstensen et al., 2000; Neubauer, Smyth, & Sliwinski, 2019). The most recent such study is the National Comorbidity Survey (NCS) replication study, which was based on the DSM-IV and used the WHO Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI) (Kessler, Andrews, Mroczek, Ustun, & Wittchen, 1998; Kessler et al., 2004). The findings described significantly lower 12-month and 30-day prevalence of major depressive episodes in the oldest age group (65+, 2.6%, and 1.0%, respectively) relative to all younger age groups (18–34, 35–49, 50–64; 7.7–10.4% and 3.0–3.7%, respectively; prevalence ratios of 3.0–4.0 and 3.0–3.7, respectively) (Kessler et al., 2010). This is a larger difference in prevalence between age groups than in the majority of our reviewed studies, suggesting a shrinking of the gap in depression between in older and younger adults, although prevalence estimates have varied in smaller epidemiological assessments and the data is limited to the United States (Blazer, 2003). Earlier national prevalence studies include the Epidemiological Catchment Area Study (ECA), which took place between 1980 and 1984 and used the DSM-III-based NIMH Diagnostic Interview Schedule (Robins, Helzer, Croughan, & Ratcliff, 1981) to assess rates of substance use and nonsubstance psychiatric disorders in a sample of participants aged 18 and older across five national centers (Regier et al., 1988). The ECA found the total 1-month prevalence of all anxiety disorders was 5.5% in participants aged 65 years and older, compared to 6.6% in participants 45–64 years old (prevalence ratio of 1.2), 8.3% in participants 25–44 years old (prevalence ratio of 1.5), and 7.7% (prevalence ratio of 1.4) in participants 18–24 years old. Prevalence distribution reported by the ECA is similar to the findings in our reviewed studies, although individual study findings are split in whether the gap in prevalence between ages has increased or decreased.

Only one study in our review found higher levels of distress among older individuals (participants aged 60 and above) relative to younger individuals (participants below 18 years of age), though scores were comparable to the young adult population (18–30 years of age) (Qiu et al., 2020). This study was the first to use the novel CPDI, which is a broad metric that assesses anxiety, depression, specific phobias, cognitive change, avoidance and compulsive behavior, physical symptoms, and loss of social functioning. Interestingly, a study using the same scale in an Italian population found lower distress in participants aged 51–60 and even lower distress in participants aged 61–71 (Costantini & Mazzotti, 2020). In this study, CPDI results had high concordance with a more established measure of post-traumatic stress symptoms, the IES-R, as 74% of participants had CPDI and IES-R scores either both in the low range (65%) or high range (9%). Discordant values were present in 26% of participants, with 21% having high CPDI scores and low IES-R scores and 4% having low CPDI scores and high IES-R scores. This suggests that the CPDI may be capturing quarantine-specific distress not captured in the IES-R in these participants, but it still does not explain the difference in trends with age compared to the original study. The rest of the assessed studies found either no correlation or increased symptomatology in younger participants.

It is noteworthy that the majority of the measures used in the assessed studies were developed for use in younger adults. Epidemiologic tracking of older adults’ mental health has been called into question given (1) older adults are prone to minimize or deny psychological symptoms, (2) the majority of studies focus on community-dwelling adults rather than older adults living in institutions, and (3) several studies suggest differences in the experience or description of experience of mental illness by older adults (Kogan, Edelstein, & McKee, 2000). Each one of these points can be further characterized. For instance, minimization may occur for various reasons, from reluctance to burden families to difficulty distinguishing between symptoms of normal aging (e.g., tiredness, sleep disturbance, appetite changes) and depressive or anxious symptoms. The population of older adults living in institutions includes those living with dementia and/or structural brain disorders, for whom memory difficulties are interwoven with psychiatric symptoms (Skoog, 2011). Moreover, assessments show that older adults, more so than younger adults, may use labels such as “concerns” rather than “worries” or “flat” rather than “unhappy,” likely due to a combination of differences in language choice and physiological symptom presentation (Wuthrich, Johnco, & Wetherell, 2015). Due to these and other concerns, scales have been developed and validated for the specific use in geriatrics. These include but are not limited to the Geriatric Anxiety Inventory (Pachana et al., 2007) and the Geriatric Depression Scale (Yesavage & Sheikh, 1986). Such scales tout not only increased accuracy in older adults, but increased ease of administration due to features such as a “yes or no” format rather than a spectrum of answers (Lyness, 1997). The advantages of such scales should be considered in selection of study measures as well as considered in the development of new scales.

An important consideration in the interpretation of these results is the low representation of older participants in the majority of the studies. Studies with moderate risk of bias recruited patients through online surveys, utilizing open web and snowball recruitment. As this recruitment strategy is internet-based, it limits the enrollment of participants who are less technologically active. This was reflected in the age distributions of the studies, which can be seen in studies that subdivided their sample into subgroups (Table 1). The oldest age group in four of the studies was 50 or 51 years and older, 35 years and older in one study, and 46 years and older in another. Even in studies with a low minimum age for the oldest group, this group had the fewest participants the majority of the time. Furthermore, the recruited older participants may not be typical members of their age group due to the web-based nature of the surveys, biasing toward participants with access to technology and fluency in use of social media platforms. One of the reviewed studies (Bruine de Bruin et al., 2020) utilized postal address to randomly select participants and provided internet-connected tablets to potential participants without internet access, a strategy that has been shown to increase national representation in surveys and successfully accomplishes this goal in the reviewed study (Tourangeau, Conrad, & Couper, 2013). Phone-based surveys utilizing random digit dialing and/or Computer Assisted Telephone Interviewing are another alternative that would be more representative and have been successfully used to capture an older adult population (Vallance et al., 2014; Wolter, Chowdhury, & Kelly, 2009). To increase representation if address or phone number-based recruitment strategies are unsuccessful, administrators at residential communities or nursing homes may be leveraged for study recruitment to address the issue of representation of older adults living in institutions. Further insight necessitates larger sample sizes of older participants for a more in-depth analysis of variations within the heterogenous population of older adults. With better representation, we can begin to evaluate the mechanisms behind the differences observed by age with analyses of potential mediator variables, including but not limited to technology use, generational cultural differences, differences in coping strategies, and unique symptom profiles.

The strengths of this review include the breadth of included countries and populations and strict inclusion of quarantine-related psychological effects in the general population. The trends in correlation with age were consistent throughout the eight different countries represented in the studies, showing cross-cultural consistency. However, this review is limited by its cross-sectional nature. Although several studies attempted to track changes in scores over time, their interpretation was limited by high rates of participant attrition and thus was not systematically evaluated. Longitudinal data, as it becomes available, will become key for tracking the evolution of the response to the pandemic across age groups. This would allow for tracking of changes in mental health as a response to quarantine, which cannot be precisely estimated by the included cross-sectional studies. Results from several of the studies suggest that though the net differences between younger and older participants have the same directionality, the gap between the populations could be narrower than in pre-pandemic studies. However, the limited number of studies representative of their country’s population and the large number of assessed countries makes such comparisons difficult. Larger studies that utilize random and stratified sampling recruitment techniques would allow for more informative comparison to similar studies conducted before the pandemic in order to compare differences in prevalence between age groups. Although we emphasize the need for more specific screening tools, this must be balanced with consideration of the potential insights that could be gained by comparing scores on the same scale at different time periods. Comparisons across scores on the same scale in similarly divided age groups would allow for more robust analysis that explore potential mediating factors, which we were not able to perform in this review due to high interstudy heterogeneity. Moreover, this review evaluates only studies published early in the COVID-19 pandemic, assessing distress shortly after the beginning of quarantine in addition to several studies of past respiratory outbreaks. Therefore, the distribution of symptoms by age may change as the stressor becomes chronic and reactions to quarantine shift. Analysis was also limited by the extent of documentation of the length of quarantine. Although each study was performed after at least 24 hours of quarantine, the number of quarantine days prior to assessment varied between and within studies.

Clinical experience shows that older patients are still very affected by quarantine, and if traditional screening methods are not capturing the full extent of distress, clinicians need to be vigilant in monitoring their patients’ mental health. This may be particularly true of patients with existing psychiatric illnesses or patients living in institutions, as this review only assesses the general non-institutionalized population. However, this review suggests that similar to epidemiologic studies conducted before COVID-19, older adults appear to be coping better than younger adults. This warrants continued exploration of mediating factors, including wisdom and socioemotional selectivity, which could be leveraged for mental health interventions for those experiencing more severe symptoms (Blazer & Hybels, 2005). One potential way to increase the reliability of screening tests in older patients and provide more insight into their well-being is to include measures specific for use in older adults. Moreover, as suggested by the first results of the CPDI, tracking of symptoms specific to the COVID-19 pandemic, including lack of physical touch, lack of interaction with family, fear of infection, hospitalization, and death, and a fear of missing out on active years of life may be helpful in tracking the psychological well-being of older adults. Regardless of scores on screening tests, clinicians must carefully assess how their patients are adjusting to the changes posed by the pandemic and encourage adherence to guidance to prevent COVID-19, as well as to effectively cope with stress to prevent psychiatric decline.
