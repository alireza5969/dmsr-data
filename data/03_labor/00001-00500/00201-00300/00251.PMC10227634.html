Impact of Antiretroviral Therapy on Cancer Treatment Outcomes among People Living with HIV in Low- and Middle-Income Countries: a Systematic Review

Abstract

Purpose of Review

The relationship between antiretroviral therapy (ART) and cancer treatment outcomes among people living with HIV (PLWH) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is complex and poorly understood for many cancers. We aimed to summarize existing evidence from LMICs regarding the benefit of ART on cancer treatment–related outcomes.

Recent Findings

We included twelve observational studies that reported associations between ART status and cancer treatment outcomes among HIV-positive patients in LMICs. Most confirmed ART was associated with improved cancer treatment outcomes. Heterogeneity in cancers under study, outcome measurement, categorization of ART status, and reporting of HIV-related immune function made formal comparison between studies untenable.

Summary

Where evaluated, ART generally has a positive effect on cancer outcomes in people with HIV in LMICs. However, there remains a substantial gap in the literature regarding the impact of ART on treatment outcomes for most cancer types. Future research should focus on the optimal timing and integration of ART and cancer treatment for PLWH with strategies applicable to constrained-resource settings.

Background

Among people living with HIV (PLWH), malignancies in the early decades of the HIV pandemic were characterized by high incidence of AIDS-defining malignancies (ADMs): Kaposi’s sarcoma (KS), non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL), and cervical cancer [1, 2]. More recently, the epidemiology of malignancies among PLWH has shifted due to expanding antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage and increasing life expectancy. Rates of ADMs have declined somewhat, but the burden of many non-AIDS-defining malignancies (NADMs) associated with HIV such as lung, anal, head and neck, and liver cancers, and other lymphoma subtypes, is increasing [1–4]. Cancer is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among PLWH [5], with NADMs accounting for a growing proportion of cancer-related disease and death [6].

Health care disparities based on HIV status have been noted in many settings [7–9]. Some evidence from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) indicates that PLWH with certain cancers may have worse treatment outcomes, including higher risk of toxicity and other treatment-related complications, and worse survival compared with their HIV-negative counterparts [6, 10–14]. Other studies have shown no disparities [15]. PLWH often present with some cancers at a younger age [12, 16–18] and more advanced stage [6, 13, 17, 19]. Furthermore, some cancers have been reported to have a more rapid progression in PLWH [12, 13]. This may be related to immune function; commencing cytotoxic chemotherapy among PLWH with low CD4 counts is associated with increased complications and poorer clinical outcomes [12]. Finally, treatment disparities remain between HIV-positive and HIV-negative populations [2, 4, 20], and many PLWH do not receive appropriate cancer therapy [2].

Advances in immunotherapy are changing the clinical management, and improving treatment success, of many cancers [5, 21–25]. There is increasing evidence that reversing immune exhaustion can be critical in successful cancer treatment, by enabling activation of the immune system toward the malignancy [26–28]. Among PLWH, ART decreases immune exhaustion over time, leading to improvement in cell-mediated immunity [29, 30]. Indeed, prognosis for some cancers has improved as access to ART has expanded and PLWH initiate ART earlier [31–34], but evidence on precise mechanisms and impact of ART on treatment outcomes is lacking. While ART is protective against development of, and improves treatment outcomes for, ADMs, the relationship between ART and NADMs is more complex and less well understood [35, 36]. Many observational studies examining cancer treatment outcomes among PLWH either do not report or fail to stratify by ART status and immune function of participants [6, 11]. In addition, PLWH are often excluded from clinical trials examining the efficacy of new cancer treatments [6, 37, 38]. The objective of this review is to summarize published evidence regarding the role of antiretroviral therapy (ART) in cancer-related outcomes among people living with HIV (PLWH) who have cancer in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC). We focus on studies comparing treatment-related outcomes, in LMIC, because limited ART and cancer therapy choices may mean reliance on more cytotoxic treatments. Current NCCN guidelines on treating PLWH with cancer lack ART-related guidance for limited-resource settings [39]. Questions about timing of initiation of ART in PLWH and cancer are particularly important when considering implementation in LMICs.

Methods

Search Strategy

We conducted a systematic search of the PubMed, MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library electronic databases using the search terms: “(ART OR HAART OR ARVs OR antiretroviral OR “antiretroviral therapy”) AND (cancer OR lymphoma OR sarcoma OR tumor OR malignancy) AND (treatment OR chemotherapy OR radiation OR surgery) AND (“HIV-infected” OR “HIV-positive” OR PLWH OR PWH).” We limited our search to English language manuscripts published in 2009 or later.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were included if they were conducted in LMICs [40] and evaluated at least 10 PLWH with cancer. The WHO treatment guidelines for HIV changed in 2009 to recommend ART for PLWH with CD4<350 cells/μL, an increase from the prior CD4<200 cells/μL recommendation [41]. We therefore excluded studies if all data collection occurred prior to 2009 to limit possible bias in availability and indication of ART. We included randomized and single-arm clinical trials, retrospective studies, and prospective observational studies. Case reports, case series with <10 cases, and systematic reviews were excluded. Publications reporting data from multiple countries were included only if outcomes were stratifiable to exclude high-income countries. If HIV-negative participants were included, the ability to stratify results by HIV status was necessary for inclusion. Publications reporting only premalignant lesions were excluded. The comparison of interest was cancer treatment among HIV-positive participants on ART compared with participants not on ART. Publications that did not report ART status, that failed to report outcomes by ART status, or where all participants received ART were excluded unless ART was stratified by timing of initiation. The primary outcomes of interest were mortality or survival. Secondary outcomes of interest were adverse events, recurrence, treatment choices, treatment interruptions, and complications. Manuscripts focusing solely on baseline characteristics of cancer, such as stage and type, or prevalence or incidence, were excluded.

Study Selection

Following database searches, full citations were downloaded to EndNote (Version X9). Duplicates were removed, and a single reviewer (MAM) conducted title and abstract screening based on predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full texts of the remaining manuscripts were obtained for further evaluation, and each full text manuscript was screened by two reviewers working independently (MAM and RABI or TSU). Disagreements about eligibility of manuscripts during full text review were discussed and resolved as a team.

Analysis

Given the heterogeneity of cancers, study design, and categorization of exposures and outcomes, we performed a narrative analysis by cancer type, summarizing results from key comparisons in each study and synthesizing conclusions.

Results and Discussion

Characteristics of the Included Studies

The database search yielded 5386 results, with 3366 manuscripts remaining after removal of duplicates. Based on title screening, 2438 were excluded, with a further 810 excluded based on abstract screening, leaving 118 for full text review (Fig. 1). Twelve manuscripts reported cancer treatment outcomes associated with ART status and fit other inclusion criteria. All were observational studies, of which nine were retrospective and three prospective. Most studies addressed ADM (Table 1), with two manuscripts reporting findings from Kaposi’s sarcoma [42], six reporting findings from lymphomas [43•–48], one reporting cervical cancer outcomes [49], one on non-small cell lung cancer [50], and two on multiple cancer types [51, 52]. All the included studies were published in 2016 or later and described treatment in China (n=3), South Africa (n=3), Thailand (n=2), Brazil, Malawi (n=2), and Peru. Baseline characteristics of study participants, treatments and outcomes under study, recruitment of participants, and analytic methods varied widely across studies. The way ART status groups were defined differed across studies, with some studies comparing groups receiving versus never receiving ART during cancer treatment and others comparing groups where all participants received ART during cancer treatment, but stratified results by pre-treatment or pre-diagnosis ART status. Characteristics of HIV disease status such as viral load (VL) and CD4 count also varied across studies and were reported by ART status in only 3 of 12 studies (Table 2).

Kaposi’s Sarcoma

Studies

Two studies from South Africa examined KS outcomes associated with ART status [33, 42]. A retrospective study measured survival among 42 AIDS-KS patients evaluated over a 6-month period by a multidisciplinary KS team of infectious disease physicians, oncologists, and dermatologists [42]. Sixty-seven percent were on ART before KS diagnosis and 31% initiated ART after KS diagnosis. Median CD4 count was 147 cells/μL. Ninety percent of patients had advanced risk disease by AIDS Clinical Trials Group criteria. Twenty-two patients (52%) received ART plus chemotherapy, and twenty (48%) received ART alone, of whom 10 responded. Two-year overall survival with ART alone was 50%. Differences in outcome were attributable to indications for not receiving cancer therapy, with death and loss to follow-up largely occurring among the 10 patients with advanced disease determined to be poor candidates for chemotherapy. Overall survival (OS) was best among patients whose KS responded well to ART alone (2-year OS 90%). Among patients with suboptimal KS response to ART alone, those who received oncologic treatment in addition to ART had better 2-year OS compared to those who did not (71% vs. 10%, p<0.001). Patients with T1 KS (n=36) who received chemotherapy had substantially better OS than those that did not (incidence rate ratio (IRR) 0.18, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.04–0.61).

A second retrospective study examined mortality among 375 HIV-positive KS patients in an oncology clinic across early (2004–2008) and later (2009–2012) periods of ART access [33]. A higher proportion of patients diagnosed with KS in the later period initiated ART prior to KS diagnosis (72.7% vs. 53.8%), although median CD4 count for the later period was only slightly higher (250 vs. 233 cells/μL). The majority of patients in both periods received ART after KS diagnosis, although a smaller proportion of patients in the later period did not receive ART either pre- or post-KS diagnosis (2.5% vs. 12.1%). Mortality was lower among KS patients in the later period compared to the early period (hazard ratio (HR): 0.37; 95% CI: 0.19–0.73), and for those receiving chemotherapy (HR: 0.3; 95% CI: 0.15–0.61). These findings were independent of CD4 count, KS stage, ART status at KS diagnosis, and receipt of radiation in multivariable analyses. There were no associations between mortality and ART status at KS diagnosis.

Conclusions and Unanswered Questions

The role of ART in KS treatment among PLWH is well established [37•, 53–55] and confirmed in these studies. ART alone may be sufficient to treat KS in slowly progressive or limited disease, although combining ART with chemotherapy is recommended for rapidly progressive or advanced disease [53–55]. Key findings from this review include evidence of high overall survival in patients provided ART alone, improved survival in patients with advanced KS who received chemotherapy in addition to ART [42], and lower mortality from KS as ART access expanded in South Africa [33], an epidemiologic shift that has been well documented in South Africa and elsewhere [55, 56]. Unsurprisingly, we found no studies examining KS outcomes in patients receiving versus not receiving ART, since ART is now a recommended part of KS treatment. Current KS clinical trials focus on new immune-based therapies such as checkpoint inhibitors and pomalidomide [57] and optimizing KS treatment in resource-limited settings, particularly for patients with advanced disease [37•, 53] employing concurrent ART [58]. Future research on integration of HIV and oncology care and management of ART in patients with KS is required. Furthermore, studies focused on diagnosis and management of concurrent Kaposi sarcoma herpesvirus (KSHV)–associated diseases such as primary effusion lymphoma, KSHV-associated multicentric Castleman disease, and KSHV-associated inflammatory cytokine syndrome may be needed to address care for patients with inflammatory symptoms that are often considered too sick to treat and often have a poor prognosis.

Lymphomas

Studies

Six studies reporting associations between ART and outcomes related to lymphoma treatment focused mostly on Burkitt lymphoma (BL) and diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL), both AIDS-defining lymphomas. A retrospective study in Brazil evaluated survival among 54 PLWH diagnosed with BL [45]. Forty-one percent were receiving ART prior to BL diagnosis, and those not previously taking ART were initiated within the first week of BL treatment. Five patients died prior to cancer treatment initiation, and the remaining 49 received cancer therapy. Complete response occurred in 44.9% and 4-year OS was 39.8% among those who received cancer treatment. Univariable analysis showed no differences in OS or complete response by CD4 count, VL, or ART status prior to diagnosis.

A retrospective study in South Africa reported associations between survival and ART status at diagnosis and during treatment of BL and unclassifiable B cell lymphomas (features intermediate between BL and DLBCL (BL/DLBCL)) [46]. Among 35 patients, 24 were diagnosed with BL, and 11 with BL/DLBCL. Nine were on ART at cancer diagnosis (7 with undetectable VL), 22 started ART after lymphoma diagnosis, and 4 were not started on ART because they were critically ill and curative-intent lymphoma treatment was prioritized over ART with the goal of starting ART after initially controlling lymphoma. ART was associated with better overall survival (HR for death: 0.19; p=0.008), but ART status at diagnosis was not associated with differences in survival.

A prospective study in Malawi reported OS among a cohort of 35 BL patients, of whom 15 were PLWH [48]. Among these, 8 were receiving ART at BL diagnosis, and 10 had suppressed VL. Median CD4 count was 130 cell/μL. There were no differences in OS by ART status at BL diagnosis (HR: 1.26; 95% CI: 0.31–5.10).

A retrospective study in China reported on risk of death in a cohort of 15 BL patients and 17 diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) patients with HIV [47]. Among DLBCL patients, 35% were on ART at diagnosis, and 65% received it after diagnosis and before chemotherapy; among BL patients, 27% were on ART at diagnosis, and 73% initiated ART after diagnosis and before chemotherapy. Initiation of ART prior to diagnosis versus just prior to chemotherapy was not significantly associated with risk of death.

A prospective study in Malawi reported mortality in a cohort of 86 newly diagnosed DLBCL patients, of whom 51 were PLWH and 35 were HIV-negative at DLBCL diagnosis [44•]. Of the 51 PLWH, 67% had been on ART for at least a month prior to DLBCL diagnosis, and the remainder initiated ART concurrent with chemotherapy. Among PLWH, having been on ART at DLBCL diagnosis was associated with worse mortality (HR: 2.35; 95% CI: 1.01–5.47). Controlling for International Prognostic Index strengthened this association (AHR: 4.8; 95% CI: 1.7–14.2).

A retrospective study from Peru explored survival associated with pre-diagnosis ART status among non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) patients [43•]. Of 47 patients, 34 were ART-naïve at NHL diagnosis, and 13 had been receiving ART for at least 3 months prior to NHL diagnosis. Median OS among ART-naïve patients was 57.1 months, compared to 21.1 months among ART-exposed patients (p=0.0176). Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that overall survival was significantly lower among ART-exposed patients (p=0.045).

Conclusions and Unanswered Questions

Concurrent ART use was nearly universal, although the number of patients on ART at diagnoses ranged from 35 to 75%, and nearly all ART-naïve patients initiated ART with chemotherapy. All five studies reported survival and compared patients who were ART-naïve at cancer diagnosis to patients who had initiated ART prior to diagnosis. The findings were heterogeneous and appear to vary by histology. Two of the five studies found that patients who were ART-naïve at lymphoma diagnosis (DLBCL, NHL) had significantly better survival than patients already on ART at diagnosis [43•, 44•]. These findings are similar to a prior retrospective study from the USA, which measured a 2-fold higher risk of death among patients on ART at lymphoma diagnosis compared with those not taking ART (AHR: 2.2; 95% CI: 1.53–3.20) [59], and suggest that DLBCL occurring in persons already on ART may be less immunologically responsive and more treatment-resistant. Conversely, the three remaining studies found that ART status prior to lymphoma diagnosis (BL, DLBCL) was not associated with survival [45–47]. Prior ART status may be less important for BL, a disease with rapid proliferation due to c-myc translocation. The three studies that reported no association between ART and lymphoma survival did not report CD4 count or VL for the ART status comparison groups [45–47]. It is possible that subpar ART adherence and unmeasured immune factors may have confounded comparisons between ART users and nonusers.

The findings from all five included studies differ from two studies conducted in LMICs prior to 2009, which compared NHL survival across PLWH who received ART during NHL treatment with those who did not [60,61]. Both of these studies found several-fold increases in risk of death among PLWH not receiving ART during lymphoma treatment. This evidence suggests that as with KS, ART is an essential component of successful treatment for lymphoma in PLWH, although timing of ART initiation may impact prognosis. Notably, most studies do not include duration of ART prior to NHL diagnosis and therefore do not consider whether the tumor presented as unmasking immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), which may also have implications for immunoresponsiveness and mortality [62]. Further research is necessary to elucidate the relationship between ART and lymphoma treatment and optimize combination treatment strategies for different lymphoma types, with potential need for additional immune-based treatment for HIV-associated DLBCL.

Other Cancers

Studies

The remaining four studies, one on cervical cancer, one on lung cancer, and two including multiple cancer types [49–52], all reported better outcomes among PLWH on ART compared to those not using ART. However, comparisons between manuscripts were limited due to the wide range of cancers under study. A retrospective study from Thailand reported survival among patients receiving treatment for cervical cancer between 2008 and 2016 and investigated expression of programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) in relation to HIV status, ART status, and survival [49]. Overexpression of PD-L1 has been identified as a prognostic indicator of poor survival in several types of cancer. Cervical cancer patients were divided into three groups: 23 with HIV and at least 1 year of ART at cancer diagnosis (ART-exposed), 25 with HIV and no ART exposure prior to cervical cancer diagnosis (ART-untreated), and 123 HIV-negative patients. PLWH were younger than HIV-seronegative women. Women on ART had earlier stage tumors than HIV-negative women, suggesting the possibility of increased access to health care for women receiving ART. Interestingly, PD-L1 immunopositivity was more common among both ART-unexposed and HIV-negative patients compared to ART-exposed patients (84 vs. 21.6%; p<0.001 and 83.7 vs. 21.6%; p<0.001, respectively). ART-treated patients had longer recurrence-free and cancer-specific survival than ART-untreated and HIV-negative patients, across a range of PD-L1 categories. However, differences were not significant in multivariable analysis considering other prognostic factors including stage, PD-L1 expression, and genomic factors.

A retrospective study conducted in China examined postoperative complications and cancer recurrence among 33 patients with HIV and stage I–IIIA non-small cell lung cancer undergoing surgical resection [50]. Thirteen patients were ART-naïve at cancer diagnosis. Twenty had initiated ART prior to cancer diagnosis, of whom 10 had detectable viral loads (VL). The mean CD4 count in the cohort was 343 cells/μL (± 149). Stage was the most important prognostic factor, and ART initiation prior to cancer diagnosis was not associated with recurrence in either univariable or multivariable analysis. Sixty-seven percent of patients developed postoperative complications, most commonly pneumonia. ART initiation prior to diagnosis was associated with lower risk of postoperative complications in univariable, but not multivariable, analysis. Detectable VL was identified as a risk factor for postoperative complications in multivariable analysis (p=0.049), and increased CD4 count was associated with decreased risk of complications (p=0.045).

A prospective study from Thailand reported immunologic characteristics during and after radiation therapy among patients with carcinomas or lymphoma [51]. Of 90 patients, 79 were on ART prior to radiation. Of those, 59 had undetectable VL. Radiation was associated with immunosuppression in a higher proportion of patients in the non-ART group compared to the ART group prior to radiation, with 18.9% of the ART group and 45.5% of the non-ART group having >3% reduction in CD4% at radiation therapy completion compared to baseline (p=0.076); 29.7% of the ART group versus 100% of the non-ART group having CD4% reduction >3% at 3 months compared to baseline (p=0.0004); and 26.2% of the ART group versus 66.7% of the non-ART group having reduction of CD4% >3% at 6 months (p=0.062).

A retrospective study conducted in China reported mortality among 149 HIV-seropositive patients with cancer [52]. The study included 50 (34%) ADM and 99 (66%) NADM, most commonly Hodgkin lymphoma and lung, gastric, and liver cancer. Sixty-seven percent of patients had been prescribed ART prior to hospitalization. HIV-positive patients with cancer who were not on ART at hospital admission were at higher risk of death compared to patients who were on ART (AHR: 2.19; 95% CI: 1.84–2.61; p<0.001).

Conclusions and Unanswered Questions

Generally, these studies support a small body of evidence indicating that PLWH using ART may have improved treatment outcomes and decreased treatment complications. Indeed, data is beginning to emerge from sub-Saharan Africa suggesting women with HIV and cervical cancer may have comparable outcomes to the general population. Recent studies from Botswana and Zambia demonstrated that HIV-positive cervical cancer patients receiving ART had similar 2-year overall survival to HIV-negative patients [15] and treatment-related toxicities during standard concurrent chemoradiation [63]. An improved understanding of the impact of ART on the tumor microenvironment, as was noted in the study from Thailand, may provide insights into treatment for cervical cancer and other solid tumors. Future studies are needed to evaluate the effects of ART in other common solid tumors that occur in the setting of HIV in LMIC. Several recent reviews of cancer among PLWH in LMICs have also concluded that further research is needed to measure effectiveness of different treatment methods in the context of differences in immune status (CD4 count) and timing of ART initiation [6, 19, 35].

Conclusion

This review provides important insights into characteristics of PLWH and cancer across a range of LMICs, as well as ART use in people with cancer in these settings. Across cancers studied, it was notable that 35–75% of PLWH were not on ART at time of cancer diagnosis. Heterogeneity in study design and objectives made it difficult to draw broad conclusions about the impact of ART on cancer treatment outcomes. Of the twelve manuscripts included in this review, most found that ART was positively associated with a range of cancer treatment outcomes, including improved survival and decreased treatment complications, supporting the need for integration of ART early in cancer care. In KS and DLBCL, this review provides increasing evidence that cancers developing in people on ART have different clinical course than those occurring in ART-naïve patients.

This review explored the associations between ART and cancer outcomes in the modern ART era. However, very few studies prospectively included ART initiation as an intervention, and most randomized clinical trials were excluded due to uniform ART use. Furthermore, ART status was not the primary exposure of interest for many included studies. ART status was most often included as a binary covariate in univariable or multivariable analyses, with limited information on duration of ART use or HIV-associated immunologic characteristics stratified by ART status. Across studies, most patients presented with moderately suppressed immunity (e.g., CD4 200–350 cells/μL), but only three manuscripts reported CD4 count and VL by ART status category [43•, 49, 51]. Success of ART for HIV treatment is in part defined by immunologic characteristics such as CD4 count [64], and reducing immune exhaustion can improve cancer treatment success [26–28, 65–67]. Many of the included studies relied on retrospective data collected from hospital systems, potentially impacting their ability to obtain measures of immune status and control for confounding due to ART duration, adherence, and initiation for treatment indication. This somewhat limited interpretability of associations between ART and cancer treatment outcomes highlights a need for additional study in this area, particularly prospective and interventional studies that can more effectively evaluate immune status in PLWH. Despite these limitations, this review provides a base of evidence, identifies unmet clinical needs, and raises new scientific questions in relation to ART and cancer care in LMIC.

In summary, this review provides evidence that there remains a need to initiate ART in a sizeable percent of PLWH who present with cancer in LMIC. ART status may matter in optimizing cancer treatment outcomes among PLWH in LMICs for KS and DLBCL and may decrease surgical complications and radiation effects on CD4 cells [68, 69]. Questions remain, however, regarding the impact of timing of ART initiation and immune reconstitution relative to cancer treatment initiation [70]. Further, there are barriers to concurrent treatment of HIV and cancer in many LMICs, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, since clinical care of these conditions is often siloed and comanagement of care can be difficult [70–74]. Because ART improves overall survival in PLWH, and achieving improved immune function curing cancer therapy is likely to further improve cancer treatment outcomes in this population, implementation research to improve integration of HIV and cancer care is crucial.
