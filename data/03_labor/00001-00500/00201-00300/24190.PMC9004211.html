Bone Versus All Soft Tissue Quadriceps Tendon Autografts for Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Purpose:

To examine existing literature on objective and patient-reported outcomes and complications after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) with bone-quadriceps-tendon (B-QT) or soft tissue-quadriceps tendon (S-QT) to further clarify the role of graft type in primary ACLR.

Methods:

In accordance with PRISMA guidelines, PubMed, Embase, and Medline were searched in October 2019 for English-language, human studies of all evidence levels on patients undergoing primary ACLR with B-QT or S-QT autograft.

Results:

24 of 1,381 studies satisfied criteria, with 20 using B-QT (1,534 patients, mean age 29.6 years [range 14 to 59], mean follow-up 41.2 months [range 12 to 120]) and 5 using S-QT (181 patients, mean age 32.4 years [range 15 to 58), mean follow-up 25.5 months [range 12 to 46]). International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) scores were 67.3 to 89.5 with B-QT and 80.4 to 81.6 with S-QT. Lysholm scores were 85.7 to 97.4 with B-QT and 81.6 to 89.2 with S-QT. More B-QT patients demonstrated rotatory laxity on pivot shift compared with S-QT (0% to 39% versus 0%, respectively). The most common complication was graft rupture, and no differences were observed between graft choices (B-QT 0% to 9% versus S-QT 0% to 3.8%).

Conclusions:

The main findings from this review report that more B-QT patients demonstrated postoperative rotatory instability than S-QT patients, and that there are no differences in graft rupture between the 2 graft choices. Although statistical conclusions may not be drawn because of heterogeneity in reporting, it appears that the B-QT group featured much wider major and minor complication profiles.

Level of evidence:

IV, systematic review of level I—IV studies

The quadriceps tendon (QT), historically, represents a less commonly used graft choice for primary anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR); in recent years, it is garnering increased attention.1 More than 200,000 primary ACLRs occur annually in the United States alone, making the procedure one of the most commonly performed in orthopedic surgery,2-4 but reported use of the QT is 1% to 11% among other graft types.5 Many physicians consider bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) or hamstring tendon (HS) autograft to be the gold standard for graft selection in the primary setting.3,6,7

Although less commonly used, QT has been gaining popularity8-14 and has been demonstrated to be a feasible graft choice.9,10,13,15 Prior anatomic studies have found that the QT autograft has not only almost twice the cross-sectional area of BPTB, but also a higher failure load, at 1,075 N compared with 876 N, respectively.16,17 Initial research into the general use of QT grafts has been promising, but further optimization of the techniques using these grafts is needed, from harvesting techniques to postoperative functional and clinical outcomes.8,18 Recent evidence reports that factors such as graft thickness and fixation technique result in no significant differences in QT ACLR outcomes.19,20 Recent registry studies have shown an increased risk of ACLR revision in QT compared with HT and BPTB.21 An important element in establishing a successful outcome for primary ACLR is maximizing graft incorporation into native tissue. Traditionally, graft healing has been achieved by either bone-to-bone or tendon-to-bone healing, depending on whether a graft includes a bone block.22,23 Both bone-quadriceps-tendon (B-QT) and soft tissue-quadriceps-tendon (S-QT) have their advantages and disadvantages. There is evidence that bone-to-bone healing is not only stronger but also faster, healing within 6 weeks as opposed to soft tissue, which can take up to twice as long.24 In contrast, S-QT has been reported with decreased incidence of patellar fractures.12 Thus, the optimal graft of these options remains highly controversial.

The purpose of this systematic review is to examine existing literature on objective patient-reported outcomes and complications after ACLR with B-QT and S-QT to further clarify the role of graft type in primary ACLR. It was hypothesized that S-QT, compared with B-QT, would be associated with improved patient-reported outcomes and fewer complications, but be challenged by less favorable clinical outcomes such as increased graft failure rate.

Methods

Search Strategy

To identify clinical studies addressing the use of B-QT and S-QT autograft for ACLR, 3 databases—PubMed, Ovid (Medline), and Embase—were searched from database inception to September 17, 2019. The search was conducted using the terms “quadriceps tendon,” “anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction,” and “autograft” (Fig 1). Studies pertaining to revision ACLR or multiligament reconstructions were excluded. The PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) statement was used for the reporting of study selection.25

Study Screening

Two reviewers independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts independently and in duplicate (R.J.C., D.d.S.) (Fig 1). If there was a disagreement regarding inclusion or exclusion of a study between reviewers, it was included for title screening stage and abstract screening stage for comprehensiveness. During the full-text screening stage, the reviewers discussed any disagreement with a third author (V.M.), who made the final decision on inclusion or exclusion.

Assessment of Study Eligibility

A priori establishment of the inclusion and exclusion criteria were conducted. The inclusion criteria were studies investigating the use of B-QT or S-QT autograft in ACLR, English-language studies, studies on skeletally mature humans, studies with reported clinical outcomes, studies with level I to IV evidence, and studies with follow-up ≥1 year. Studies involving a non-QT autograft were included so long as they also included a QT autograft comparison group. Exclusion criteria included studies assessing only non-QT autografts or only QT allografts, studies that did not report follow-ups, anatomic studies, animal or preclinical studies, and studies including skeletally immature humans.

Assessment of Agreement

Interreviewer agreements for the title, abstract, and full-text articles were assessed with the Cohen’s κ coefficient statistic for quantifying interrater agreement. The values were categorized a priori as follows: κ of 0.81 to 0.99 was considered almost perfect agreement; κ of 0.61 to 0.80, substantial agreement; κ of 0.41 to 0.60, moderate agreement; κ of 0.21 to 0.40, fair agreement; and κ of 0.20 or less, slight agreement.26

Quality Assessment

The level of evidence of all included studies was assessed by 2 independent reviewers using the recommended American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons classification system (R.J.C., D.d.S.).27 All included studies were reviewed for methodological quality using the MINORS (Methodological Index for Non-randomized Studies) checklist.28 Using the MINORS checklist, comparative and noncomparative studies can achieve a maximum score of 24 or 16, respectively. Studies must attain a minimum follow-up time of 1 year to be deemed appropriate for use, in accordance with MINORS. Any discrepancies in scoring were initially discussed between reviewers, and when needed, the senior author was consulted.

Data Extraction and Statistical Analysis

Data was extracted from the included studies and recorded in an Excel spreadsheet (version 2016; Microsoft, Redmond, WA). The recorded data included study characteristics (e.g., author, year of publication, sample size, study design). The reviewers also recorded descriptive statistics including the patients’ age and sex, follow-up interval, type of QT, and details of surgical management. Clinical outcomes at final follow-up were recorded; those that were most commonly documented are reported in this review. The primary outcome assessed was clinical outcome, including patient-reported outcome measures (e.g., Lysholm scores, International Knee Documentation Committee [IKDC] Subjective Knee Function scores, anterior knee pain), return-to-sport rates, postoperative rotatory and anterior-posterior (AP) stability, and complication rates. Complications were graded using a modified Clavien-Dindo surgical complication scale specific for orthopedics. Complications were graded on a scale of increasing severity from I to V.29 Complications given a grade of I were considered minor. Complications graded as II and III were considered major, and surgical intervention was necessary for elevating a type II complication to a type III complication. Grade IV complications were characterized by the necessity for life-saving surgical intervention; the only complication that fit grade V was death.

Because of the selection and various inherent biases that exist with combining studies of low sample size and heterogeneous reporting, as well as the fact that most included studies did not adjust for confounding variables, it was decided to not perform a meta-analysis.30 As such, it was decided to report ranges across various outcomes measures, derived from the included studies. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, ranges, and percentages, were calculated as appropriate.31 Descriptive statistics were calculated using SPSS software (version 24; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Search Strategy

The initial search yielded a total of 1320 studies. Sixty-four duplicates were removed, and 1256 unique studies remained for assessment. Systematic screening of the titles, abstracts, and full-text articles was performed, resulting in 18 studies that met the inclusion criteria for evaluation in this review (Fig 1).14,32-54 Interreviewer agreement was substantial at the title screening stage (κ = 0.72) and almost perfect at the abstract (κ = 0.81) and full-text (κ = 0.91) screening stages. The repeat search generated 61 potential studies, 6 of which were deemed inclusive.

Study Quality

Of the 24 studies assessed, 1 was a randomized clinical trial (level of evidence: I), 1 was a prospective randomized study (II), 1 was a prospective comparative study (III), 3 were prospective cohort studies (III), 9 were retrospective comparative studies (III), 2 were prospective case series (IV), 5 were retrospective case series (IV), 1 was a therapeutic study (III), and 1 was a matched case-control study (III). The median MINORS score for the 9 noncomparative studies was 9.5/16, and the MINORS score for the 15 comparative studies was 18/24. All studies included a minimum follow-up period of 1 year, and 9 of 24 studies (37.5%) used prospective data collection. A detailed summary of the characteristics of the included studies is provided (Table 1).

Study Characteristics

A total of 1715 patients in the 24 studies had ACLR with a QT autograft. Twenty studies reported on the use of B-QT on 1534 patients. Five studies (including 1 reporting both S-QT and B-QT) reported on use of S-QT on 181 patients. Regarding patient demographics, 26.1% of patients were female receiving B-QT, and 44.9% were female receiving S-QT. The mean age was 29.6 years (range 14 to 59) and 32.4 years (range 15 to 58) for B-QT and S-QT, respectively. The mean follow-up time was 41.2 months (range 12 to 120) and 25.5 months (range 12 to 46 months) for B-QT and S-QT (Table 1).

Objective Outcomes

A detailed summary of the outcomes reported in all included studies is shown in Table 2. With regard to rotatory stability, a positive pivot shift postoperatively was found more often with B-QT. The rate of positive postoperative pivot shifts with B-QT was reported in 13 studies and ranged from 0% to 39% (124/779 total patients) (Fig 2). For S-QT patients, the rate of positive postoperative pivot shifts, reported in 3 studies, was significantly reduced compared with B-QT and was 0% (P < .05) (0/116 patients) (Fig 2). Anterior-posterior (AP) stability was evaluated with side-to-side differences to a manual maximum force, measured with KT-1000, KT-2000, or Rolimeter. As per testing with KT-1000, the mean side-to-side difference for B-QT was reported in 7 studies (782 patients); the 7 studies reported the range of means 0.69 to 2.40 mm (Fig 3). The mean side-to-side difference with KT-1000 for S-QT was reported in 2 studies, demonstrating a range of 0.51 to 2.1 mm. With KT-2000, the mean side-to-side difference was reported in 2 B-QT studies (93 patients) with a range of 1.4 to 2.1 mm. One S-QT study reported a mean side-to-side difference of 3.3 mm at 12 months’ follow-up (26 patients). As per testing with Rolimeter, mean side-to-side difference was reported in 1 study for B-QT at 1.6 mm.32 For S-QT, 1 study reported a mean of 1.8 mm.52

Quadriceps strengths reported were too heterogeneous to allow for relevant comparisons. Quadriceps strength measurements from those studies where reported are included in Table 2.

Patient-Reported Outcomes

Mean IKDC subjective scores after use of B-QT were reported in 8 studies (379 patients), including a range of mean scores of 67.3 to 89.66 (Fig 4). Scores were also reported for S-QT in 1 study (55 patients) with a mean value of 80.4. Lysholm scores after use of B-QT were reported in 13 studies (1227 patients), with mean scores ranging from 85.7 to 97.4 (Fig 5). Lysholm scores were also reported for 2 S-QT studies (94 patients) with a range of 89 to 89.2. Because kneeling pain was reported in only 1 study with S-QT, there is insufficient information available to draw meaningful comparisons between B-QT and S-QT regarding postoperative knee pain.

Major Complications

Major complications included graft rupture, patellar fracture, and deep vein thrombosis (DVT). The reported rate of B-QT graft rupture in 10 studies ranged from 0% to 9% (29/1120 patients) (Fig 6) and in SQT ranged from 0% to 3.8% (2/122 patients) (Fig 6). No difference between graft rupture rate of B-QT versus S-QT was observed. Of the 29 graft ruptures in B-QT, 7 (24%) were atraumatic in etiology. No atraumatic graft ruptures were reported for S-QT. Eight patellar fractures were noted in the B-QT group, all due to direct trauma 5 to 6 months postoperatively. Regarding the documented B-QT patellar fractures, 3 occurred 5 to 6 months postoperatively from a traumatic fall, 2 occurred intraoperatively, 1 occurred during strength testing, and 2 occurred postoperatively from an unknown and presumed atraumatic etiology. There was 1 reported DVT in B-QT patients, and there were no reported instances of DVT for S-QT. Because data regarding S-QT was limited concerning patellar fracture and DVT rates was absent, no comparisons were made between graft choices.

Minor Complications

Minor complications reported included lateral numbness, tibial screw irritation, and infection. Two studies reported lateral numbness in 4 B-QT patients, and 1 study reported this finding in 1 S-QT patient. Irritation at the tibial screw was noted twice (2 studies) for B-QT patients, and 1 instance required removal of the tibial graft fixation screw owing to screw protrusion and localized pain. Additionally, in the B-QT group, 2 stitch abscesses postoperatively were noted (1 study), and 1 superficial infection that resolved and intravenous antibiotics were administered (1 study).36,51 One additional superficial infection was reported for S-QT (1 study).52 Because of heterogeneity of reporting minor complications, no comparisons were made between B-QT and S-QT.

Discussion

The main findings from this review report that more B-QT patients demonstrated postoperative rotatory instability than S-QT, and that there is no differences in graft rupture between the 2 graft choices. Although statistical conclusions may not be drawn because of heterogeneity in reporting, it appears that the B-QT group featured much wider major and minor complication profiles.

Objective and subjective functions postoperatively demonstrated no appreciable differences between B-QT and S-QT, except for difference in favor of S-QT regarding postoperative measures of rotatory laxity. Increased rotatory laxity after ACLR, assessed by tests such as the pivot shift maneuver,64,65 has been associated with poorer patient-reported outcomes (e.g., decreased satisfaction, partial and full giving-way symptoms, activity limitations), increased osteoarthritis risk, and failure to return to sport at preinjury levels.66-68 The results herein demonstrate that B-QT patients have poorer rotatory laxity compared with S-QT patients, possibly explaining the greater rate of postoperative complications seen in B-QT.

Regarding the complication rates of B-QT and S-QT, there was no difference reported between the graft rupture rates for B-QT and S-QT. When subcategorized and reported as either traumatic or atraumatic graft rupture, B-QT reported a substantially higher rate of atraumatic graft rupture than S-QT. Although not well reported and characterized in all studies, this result may suggest that S-QT results in superior outcomes regarding atraumatic graft rupture. However, patellar fractures were reported only in patients receiving B-QT. Given the larger patient population represented in the B-QT group as well as the rare frequency (1.4%), it is possible that patellar fracture is a rare complication that becomes apparent only in a larger sample size. The rate of patellar fracture in B-QT reported in this review is comparable to the range of 0.2% to 2.3% commonly reported in BPTB.69 However, this must be interpreted with caution, as the risk factors for patellar fracture can be dramatically influenced by surgical technique; it might also be possible that this difference in patellar fracture rate represents a true phenomenon.

In the included studies, the size of the bone block was reported as 18 to 25 mm in length, 10 mm in width, and 7 mm thick. Although not specified across all studies, the width and length of the bone block significantly affects clinical outcomes, as the patella has a different shape and morphology superiorly as well as laterally. The thinner morphology of the patella superiorly is more likely to fracture in isolating the B-QT graft compared with an inferior approach, as in BTB. It will be important in future studies looking at B-QT and S-QT to report the dimensions of the bone block as well as the interpretation of the preoperative magnetic resonance imaging, as individual patient patella osseous morphology can have tremendous influence on the patellar fracture risk for an individual patient.

This review evaluates the work of 24 studies after screening an initial 1346 for information about primary ACLR using QT. Other QT systematic reviews have reviewed fewer studies.5,18 Furthermore, this work serves as the first systematic review to specifically compare the clinical and functional outcomes of ACLR with B-QT and S-QT autografts. Specifically, it demonstrates decreased rotatory stability in B-QT in comparison to S-QT and no statistical differences in graft rupture rates. Still, this review also reveals similar outcomes between B-QT and S-QT, such as comparable levels of reported kneeling symptoms, graft failure, and subjective patient-reported scores. This study also reveals the paucity of literature directly comparing B-QT and S-QT.

Limitations

Limitations of the present study include an inability to perform a complete meta-analysis of the studies, representing an inconsistent reporting of surgical technique and postoperative findings. Because of inconsistent outcome reporting with measures of variance as well as the low level of evidence in included studies, it was impossible to perform a meta-analysis of the reported outcomes. Inclusion of all studies in meta-analysis would strengthen the evidence reported in this study, but also requires identical representation of results across each study. There are many factors that play a role in ACLR surgical technique optimization, including tunnel positioning, drilling, fixation, and graft choice. Graft choice continues to be a key element for discussion, most notably the bone block versus all soft tissue debate.

Conclusion

The main findings from this review is that more B-QT patients demonstrated postoperative rotatory instability than S-QT, and that there are no significant differences in graft rupture between the 2 graft choices. Although statistical conclusions may not be drawn because of heterogeneity in reporting, it appears that the B-QT group featured much wider major and minor complication profiles.
