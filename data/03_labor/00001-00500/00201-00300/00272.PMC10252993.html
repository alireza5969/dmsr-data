Association of metabolic syndrome and its components with the risk of depressive symptoms: A systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies☆

Hong-Bae Kim[a]

Abstract

Background:

The purpose of this meta-analysis was to quantitatively analyze the association between metabolic syndrome (MetS) and the risk of depressive symptoms.

Methods:

Three electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, and PsycINFO) were searched for articles published through February 15, 2022. Cohort studies evaluating the association between MetS and depressive symptoms were selected for inclusion in this study. A random-effects model was used to evaluate the pooled estimates of MetS, including each of its components, associated with depressive symptoms.

Results:

A total of 11 cohort studies were selected including >2.65 million participants. There was a significant association between MetS and depressive symptoms risk, albeit with a high degree of heterogeneity (relative risk = 1.29, 95 % confidence interval: 1.12–1.48; I2 = 79.3 %). This association was consistently significant in Western countries, but it was not significant in Asian countries. When stratified by sex, age, and geographical region, MetS and its components exhibited varying degrees of association with depressive symptoms.

Conclusions:

MetS is a risk factor for depressive symptoms. Further large-scale prospective cohort studies are required to confirm our findings.

Introduction

1.

Depression is one of the most common illnesses worldwide, affecting approximately 280 million individuals. This includes 5 % of adults, and the prevalence increases further to 5.7 % in those aged >60 years. Depression not only harms health and causes a loss of function but also leads to fatal consequences, such as suicide, in severe cases (World Health Organization (WHO), 2020). Most cases of depression are idiopathic, and the causes have not been entirely identified. In addition to psychological stressors and maternal depression (Hammen, 2018), traumatic experiences, low-quality diet, overweight and obesity, smoking, lack of physical activity, and loss of sleep are risk factors for depression (Berk et al., 2013).

Depression may precede cardiovascular disease (CVD), and vice versa. CVD and depression have common risk factors, such as inflammation, hypercoagulability, rhythm disturbances, and neurohormonal activation, which can cause both pathological conditions (Joynt et al., 2003). Therefore, it may be natural to observe harmful associations between cardiovascular risk factors, such as overweight or obesity (Luppino et al., 2010), and abdominal obesity (Xu et al., 2011), and the risk of depression.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is an assemblage of various CVD risk factors, including central obesity, high triglyceride status, reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, elevated blood pressure, and hyperglycemia (Grundy et al., 2005). Although various international criteria for MetS differ slightly, a combination of components is commonly approved (Amihăesei and Chelaru, 2014). In the past 30 years, the prevalence of MetS has rapidly increased from 12 % to 37 % in Asia and from 12 % to 26 % in Europe (Engin, 2017; Sigit et al., 2020). The prevalence of MetS in the United States increased from 33 % in 2011 to 37 % in 2016, although the increase was not statistically significant. The prevalence of MetS tends to increase as age increases, with the prevalence among those aged 20–39 years being 20 %, whereas the prevalence among those aged ≥60 years being as high as 49 % (Hirode and Wong, 2020).

Several meta-analyses demonstrating the association between depression and MetS have been recently performed (Vancampfort et al., 2014; Mordi et al., 2021). However, these meta-analyses examined people who reported depressive symptoms or major depressive disorder prior to MetS. It is important to consider whether MetS is a risk factor for depression in people who did not have depressive symptoms at baseline. A meta-analysis by Pan et al. (2012) investigated the association between MetS and depression in 12 cross-sectional studies. The adjusted odds ratio with depression as the outcome was 1.27 (95 % confidence interval [CI]: 1.07–1.57). However, this meta-analysis included only one study conducted in Asia and more recent studies in this region have been described in the literature and should be considered. Additionally, MetS is composed of five components and it is plausible that individual components defining MetS are more strongly associated with risk of depressive symptoms. Thus, the current study aimed to investigate the association between MetS and between the component of MetS and the risk of depression or depressive symptoms by conducting a thorough systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies in the literature.

Methods

2.

The present study was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al., 2009). The available PRISMA checklist is shown in Supplementary File 1. The protocol for this study is listed in the PROSPERO registry (registration number: CRD42021281113).

Data sources and searches

2.1.

Three electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, and PsycINFO) were systematically searched by each author using common keywords and comprehensive medical subject headings associated with MetS and depressive symptoms. The final literature search was conducted on February 15, 2022. The common keywords used during the search were as follows: “metabolic syndrome,” “syndrome x,” “plurimetabolic syndrome,” and “insulin resistance syndrome” for the exposure factors, and “depression,” “depressive symptom,” “depressive disorder,” “mood disorder,” and “antidepressant agents” for the outcome factors. The detailed literature search strategy is presented in Supplementary File 2. To identify additional studies, a manual review of the reference lists of all selected original articles and reviews was performed. To investigate all plausible studies, there were no language restrictions implemented in the search procedure.

Study selection

2.2.

Based on predetermined selection criteria, each author separately assessed the eligibility of all of the selected articles. Disagreements in the selection of studies among investigators were resolved by consensus. For the present meta-analysis, the following inclusion criteria were used: (1) observational studies with a prospective or retrospective cohort design; (2) studies with participants diagnosed with MetS according to the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP-III), International Diabetes Foundation (IDF), and American Heart Association (AHA) criteria, and other internationally recognized criteria or modified versions of these criteria for metabolic syndrome (Grundy et al., 2005; Alberti et al., 2005); (3) studies that analyzed and reported the association between “MetS” and “depressive symptoms”; (4) studies on human subjects, with comparison between adults aged ≥18 years with MetS and those without MetS; (5) studies in which MetS was diagnosed before the development of depressive symptoms; and (6) large (n > 500) observational studies with a follow-up period of ≥1 year.

The exclusion criteria of the current meta-analysis were as follows: (1)observational studies with a cross-sectional or case-control design; (2)studies demonstrating a single component of MetS and those not using standardized criteria for MetS; 3) studies using data from preexisting psychiatric disorders or depressive symptoms; 4) studies performed on the pediatric population (<18 years old); 5) studies with <500 participants or with a follow-up duration of <1 year; and 6) letters to the editor, review articles, case reports, unpublished studies, and abstracts that were presented only at academic conferences.

If the data were duplicated or extracted from the same population, the most recently published study with a longer follow-up period was selected for inclusion in our study.

Data extraction and quality assessment

2.3.

A standardized form was used to extract the following information from the retrieved studies: name of the first author, publication year, study design, geographical region, follow-up duration, demographics (mean age or age range and sex), sample size, prevalence of MetS, ascertainment of the exposure and outcome measurement, confounding variables that underwent adjustment, and adjusted relative risk (RR) and 95 % CI. When the selected papers had insufficient data, the original corresponding author was contacted for obtaining the unpublished data, if possible.

As all included papers were nonrandomized and had a cohort design, the methodological quality of each selected study was established using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) (Wells et al., n.d.). Two investigators (H.-B. K. and J.-H. K.) independently assessed the methodological quality of each selected study based on the NOS. Disagreements between the two investigators were resolved by discussion with a third author (B. J. W.). A NOS score of >7 was considered to indicate a high quality (low risk of bias).

Data synthesis and analysis

2.4.

The primary analysis investigated the association between MetS and risk of depressive symptoms. Several subgroup analyses were performed, including those according to sex, mean age of the participants, geographical region, number of participants, ascertainment of the exposure and outcome assessments, prevalence of MetS, follow-up duration, methodological quality, and confounding factors, such as cardiovascular risks, body mass index (BMI), and socioeconomic status (SES).

We also investigated the risk of depressive symptoms for each MetS component. Additionally, we examined in detail how each MetS component was related to depressive symptoms by geographical region, sex, and age.

The Higgins I2 test was applied to determine the percentage of total variation to assess the heterogeneity among the studies (Higgins and Thompson, 2002). Negative values of I2 were set to zero, and the I2 results were between 0 % (no observed heterogeneity) and 100 % (maximum heterogeneity). An I2 of 0–25 % represented insignificant heterogeneity; 26–50 %, low heterogeneity; 51–75 %, moderate heterogeneity; and 76–100 %, high heterogeneity (Higgins and Thompson, 2002). A random-effects model based on the DerSimonian and Laird method was applied to calculate the overall RR and 95 % CI values, given that the individual characteristics and research methods of each selected article were distinct (Borenstein et al., 2010). When multiple confounding variables were statistically adjusted, the RR of the highest number of confounding variables was chosen. A meta-regression analysis was used to confirm whether covariates at the study level could explain the observed heterogeneity. Univariate random effects meta-regression analysis was used to confirm whether covariates at the study level could explain the observed heterogeneity. Study characteristics considered in meta-regression models included: sex; mean age; geographic region; number of participants; ascertainment of depressive symptoms; ascertainment of exposure; prevalence of MetS; follow-up duration; methodological quality; MetS component; and adjustment for confounders.

Finally, publication bias was evaluated using Begg's funnel plot and Egger's test (Begg and Mazumdar, 1994; Egger et al., 1997). An asymmetric Begg's funnel plot or a P-value of <0.05, as determined by Egger's test, indicated the existence of publication bias. In the case of publication bias, the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method (Duval and Tweedie, 2000) was employed as an adjustment of the meta-estimates to measure the impact of publication bias. Plausible outliers were visually demonstrated, and their impact on the significance of the effect size was checked. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata SE, version 13.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

3.

Identification of relevant articles

3.1.

The process of selecting an adequate number of papers was shown in Fig. 1. A total of 7195 papers were identified by surveying three databases (PubMed, Embase, and PsycINFO) and by manually investigating the pertinent bibliographies. After removing 2861 duplicate studies, the titles and abstracts of each paper were examined, and an additional 4272 papers that did not satisfy the predetermined selection criteria were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 62 papers were inspected, and 51 papers that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. The remaining 11 studies comprised 10 prospective cohort studies (Koponen et al., 2008; Akbaraly et al., 2010; Almeida et al., 2009; Takeuchi et al., 2009; Akbaraly et al., 2011; Vogelzangs et al., 2011; Olvera et al., 2015; Jeon et al., 2019; Limon et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2020) and one retrospective cohort study (Han et al., 2019). Moreover, two additional prospective cohort studies (Skilton et al., 2007; Mast et al., 2008) were identified and selected after investigating the list of references elicited after examining the full texts. Ultimately, 13 cohort studies (Koponen et al., 2008; Akbaraly et al., 2010; Almeida et al., 2009; Takeuchi et al., 2009; Akbaraly et al., 2011; Vogelzangs et al., 2011; Olvera et al., 2015; Jeon et al., 2019; Limon et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2020; Han et al., 2019; Skilton et al., 2007; Mast et al., 2008) were included in the present systematic review. Among these, two studies (Skilton et al., 2007; Olvera et al., 2015) were excluded from final meta-analysis because they did not explicitly state that only people without depressive symptoms at baseline were included.

Characteristics of studies and methodological quality assessment

3.2.

Table 1 showed the general characteristics of the 11 cohort studies included in the final meta-analysis. The included articles, which were published between 2008 and 2020, analyzed >2.65 million participants, including >1.32 million patients with depressive symptoms. Most of the studies involved community-based general populations, but one study (Jeon et al., 2019) involved examinees who visited a particular hospital for a health examination. All studies adopted a prospective cohort design, with the exception of one (Han et al., 2019), which had a retrospective design. The mean follow-up duration ranged from 1 to 8 years. Of the 10 prospective cohort studies, the participants were followed up for >5 years in six studies (Koponen et al., 2008; Akbaraly et al., 2010; Almeida et al., 2009; Vogelzangs et al., 2011; Limon et al., 2020; Han et al., 2019). Three of these studies (Koponen et al., 2008; Almeida et al., 2009; Limon et al., 2020) had a follow-up period of >7 years. The number of participants in the selected studies ranged from 604 to 2,506,095. The mean age of the participants ranged from 39.1 to 73.6 years. The mean percentage of the female participants was 42.2 %. Among the studies that presented data for both men and women, three studies (Koponen et al., 2008; Limon et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2020) demonstrated results stratified by sex.

The selected articles were conducted in the following nine countries: United States (n = 2) (Limon et al., 2020; Mast et al., 2008), France (n = 1)(Akbaraly et al., 2011), United Kingdom (n = 1) (Akbaraly et al., 2010), Finland (n = 1) (Koponen et al., 2008), Italy (n = 1) (Vogelzangs et al., 2011), Australia (n = 1) (Almeida et al., 2009), South Korea (n = 2) (Jeon et al., 2019; Han et al., 2019), Japan (n = 1) (Takeuchi et al., 2009), and China (Yu et al., 2020). Regarding the exposure assessment, seven studies (Koponen et al., 2008; Akbaraly et al., 2010; Akbaraly et al., 2011; Vogelzangs et al., 2011; Jeon et al., 2019; Han et al., 2019; Mast et al., 2008) defined MetS according to the NCEP ATP-III criteria, while two studies (Takeuchi et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2020) utilized the IDF criteria for MetS. Furthermore, one study (Limon et al., 2020) followed the AHA definition for MetS. In the remaining study (Almeida et al., 2009), the authors presented their own criteria for MetS, which was validated in a previous study. Regarding the outcome assessment, three studies (Almeida et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2020; Han et al., 2019) used a diagnosis of major depressive disorder. The remaining 8 studies (Koponen et al., 2008; Akbaraly et al., 2010; Takeuchi et al., 2009; Akbaraly et al., 2011; Vogelzangs et al., 2011; Jeon et al., 2019; Limon et al., 2020; Mast et al., 2008) defined depressive symptoms using various types of questionnaires.

All the selected studies were written in English. Supplementary File 3 presented the variables adjusted for in each article and the RR of incident depression according to MetS and its components.

The NOS scores of the selected papers ranged from 7 to 9 stars (Supplementary File 4), and the mean scores of the included cohort studies were 8.1. Of the 11 selected papers, four (Akbaraly et al., 2010; Almeida et al., 2009; Vogelzangs et al., 2011; Limon et al., 2020) acquired 9 stars on the NOS, six (Koponen et al., 2008; Akbaraly et al., 2011; Jeon et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Han et al., 2019; Mast et al., 2008) acquired 8 stars, and one (Takeuchi et al., 2009) acquired 7 stars, indicating an overall low risk of bias. All of the selected studies, except the one by Takeuchi et al. (Takeuchi et al., 2009), were considered to be of a high quality.

Metabolic syndrome in association with risk of depressive symptoms

3.3.

A significant association between MetS and the risk of depressive symptoms was observed in the random-effects meta-analysis (RR = 1.29, 95 % CI, 1.12–1.48; Fig. 2), although high heterogeneity was present (Cochran's Q statistic = 48.23, P < 0.001, I2 = 79.3 %). When stratified by geographical region, a significant association was maintained in the Western region (RR = 1.47, 95 % CI, 1.15–1.87, I2 = 59.6 %), but significance was absent in the Asian region (RR = 1.08, 95 % CI, 0.94–1.24, I2 = 81.5 %) (Table 2).

Table 2 showed the subgroup analyses according to the various factors. MetS was consistently linked to the risk of depressive symptoms in the subgroup analyses by the mean age, sex, number of participants, follow-up duration, and methodological quality. Stronger associations were observed in participants aged >60 years (RR = 1.46 vs. 1.17), male sex (RR = 2.18 vs. 1.71), in studies with a small number of participants (⟨2000) (RR = 1.52 vs. 1.20), in studies utilizing an ascertainment of exposure other than NCEP ATP-III criteria for the definition of MetS (RR = 1.61 vs. 1.30), and in studies with a low methodological quality (RR = 2.14 vs. 1.26).

When BMI, SES, and physical activity were adjusted for, a significant association between MetS and depressive symptoms was sustained. The association across studies that did not adjust for cardiovascular risks was stronger (RR = 1.61 vs. 1.11), and adjustment for BMI also showed a stronger association (RR = 1.56 vs. 1.23).

In addition, meta-regression analysis indicated that no particular factor accounted for between-study heterogeneity.

Finally, regarding each diagnostic component of MetS, a significant positive association was found between the risk of depressive symptoms and central obesity (1.34 [1.07–1.69]), hypertriglyceridemia (1.07 [1.05–1.09]), and a low HDL level (1.20 [1.09–1.32]). However, the risk of depressive symptoms was not found to be associated with high blood pressure (0.96 [0.90–1.03]) or hyperglycemia (1.10 [0.96–1.26]).

Associations between each component of metabolic syndrome with risk of depressive symptoms

3.4.

Table 3 showed the relationship between each component of MetS and depressive symptoms stratified by geographical region. In Western countries, central obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, and a low HDL level were associated with an increased risk of depressive symptoms, which was similar to the overall results. Compared with the overall results, the association with central obesity was slightly lower (RR = 1.17 vs. 1.34), and the association with hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL levels was slightly higher (RR = 1.24 vs. 1.10, 1.42 vs. 1.20, respectively). In Asia, the strongest significant association with the risk of depressive symptoms was central obesity, although there was no significant association between MetS and depressive symptoms in this area. Unlike the other components of MetS, high blood pressure was marginally associated with a slightly decreased risk of depressive symptoms (RR = 0.99, 95 % CI, 0.98–1.00, I2 = 0.0 %) in the Asian population.

Each MetS component associated with the risk of depressive symptoms stratified by sex was shown in Table 4. Interestingly, although MetS had a significantly moderate association with depressive symptoms in female participants, none of its components exhibited a significant association with depressive symptoms. Among the male participants, low HDL-C levels had the strongest association with depressive symptoms risk.

Table 5 showed the association between the diagnostic components of MetS and depressive symptoms risk with respect to the mean age. Individuals <60 years of age showed a similar pattern with an overall association with depressive symptoms, for which central obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, and a low HDL-C level significantly increased the risk of depressive symptoms. The results for individuals aged >60 years, all in Western individuals, showed that the association between central obesity and depressive symptoms was weaker (RR = 1.10 vs. 1.34). The association between hypertriglyceridemia and depressive symptoms was not significant in older individuals. Hyperglycemia was associated with an increased risk of depressive symptoms (RR = 1.49, 95 % CI, 1.23–1.80; I2 = 0.0 %), contrary to the results of other analyses. Because the population aged <60 years was a combination of Western and Asian people, only Western individuals aged <60 years were separately analyzed, as shown in Fig. 3. In this population, central obesity and hypertriglyceridemia showed the greatest harmful association with depressive symptoms. Unlike the outcomes in other population groups, there was no significant association between a low HDL-C level and depressive symptoms in the young Western population.

Publication bias

3.5.

Supplementary File 5 presented a statistically significant asymmetry of the funnel plot (P = 0.043) through the Egger regression test, indicating the existence of publication bias. Consequently, it was possible that small-scale cohort studies comprising negative or null results were not published through a peer-reviewed procedure or were selected in the current study. Therefore, we applied the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method (Duval and Tweedie, 2000) to recalculate the pooled RR with the inclusion of missing hypothetical articles. The recalculated RR of association between MetS and depressive symptoms was 1.14 (95 % CI: 1.02–1.28). Supplementary File 6 showed that after adding four missing hypothetical articles, the redrawn funnel plot became symmetrical.

Discussion

4.

MetS demonstrated a significant association with the risk of depressive symptoms based on the results of our meta-analysis, and this association was robust in subgroup analyses across diverse factors. Among the MetS components, central obesity, a low HDL level, and hypertriglyceridemia showed a prominent association with the risk of depressive symptoms.

A previous meta-analysis by Pan et al. (2012) examined the bidirectional association between MetS and depressive symptoms in both cross-sectional and cohort studies. A potential disadvantage of the study by Pan et al. is that the scope of the investigation was broad and the level of evidence might have been weakened. The result of the current meta-analysis were similar to Pan et al.; however, an important difference is that our study investigated the association between each MetS component and depressive symptoms stratified by sex, age, and region.

Several probable biological mechanisms may explain the association between MetS and an increased risk of depressive symptoms. First, chronic low-grade inflammation, a common mechanism of MetS and depressive symptoms, may account for this association. MetS can also promote oxidative stress, resulting in the activation and enhancement of inflammatory pathways (Varghese et al., 2018). An inadequate regulation of the immune response can lead to depressive symptoms (Beurel et al., 2020). Chronic inflammation may also cause insulin resistance, which may lead to depressive symptoms (Frangou et al., 2019). Second, abnormalities in the hypothalamus–pituitary adrenal (HPA) axis may contribute to the occurrence of depressive symptoms in patients with MetS. Elevated cortisol levels due to HPA axis dysfunction are a hallmark of MetS (Gragnoli, 2014), and hypercortisolism causes serotonergic dysfunction, which is a substrate for depressive symptoms (Pompili et al., 2010). Third, an abnormal functioning of the autonomic nervous system (ANS), a common mechanism underlying both MetS and depressive symptoms, may be involved in the occurrence of depressive symptoms. An elevated resting heart rate (RHR), a typical marker of ANS disability, is usually observed in patients with MetS (Festa et al., 2000). In addition, a decline in heart variability, baroreflex dysfunction, and increased RHR, all of which are representative signs of ANS disruption, were commonly observed in patients with depressive symptoms (Carney et al., 2005).

Apart from the possible biological mechanisms, depressive symptoms could be a context for the insalubrious habits of patients with MetS. The intake of unhealthy foods, such as those in the Western diet, can contribute to the development of MetS (Wirfält et al., 2001), whereas eating plenty of vegetables and fruits shows a protective function against MetS (Esmaillzadeh et al., 2006). In contrast, the inordinate intake of carbohydrates, cholesterol, and calories, all of which are typical characteristics of a Western diet, is prevalent in people with depressive symptoms (Bonnet et al., 2005a; Bonnet et al., 2005b). Furthermore, the Mediterranean diet pattern, in contrast to the Western diet, is closely linked to a low risk of depressive symptoms (Psaltopoulou et al., 2013). Besides the intake of healthy foods, physical activity is one of the most pivotal factors that can represent a preventable association against the development of both MetS and depressive symptoms (Xu et al., 2019; Kandola et al., 2019). Thus, physical activity, as a common healthy habit, may explain this association.

Among MetS components, central obesity showed the strongest association with depressive symptoms in the current meta-analysis. This result is mainly attributed to leptin resistance underlying both MetS and depressive symptoms (Ghadge and Khaire, 2019; Morris et al., 2012). In addition to leptin resistance, a strong association between central obesity and depressive symptoms can be attributed to chronic low-grade inflammation. Abdominal adipose tissue acts as an endocrine organ and releases various inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α, C-reactive protein, and interleukin-6 (Elks and Francis, 2010), which can lead to the occurrence of depressive symptoms.

The current meta-analysis has several limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed, which is mainly due to the variability in the ascertainment of MetS among the studies. Differences in the geographical region, number of participants, and methodological quality of the studies may also have contributed to the high heterogeneity. Although we conducted subgroup analyses to explicate heterogeneity, it remained unchanged. Second, there is a possibility that additional confounding factors remained unaccounted for, which may influence exposure or outcome measurements. For example, anxiety, an emotional state similar to depressive symptoms, tends to coexist with MetS (Nousen et al., 2013). When anxiety was considered simultaneously, there was no relationship between MetS and depressive symptoms in a case-control study of young Finnish adults (Herva et al., 2006). None of the selected articles in our study was statistically corrected for anxiety. Moreover, the use of antidepressants or comorbidity with mental disorders that could affect the association between MetS and depressive symptoms was corrected in the study by Koponen et al. (2008). Third, the incidence of depressive symptoms could not be quantified according to the number of MetS components. Three studies (Takeuchi et al., 2009; Jeon et al., 2019; Skilton et al., 2007) included in the present meta-analysis showed a tendency for the prevalence of depressive symptoms to increase as the number of MetS components increased. However, Jeon et al. (2019) specified the RR values according to the number of MetS components. Finally, there was not enough data to demonstrate the effect of MetS by dividing the subtypes of depressive symptoms, such as melancholic features, atypical depression, and undifferentiated depressive symptoms.

In conclusion, the risk of depressive symptoms increased in association with MetS, most notably with a specific component of central obesity. The concept of the vascular subtype of depressive symptoms has recently emerged in relation to metabolic modifications by cardiovascular risk factors. Consequently, depressive symptoms can result from metabolic disabilities caused by circulatory diseases (Marazziti et al., 2014). To confirm the results of our study, a large-scale prospective cohort study that addresses numerous confounding variables is required. It is essential to determine the components of MetS that are associated with depressive symptoms in specific population groups. In addition, it is necessary to examine whether improving the status of MetS has a positive effect on reducing the prevalence of depressive symptoms.
