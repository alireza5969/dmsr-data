Gender Differences in Treatment Delays, Management and Mortality among Patients with Acute Coronary Syndrome: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

Tetiana Lunova[1][*]

Roman Komorovsky[1]

Ivan Klishch[1]

 

[1] Horbachevsky Ternopil National Medical University, Ternopil, Ukraine

Author notes:

Correspondence to: [*] Address correspondence to this author at the Horbachevsky Ternopil National Medical University, Ternopil, Ukraine; Tel: 0989214640; E-mail: lunova@tdmu.edu.ua

Abstract

Background

We aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of existing gender differences in acute coronary syndrome (ACS), with respect to treatment delays, invasive management of ACS, and short and long-term mortality in patients with ACS.

Methods

We defined 37 observational studies (OSs) and 21 randomized trials (RCTs) that best corresponded to our interests. OSs and RCTs were analyzed separately.

Results

Women with ACS more often experienced delays in treatment compared to men (OR 1.43; 95% CI, 1.12-1.82) in RCTs. Female patients were less often treated invasively: RCTs (OR 0.87; 95% CI, 0.83-0.9), OSs: (OR 0.66; 95% CI, 0.63-0.68). Women had higher crude in-hospital mortality (OR 1.56; 95% CI, 1.53-1.59) and 30-day mortality (OR 1.71; 95% CI, 1.22-2.4) in OSs and (OR 2.74; 95% CI, 2.48-3.02) in RCTs. After adjustment for multiple covariates, gender difference was attenuated: in-hospital mortality (OR 1.19; 95% CI, 1.17-1.2), 30-day mortality (OR 1.18; 95% CI, 1.12-1.24) in OSs. Unadjusted long-term mortality in women was higher than in men (OR 1.41; 95% CI, 1.31-1.52) in RCTs and (OR 1.4; 95% CI, 1.3-1.5) in OSs.

Conclusion

Women with ACS experience a delay in time to treatment more often than men. They are also less likely to be treated invasively. Females have shown worse crude short-and long-term all-cause mortality compared to males. However, after adjustment for multiple covariates, a less significant gender difference has been observed. Considering the difference between crude and adjusted mortality, we deem it reasonable to conduct further investigations on gender-related influence of particular risk factors on the outcomes of ACS.

INTRODUCTION

1

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death among women all over the world, with the acute coronary syndrome (ACS) representing a major proportion [1]. CVD causes 51% of female deaths in Europe and accounts for 1 of every 3 female deaths in the United States [2, 3]. The existence of gender differences in the presentation, management, and prognosis of the acute coronary syndrome has been acknowledged by the European Society of Cardiology with a Class I (B) recommendation that both genders should be treated in the same way [1]. Nevertheless, women continue to demonstrate higher mortality rates as compared to men. The question of whether female gender itself is an independent predictor of adverse outcomes [4-8], or this can be explained by higher cardiovascular risk profiles [9-18] in women, along with the differences in the management of ACS, remains controversial. In this meta-analysis, we aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of existing gender differences in ACS in several aspects: 1) gender-related differences in treatment delays; 2) gender disparities in the management of ACS with a focus on invasive treatment; 3) gender differences in short-term and long-term outcomes in patients with ACS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

2

Data Sources and Study Selection

2.1

We conducted a literature search in PubMed, EMBACE, and Cochrane library databases up to July 2020. ACS was defined as acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina (UA). No limitations concerning age or region of research were introduced. Studies that reported patients with stable ischemic heart disease were omitted. Same applied to the researches, including patients during hospital admission with no defined diagnosis. Lastly, we involved studies that encompassed both genders, hence research works based exclusively on female or male populations were excluded. According to the established criteria, 37 observational studies and 21 post hoc analyses of data from randomized controlled trials were selected. The process of search is represented in a PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1).

Data Extraction

2.2

Study selection and data extraction were done by two independent reviewers (TL and RK) according to pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria, and all disagreements were resolved through discussion. The data included the study name, publication year, study design, the number of participants in the study, and their baseline cardiovascular risk profile (Table 1). The outcome of interest for the analysis of delays in treatment was the pooled index of door-to-balloon time in minutes [19], the percentage of patients treated within the established time frames [20, 21], the percentage of patients who experienced a delay in hospital care [22, 23]. This all was summarized as the odds ratio of treatment delay in women vs. men.

The area of interest for the analysis of sex differences in treatment strategies was the number (%) of women and men with ACS treated invasively. Coronary angiography, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and coronary artery bypass surgery (CABG) were referred to as invasive treatments.

For the analysis of gender differences in short-term and long-term outcomes after acute coronary syndrome, articles that reported in-hospital, 30-day, and at least 1-year all-cause mortality were extracted. In case the OR was not provided by a study, we calculated it using the available data.

Newcastle-Ottawa scale was utilized to assess the quality of the included studies (Table 1).

Statistical Analysis

2.3

Statistical data were analyzed with the assistance of the statistical software Stata 14.0 (Stata Corp LLC, Texas, USA). In case the OR was not provided by a study, we calculated it using the available information. For all outcomes, dichotomous data were pooled as Mantel-Haenszel odds ratio (OR) with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity across studies was assessed with I2 test with I2 value of less than 25% considered low heterogeneity, 25-50% moderate, and greater than 50% as substantial heterogeneity. The p-values were reported for the test of I2, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Subgroup analysis was performed in case of substantial heterogeneity. To avoid publication bias, clinical trials and observational studies were analyzed separately. Moreover, for the analyses of all-cause mortality, crude and adjusted odds ratios were pooled separately to determine whether female gender is an independent predictor of adverse outcomes. The adjusted measures of association with mortality were also extracted and analyzed.

RESULTS/ FINDINGS

3

Study Characteristics

3.1

We defined 37 observational studies (OSs) and 21 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that best corresponded with our outcomes of interest (Table 1). For the analysis of gender differences in time to treatment, only RCTs were selected. Data on sex differences in invasive treatment strategies of ACS were collected via the review of 7 RCTs and 13 OSs. For the analysis of long-term and short-term all-cause mortality in both genders, 9 RCTs and 30 OSs were reviewed, among which 15 reported adjusted analyses. Most of the studies were adjusted for age, heart failure, diabetes, hypertension, symptom-to-balloon (STB), and door-to-balloon (DTB) times.

Characteristics of Patient Population

3.2

Most studies included more men than women. Women were generally older than men presenting with ACS. Most studies also reported female patients to have a higher prevalence of comorbidities, such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. At the same time, they were less often smokers than men (Table 2).

Treatment Delays

3.3

Five RCTs reported delays in treatment in women compared to men and involved 33, 975 participants (24937 men and 9038 women). They showed the female sex to be significantly associated with a longer time to treatment. In the analysis of the adjusted odds ratios from selected trials, women with ACS proved to have higher odds of experiencing a delay in treatment (OR 1.43 [1.12-1.82] I2 = 23.7%) (Fig. 2).

Invasive Treatment

3.4

Seven RCTs with a total of 85372 participants (53401 men and 31971 women) showed that women are treated invasively less often than men (OR 0.87 [0.83-0.9] I2 = 68.4%). Data from OSs confirm that women are less likely to undergo invasive treatment for ACS, including CA, PCI, and CABG, than men (Fig. 3). 13 OSs were investigated, which included 148638 participants (107987 men and 40651women). Pooled analysis from these studies shows that women have lower odds of invasive treatment compared to men (OR 0.66 [0.63-0.68] I2= 65.5%) (Fig. 4). Due to high heterogeneity (I2 >50%), subgroup analysis was performed according to various prognostic factors. Nevertheless, the source of heterogeneity could not be identified; hence, the random effects model was used. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that the results were stable and credible.

Short-Term All-Cause Mortality

3.5

In-Hospital Mortality

3.5.1

Nine OSs reported unadjusted in-hospital all-cause mortality in women vs. men, with a total of 321,038 men and 186,663 women. In the unadjusted analysis, women had a substantially higher risk of in-hospital mortality compared to men (OR 1.56 [1.53-1.59], I2 = 82.3%) (Fig. 5). In the adjusted analysis of ten OSs, which involved 482,307 men and 248,026 women, the difference in mortality between genders was attenuated (OR 1.19 [1.17-1.20], I2 = 28.9%) (Fig. 6). Pooling of unadjusted ORs showed high heterogeneity, but the factors accounting for such heterogeneity could not be identified. Therefore, a random effects model was used. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the results to be reliable.

30-day Mortality

3.5.2

The data from seven OSs that reported 30-day mortality in female and male patients with ACS were collected and pooled. In the unadjusted analysis, with a total of 8373 men and 2729 women, female patients showed significantly higher odds of 30-day all-cause mortality (OR 1.71 [1.22-2.4], I2 = 0.0%) (Fig. 7). However, after adjustment for age, comorbidities, and delay in treatment, the female gender itself did not prove to be an independent prognostic factor for 30-day mortality (OR 1.18 [1.12-1.24], I2 = 61.9%) (Fig. 8). Random effects model was used for the analysis of adjusted 30-day mortality, as heterogeneity could not be explained in the subgroup analysis.

Short-term Mortality (Clinical Trials)

3.5.3

Seven RCTs, which included 11,734 women and 32,755 men, were considered relevant and showed that women have more than two times higher chances of all-cause death within 30 days following ACS (OR 2.74 [2.48-3.02], I2 = 49.7%) (Fig. 9). The adjusted risk estimates were not represented in the study due to the lack of data.

Long-Term All-Cause Mortality

3.5.4

Pooled analysis of seven observational studies with a total of 25,438 men and 8753 women reported long-term (>= 1 year) all-cause mortality and showed that women had significantly higher unadjusted odds of death (OR 1.4 [1.3-1.5], I2 = 63.1%), (Fig. 10). The 4 RCTs (n= 19,574) exposed the same results (OR 1.41 [1.31-1.52], I2= 89.8%) (Fig. 11). However, in four observational studies, stepwise regression analysis did not identify female gender as an independent risk factor for long-term all-cause mortality [OR 0.94 (0.92-0.97), I2=0.0%), 4 observational studies (n= 83,005)] (Fig. 12). Due to the high heterogeneity of obtained results (I2 >50%), a subgroup analysis was performed. Still, the source of heterogeneity could not be identified; hence the random effects model was used. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the results to be reliable.

DISCUSSION

4

After pooling and analyzing data from 37 observational studies and 21 clinical trials, we have produced the following results: women experience significant delays in time to treatment compared to men, and they are less likely to be treated invasively. Female patients showed higher rates of unadjusted in-hospital, 30-day, and long-term mortality compared to their male counterparts, which was attenuated by adjusting for cardiovascular risk profile.

Women with ACS are more likely to experience a delay in time to treatment on both pre-hospital and in-hospital levels [24], which results in longer total ischemic time. The reason it takes longer for women to refer to the hospital may be subtle or atypical symptoms of ACS [25] as well as the lack of awareness of possible clinical patterns of ACS among the female population. Post hoc analysis of data from GUSTO-I and GUSTO-III clinical trials also exposed older age and comorbidities as contributive factors to patient delay. At the same time, medical insurance and higher levels of education were associated with earlier arrival at the hospital. Numerous studies indicate that a longer time to treatment substantially correlates with larger mortality [26-28].

As regards ACS treatment, women represent a group of patients at risk of not receiving the guideline-recommended invasive treatment, regardless of the severity of AMI [29-34]. At the same time, it has been reported that nonperformance of PCI is strongly associated with worse outcomes [35, 36]. Vice versa, data from the Italian Elderly ACS Study, FAST-MI Registry, and EXAMINATION trial suggest that women undergoing revascularization had lower short- and long-term mortality rates [37], compared to women who were treated non-invasively and had a reduced rate of repeated revascularization [38]. Admittedly, women are invasively undertreated for a number of reasons: delay in presentation could prevent women from having primary PCI within the recommended time frame, and the baseline cardiovascular profile in females is often less favorable than in males, which might prevent physicians from aggressive treatment. Another probable reason is the atypical symptoms of ACS in women, which leads to a false diagnosis, as atypical angina in female patients is often mistakenly perceived as a benign symptom. Lastly, females, especially those of a younger age, are known to have non-obstructive CAD more frequently than males, with a high prevalence of coronary spasm and endothelial dysfunction. However, despite this, it is still recommended that women and men should be treated in the same way.

Our meta-analysis indicated that women have significantly higher unadjusted short-term [39-44] and long-term [45-50] all-cause mortality compared to men. According to the achieved results, women have greater odds for short-term mortality, which was especially notable after the pooled analysis of clinical trials. However, after adjustment, the difference in both short-term and long-term mortality was attenuated [51-55]. Women do experience more post-infarction angina and heart failure though, which is a valid point for further investigation [55].

Most of the studies were adjusted for age, TIMI risk score, medical insurance, time to treatment, comorbidities (heart failure, DM, CAD, hypertension, renal failure, cerebrovascular disease), atrial fibrillation, and medications prescribed. Most of the included studies reported that women were older than men. Nevertheless, age alone should not be considered the key factor of adverse outcomes in female patients, as there are various studies reporting alarming mortality rates in young women with ACS compared to age-matched men [56]. This may be related to different factors, such as the relatively smaller diameter of coronary arteries, differences in thrombotic activity, and microvascular dysfunction [56]. The role of characteristic features of female lifestyle, like, for example, the use of oral contraceptives and the lack of awareness in the patient and physician level of the possibility of MI in women at this age can not be downplayed either [57].

In the analysis of selected studies, we identified that lower body weight and, consequently, lower BSA in women is also a risk factor not only for bleeding but also for higher mortality. This is also known as the “obesity paradox” [58].

Almost every included study reported that women have prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus and dyslipidemia, and heart failure, but they are less often smokers than men. Clustering of comorbidities in women is undoubtedly one of the reasons for the variety of bleeding complications and increased mortality. It is also possible that the poor baseline risk profile of female patients can often detain physicians from more radical treatment strategies.

In the selected studies, atrial fibrillation, multivessel CAD, cardiogenic shock on admission, WBC count> 11*109/L, anemia, severe renal failure, and blood glucose level >198 mg/dL showed a significant impact on the long-term all-cause mortality, but whether this effect is gender-related remains a relevant point to investigate.

Our findings are consistent with the results of the works of Bavishi et al. [59] and Liakos et al. [60]. However, in this meta-analysis, studies that included patients with either ACS [61-63], STEMI [64-69], NSTEMI [70] or UA [71] were analyzed, and not only those with STEMI. Moreover, apart from indicating gender differences in ACS, we also tried to analyze their causation.

Strengths of our meta-analysis are as follows: (1) our study included large sample size (37 observational studies and 21 randomized trials), which provides greater precision and reliability of the results; (2) observational studies and RCTs were analyzed separately, which reduced the possibility of bias; (3) our meta-analysis involved different aspects of gender differences in ACS (time to hospital care, treatment strategies of ACS and outcomes in patients with ACS); thus, our meta-analysis provides a comprehensive insight into this topic.

Limitations of this meta-analysis are as follows: (1) the studies were conducted in different countries, so the standards and trends of care may vary; (2) some pooled analyses showed high heterogeneity between studies, so the random effects model was adopted; (3) the number of male patients included in the meta-analysis was greater than female, hence, due to unequal distribution, there might be a bias.

NOVELTY AND IMPLICATIONS

5

Although gender differences in ACS have been widely discussed, conflicting data regarding the outcomes of patients exist. While some data suggest that female patients with ACS show higher mortality rates as compared to male patients, other researchers report no difference in short-term and long-term mortality. In this meta-analysis, raw and adjusted data have been analyzed separately, and associations between mortality rates and the severity of cardiovascular risk profiles, treatment strategy, and delays in time to treatment have been demonstrated. This provides us with a rationale to assume that modifying the algorithms of evidence-based treatment in female patients with ACS as well as effective public awareness programs may contribute to the improvement of the outcomes in patients.

CONCLUSION

Female gender identifies a group of patients at high risk of delay in hospital care. According to the results obtained, women are also more likely to be treated non-invasively and not always receive timely reperfusion as compared to their male counterparts. Female patients with ACS showed worse crude in-hospital, 30-day mortality and long-term all-cause mortality compared to male patients. However, after adjustment for multiple covariates, a less significant gender difference was observed. Consequently, we attribute such rates of mortality to poorer baseline cardiovascular profiles, which, along with delays in hospital care and different treatment approaches, may account for adverse outcomes. Meanwhile, the female gender itself was not an independent predictor of worse mortality. Taking into account the significant difference between crude and adjusted mortality, we deem it reasonable to conduct further investigations on gender-related influence of particular risk factors on short-term and long-term outcomes of ACS and to define those with the highest predictive value.

## INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death among women all over the world, with the acute coronary syndrome (ACS) representing a major proportion [1]. CVD causes 51% of female deaths in Europe and accounts for 1 of every 3 female deaths in the United States [2, 3]. The existence of gender differences in the presentation, management, and prognosis of the acute coronary syndrome has been acknowledged by the European Society of Cardiology with a Class I (B) recommendation that both genders should be treated in the same way [1]. Nevertheless, women continue to demonstrate higher mortality rates as compared to men. The question of whether female gender itself is an independent predictor of adverse outcomes [4-8], or this can be explained by higher cardiovascular risk profiles [9-18] in women, along with the differences in the management of ACS, remains controversial. In this meta-analysis, we aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of existing gender differences in ACS in several aspects: 1) gender-related differences in treatment delays; 2) gender disparities in the management of ACS with a focus on invasive treatment; 3) gender differences in short-term and long-term outcomes in patients with ACS.
## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Data Sources and Study Selection

We conducted a literature search in PubMed, EMBACE, and Cochrane library databases up to July 2020. ACS was defined as acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina (UA). No limitations concerning age or region of research were introduced. Studies that reported patients with stable ischemic heart disease were omitted. Same applied to the researches, including patients during hospital admission with no defined diagnosis. Lastly, we involved studies that encompassed both genders, hence research works based exclusively on female or male populations were excluded. According to the established criteria, 37 observational studies and 21 post hoc analyses of data from randomized controlled trials were selected. The process of search is represented in a PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1).

### Data Extraction

Study selection and data extraction were done by two independent reviewers (TL and RK) according to pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria, and all disagreements were resolved through discussion. The data included the study name, publication year, study design, the number of participants in the study, and their baseline cardiovascular risk profile (Table 1). The outcome of interest for the analysis of delays in treatment was the pooled index of door-to-balloon time in minutes [19], the percentage of patients treated within the established time frames [20, 21], the percentage of patients who experienced a delay in hospital care [22, 23]. This all was summarized as the odds ratio of treatment delay in women vs. men.

The area of interest for the analysis of sex differences in treatment strategies was the number (%) of women and men with ACS treated invasively. Coronary angiography, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and coronary artery bypass surgery (CABG) were referred to as invasive treatments.

For the analysis of gender differences in short-term and long-term outcomes after acute coronary syndrome, articles that reported in-hospital, 30-day, and at least 1-year all-cause mortality were extracted. In case the OR was not provided by a study, we calculated it using the available data.

Newcastle-Ottawa scale was utilized to assess the quality of the included studies (Table 1).

### Statistical Analysis

Statistical data were analyzed with the assistance of the statistical software Stata 14.0 (Stata Corp LLC, Texas, USA). In case the OR was not provided by a study, we calculated it using the available information. For all outcomes, dichotomous data were pooled as Mantel-Haenszel odds ratio (OR) with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity across studies was assessed with I2 test with I2 value of less than 25% considered low heterogeneity, 25-50% moderate, and greater than 50% as substantial heterogeneity. The p-values were reported for the test of I2, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Subgroup analysis was performed in case of substantial heterogeneity. To avoid publication bias, clinical trials and observational studies were analyzed separately. Moreover, for the analyses of all-cause mortality, crude and adjusted odds ratios were pooled separately to determine whether female gender is an independent predictor of adverse outcomes. The adjusted measures of association with mortality were also extracted and analyzed.
