Association between prior nephrectomy and efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy in metastatic renal cell carcinoma - A systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Background:

Immune checkpoint-inhibitor (ICI)-based therapy is the standard of care for first-line treatment of metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC). It is unclear whether prior removal of the primary tumor influences the efficacy of these treatments. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies of first-line ICI in mRCC to determine whether the efficacy of ICI-therapy, compared to sunitinib, is altered based on receipt of prior nephrectomy.

Methods:

We systematically reviewed studies indexed in MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, and Scopus and conference abstracts from relevant medical societies as of August 2020 to identify randomized clinical trials assessing first-line immunotherapy-based regimes in mRCC. Studies were included if overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) outcomes were reported with data stratified by nephrectomy status. We pooled hazard ratios (HRs) stratified by nephrectomy status and performed random effects meta-analysis to assess the null hypothesis of no difference in the survival advantage of immunotherapy-based regimes based on nephrectomy status, while accounting for study level correlations.

Results:

Among 6 randomized clinical trials involving 5,121 patients, 3,968 (77%) had undergone prior nephrectomy. We found an overall survival benefit for immunotherapy-based regimes, compared to sunitinib, among both patients who had undergone nephrectomy (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.63 −0.88) and those who had not (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.59 −0.92), without evidence of difference based on nephrectomy history (P = 0.70; I2 = 36%). Results assessing PFS were similar (P = 0.45, I2 = 0%).

Conclusions:

These clinical data suggest that prior nephrectomy does not affect the efficacy of ICI-based regimens in mRCC relative to sunitinib. © 2021 Elsevier Inc. All rights reserved.

Introduction

1.

Immunotherapy in the treatment of metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) was initially established more than 2 decades ago with the use of interferon and interleukins [1]. Recently, treatment regimens relying on an immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) backbone have been approved in the first line setting for mRCC [2−7]. In these studies, most patients (>70%) had undergone nephrectomy prior to randomization. This may reflect either cytoreductive nephrectomy prior to trial enrollment for synchronous metastasis or metachronous metastases following nephrectomy for localized disease.

Whether there is an association between prior removal of the primary tumor and the efficacy of immunotherapy is unclear. Effector lymphocytes that infiltrate renal tumors are downregulated by PD-L1 expressing tumor cells and tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) [8]. Targeting the B7-H1/PD-1 axis has led to significant clinical benefit across a variety of cancer types. However, multiple factors in the local tumor environment such as secreted inhibitory cytokines (e.g. TGF–β), regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells protect tumor cells from immune mediated destruction and may represent important mechanisms to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy resistance [9]. Further, there is emerging evidence that systemic immunity is modulated (dampening) by local tumor-immune interactions, leading to T-cell dysfunction beyond tumor-specific T-cells [10] and can be potentially reversed by resection of the primary tumor in the presence of metastatic disease [11,12]. While this suggests potential synergy between surgical resection and immunotherapy, response to immunotherapy is inversely proportional to tumor antigenic load which may be impaired after removal of the primary tumor [13]. How these opposing interactions affects clinical outcomes is, to our knowledge, yet to be determined.

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of trials examining first-line immune checkpoint inhibitors for mRCC to determine whether the magnitude of benefit of ICI-based therapy, compared to sunitinib, differed based on whether the patient had previously undergone nephrectomy.

Methods

2.

We performed this systematic review according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14]. This study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42020217953), the international prospective registry of systematic reviews.

Types of studies

2.1.

We included randomized controlled trials, with all observational studies (regardless of design), editorials, commentaries, and review articles excluded. Additionally, publications which were not peer-reviewed were excluded. Where there was more than once publication from the same study cohort, we selected the study for inclusion based on contemporaneous timing, thereby preventing duplication of patients.

We included studies comparing immunotherapy in the first line treatment of mRCC vs. tyrosine kinase inhibitor-based regimes, the previous standard of care. We excluded studies which compared only various immunotherapy regimes.

Outcomes and exposure of interest

2.2.

The primary outcome of interest was overall survival (OS) and secondary outcomes were progression free survival (PFS) and objective response rate (ORR). The primary exposure of interest was nephrectomy status.

Search strategy

2.3.

Systematic literature searches were conducted with the assistance of a medical librarian on August 6, 2020 in four databases for randomized controlled trials of human studies written in English, with no filters on publication date or other search limits applied. The databases searched were: (1) MEDLINE (via PubMed), (2) Embase (via OVID), (3) The Cochrane Library (via Wiley), and (4) Scopus (via Elsevier). Controlled vocabularies and text words were used in the development of the search strategies in PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane. Search results were combined (EndNote) and duplicates removed. Covidence was used for review which included title/abstract screening and full-text screening phases.

The search terminology included 3 major concepts, all linked together with the AND operator: (1) first-line advanced renal cell carcinoma—including newly diagnosed, metastasis, and kidney carcinoma terms; (2) monoclonal antibodies—including ipilimumab, tremelimumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab, pidilizumab, atezolizumab, durvalumab, and avelumab terms; (3) survival—including progression-free survival terms (Supplemental Table B.1).

In addition, we performed a concurrent hand search of abstracts and presentations from conference meetings focused on genitourinary oncology (e.g. ASCO, GU ASCO, ESMO) to identify unpublished data.

Review methods

2.4.

2 authors performed study selection independently (R.S. and J.C.A.G.) with disagreements resolved by consensus with the senior author (C.J.D.W.). Initial study inclusion was determined by screening titles and abstracts. Full-text review was utilized when abstracts provided insufficient information to determine whether the study met inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extracted included study first author, year of publication, immunotherapy and control drugs, cohort size, IMDC risk category, cohort median age, and median follow up. In addition, OS and PFS data reported as a hazard ratio (with 95% confidence interval) and ORR reported as n/N data, stratified by nephrectomy status, were abstracted. We approached the study sponsor to obtain estimates of OS and PFS stratified by nephrectomy status where these were not available in published manuscripts and to obtain the most up to date estimates available given ongoing maturation of data in each included trial. Individual patient level data was not obtained for this analysis. The intention to treat population was used for all analyses. We performed risk of bias assessment using The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool (Supplemental Table B.2).

Assessment of heterogeneity and statistical analysis

2.5.

We identified heterogeneity between studies using the Q test, estimated it using the DerSimonian-Laird method, and quantified it using I2 values [15]. We performed the meta-analysis using the Review Manager (Version 5.4.1, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014) software. The inverse variance technique was used for meta-analysis of HRs. We used random-effects models in order to account for the clinical heterogeneity inherent in the data and performed separate meta analyses for OS, PFS, and ORR. To examine differences in OS and PFS on the basis of a history of nephrectomy while accounting for study-level correlation, we calculated the difference in log hazard ratio (logHR) between patients with a history of nephrectomy and those without nephrectomy for each study. We then used random-effects meta-analysis to assess whether this difference in logHR differed from the null using the χ2 test and quantified heterogeneity by I2 values. All reported P values are 2-sided, and P = 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance.

Subgroup analyses

2.6.

We performed prespecified subgroup analysis to determine whether the mechanisms of action of the checkpoint inhibitor, whether targeting PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) or PD-L1 (avelumab, atezolizumab), modified the effect of nephrectomy status on immunotherapy efficacy. In a post-hoc analysis, we subsequently performed further subgroup analyses to explore apparent differences in efficacy between anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 targeting agents as it relates to a prior history of nephrectomy.

Results

3.

Literature search results

3.1.

We identified a total of 334 unique references: 332 unique references from our systematic review and two abstracts identified by hand search of recent conference abstracts. After a full-text review of 16 manuscripts, 6 relevant clinical trials were included [Fig. A.1]. Nephrectomy status (reported consistently across all studies as “previous nephrectomy”) was coded as binary variable without additional details such as cytoreductive nephrectomy or the timing of nephrectomy.

Characteristics of identified trials

3.2.

Among 6 included trials, 5,121 patients were included in the analysis, of whom 3,968 (77%) underwent prior nephrectomy and 1153 (23%) did not. (Table A.1) lists the main characteristics of the 6 trials included in the meta-analysis. We obtained subgroup data by nephrectomy status directly from the sponsor for Checkmate 214 and Keynote 426. All 6 included studies were published since 2018. 5 studies examined the combination of immunotherapy with a VEGF-pathway targeted therapy as the experimental arm, while 1 study (CheckMate 214) examined an anti-PD-1 agent (nivolumab) with CTLA-4 blockade (ipilimumab). The proportion of IMDC poor risk patients across included studies ranged from 9% – 20%. Median age was typically in the seventh decade, comparable across studies, and median follow up was greater than 12 months in all studies.

Risk of bias

3.3.

Risk of bias of the included 6 trials is summarized in Supplementary Table B.2. All trials included random sequence generation with variable block sizes and were at low risk for selection bias. Generally, all studies were at low risk of attrition and reporting bias.

Primary analysis

3.4.

Meta-analysis of all studies included demonstrated significantly improved OS (HR 0.74, 95% CI: 0.66 −0.84, P <0.00001; I2 = 42%) and PFS (HR 0.64, 95% CI: 0.54 −0.76, P <0.00001; I2 = 79%) for patients undergoing immunotherapy-based therapies versus sunitinib.

There was an overall survival benefit for the use of immunotherapy-based therapies versus sunitinib for both patients that underwent prior nephrectomy (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.63 −0.88, P = 0.0005) and those that did not (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.59 −0.92, P = 0.006). There was no statistically significant difference in OS advantage by nephrectomy status (P = 0.70; I2 = 36%) [Fig. A.2]. Moderate heterogeneity was observed among those that underwent prior nephrectomy (Tau2 = 0.02, χ2 = 10.57, P = 0.06; I2 = 53%) and among patients that did not undergo a prior nephrectomy (Tau2 = 0.03, χ2 = 8.31, P = 0.14; I2 = 40%).

Assessing PFS, we again observed a benefit for immunotherapy-based approaches vs. sunitinib in both patients that underwent prior nephrectomy (HR 0.62, 95% CI: 0.48 −0.80, P = 0.0002) and those that did not (HR 0.69, 95% CI: 0.57 −0.83, P <0.0001). There was no statistically significant difference in PFS advantage by nephrectomy status (P = 0.45, I2 = 0%) [Fig. A.3] Heterogeneity was greater among those that underwent prior nephrectomy (Tau2 = 0.09, χ2 = 43.89, P <0.0001; I2 = 89%) than among patients that did not undergo a prior nephrectomy (Tau2 = 0.02, χ2 = 7.23, P = 0.2; I2 = 31%).

Finally, examining ORR among three trials with available data, we found benefit to immunotherapy-based approaches vs. sunitinib among both patients with a history of nephrectomy (odds ratio 1.92, 95% CI: 1.16 −31.7, P = 0.01) and without (odds ratio 2.88, 95% CI: 1.94 −4.28, P <0.00001) with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.21, I2 = 36%).

Subgroup analysis

3.5.

We performed a prespecified subgroup analysis examining whether the benefit of ICI-based therapy as compared to sunitinib in patients with or without prior nephrectomy differed when stratified by ICI mechanism of action [targeting PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) or PD-L1 (avelumab, atezolizumab)]. We observed an OS benefit in patients receiving anti-PD-1 ICI as compared to sunitinib in patients that underwent prior nephrectomy (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.59 −0.84) and those that did not (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.50 −0.78) [Table A.2]. In contrast, we did not observe a statistically significant OS benefit for the use of anti-PD-L1 agents vs. sunitinib for both patients that underwent prior nephrectomy (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.60 −1.18) and those that did not (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.74 −1.22). In each case, there was no evidence of effect modification on the basis a prior history of nephrectomy [Table A.2]. Similar findings were observed for PFS with respect to mechanism of action of ICIs [Table A.2].

In a post hoc fashion, we undertook further analyses to examine the apparent difference in efficacy between anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 agents. First, we examined the relative efficacy of anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 agents, irrespective of nephrectomy status. Assessing OS, we found somewhat higher efficacy, compared to sunitinib, for patients receiving anti-PD-1 therapy (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.63 −0.79) versus anti-PD-L1 therapy (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.75 −1.03) with marginal significance to this difference (P = 0.03, I2 = 79%). Such differences were attenuated and no longer significant when we examined PFS: anti-PD-1 (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.43 −0.83) and anti-PD-L1 therapy (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.64 −0.91) (test for subgroup differences, P = 0.21, I2 = 37.2%). Next, to assess whether there was a differential response to anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 agents that was mediated by the presence of the primary tumor being in situ, we examined the relationship between ICI-therapy, compared to sunitinib, stratified by immunotherapy target and nephrectomy status. As summarized in (Table A.3), we found evidence of a greater OS benefit to anti-PD-1 targeting agents, relative to anti-PD-L1 agents, vs. sunitinib among patients with the primary tumor in situ (test for subgroup differences, P = 0.01) while no difference was apparent among patients with a history of prior nephrectomy (test for subgroup differences, P = 0.37). While the trends were similar when assessing PFS, differences were not significant.

Discussion

4.

In this meta-analysis examining all contemporary randomized trials of immune checkpoint inhibition for first line metastatic RCC, we found a consistent OS and PFS benefit irrespective of nephrectomy status. Furthermore, we did not find evidence of effect modification by a history of nephrectomy on the efficacy of ICI-based therapies when stratified by anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 agent. However, in exploratory and hypothesis-generating subgroup analyses, we demonstrated evidence that patients without a prior history of nephrectomy (with primary tumor in situ) may derive greater benefit from anti-PD-1 therapies than anti-PD-L1 therapies because of its inherent molecular mechanisms between anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1. The putative mechanism that underscores the therapeutic difference between anti-PD-1/PD-L1 may derive from the ability of PD-1 antibodies to deter inhibitory interactions with both of its ligands—PD-L1/PD-L2, while PD-L1 antibodies lack the inhibition on PD-L2 pathway [16].

These findings are in contrast to those predicted from experimental studies suggesting that the presence or absence of the primary tumor may modulate the response to ICI therapy [10–12]. The timing of prior nephrectomy (whether cytoreductive or nephrectomy prior to metachronous metastasis) is not delineated in the data reported by studies included in our meta-analysis. Immunotherapy given shortly after cytoreductive nephrectomy may impact local and systemic immune responses differently than immunotherapy given for metachronous metastasis after the primary tumor had long since been resected. Furthermore, these data cannot be extrapolated to the circumstance of synchronous mRCC to infer whether there is a survival benefit to cytoreductive nephrectomy in the immunotherapy era. The role of cytoreductive nephrectomy in newly diagnosed mRCC remains unclear and with relevant trials underway (NORDIC-SUN/ NCT03977571, PROBE/ NCT04510597).

Among included studies, approximately 80% of patients had a prior nephrectomy: either via cytoreductive nephrectomy prior to trial enrollment or had metachronous metastasis after primary resection. This proportion is consistent with the IMDC multi-center prospective cohort, in which 79% of patients had received a nephrectomy [17,18]. Studies from SEER indicate that a significant proportion of patients presenting with synchronous metastasis (68%) do indeed go onto receive nephrectomy, which supports the generalizability of these results [19].

The results of this meta-analysis imply that patients who had already received nephrectomy did not respond differently to ICI than those who had not, but further work is required to better delineate sub-populations where there may be a potential for improved ICI response. This study has limitations in that the data available for the meta-analysis lacks granular details such timing of nephrectomy (cytoreductive vs. previous), IMDC risk stratification, proportion of localized disease vs. metastatic (disease burden and impact of nephrectomy), and the proportion of patients that underwent deferred cytoreductive nephrectomy. Future efforts are needed by way of individual patient data meta-analysis to better identify subgroups where ICI response may indeed differ by nephrectomy status.

The strengths of our meta-analysis include the strict methodologic inclusion criteria requiring peer-reviewed randomized controlled trials comparing ICI therapy to a targeted therapy control, inclusion of all approved ICI agents, and the rigorous, up-to-date search strategy. To ensure complete examination of all contemporary studies we combined published studies with data from study sponsors.

Conclusion

5.

In conclusion, these data show that prior nephrectomy does not appear to impact the efficacy of ICI in mRCC. Identifying novel subgroups where surgical resection may favourably modulate the systemic immune response is important for improving ICI efficacy and resistance while limiting the risks of surgical complications in patients unlikely to benefit.
