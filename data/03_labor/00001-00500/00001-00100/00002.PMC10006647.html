Effect of exercise on rehabilitation of breast cancer surgery patients: A systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials

Abstract

Aim

To determine the effective exercise methods for different complications of breast cancer patients after surgery.

Design

Systematic review and meta‐analysis.

Methods

A comprehensive electronic search was carried out with no time limit until May 2020. Studies must have been randomized controlled trials of patients after breast cancer surgery, without limit to the way of exercise. Literature quality was evaluated by the modified Jadad scale. The meta‐analysis was conducted with CMA2.0 software.

Results

Aerobic exercise reduced the intensity of the pain (MD = −1.043), improved shoulder flexion (MD = 3.398) and internal rotation range (MD = 3.868), lessened upper limb dysfunction (MD = −5.231) and improved muscle strength during flexion (MD = 1.076) and abduction (MD = 0.991). Shoulder elbow movement improved the range of shoulder external rotation (MD = 2.691) and reduced the incidence of arm lymphedema (RR = 0.343). Anti‐resistance exercise also lessened upper limb dysfunction (MD = − 4.094).

INTRODUCTION

1

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignant tumour in women (Sung et al., 2021). According to the WHO in 2018, 11.6% of the number of new cancers worldwide were breast cancer (Bray et al., 2018). Breast cancer accounts for 13% of all cancer patients aged 15–49 (Mattiuzzi & Lippi, 2019). Early screening, diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer standardization reduce the incidence rate of breast cancer‐related mortality and raise the 5‐year survival rate. The overall 5‐year survival rates of stage I, II and III patients were 98%, 92% and 75% respectively (DeSantis et al., 2019). Therefore, the number of breast cancer survivors will continue to increase.

Radical surgery (Bogach et al., 2022) supplemented by chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy and other therapies (Bhat et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020) is the main treatment for BC. Radical surgery for BC primarily includes BC standard radical surgery (Halsted surgery), BC modified radical surgery, simple mastectomy, breast‐conserving surgery, axillary lymph node dissection and sentinel lymph node biopsy (Grebić et al., 2021). Although the treatment of BC has developed from extensive radical resection to less invasive treatment, the postoperative complications still, mainly including lymphedema, pain, upper limb dysfunction, etc., which seriously affect patient quality of life (QOL) (Dams et al., 2022; Ghanei Gheshlagh et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022). According to studies the incidence of lymphedema is 4.4%–32.5% (Zou et al., 2018) and it is closely associated with axillary lymph node dissection and anterior lymph node biopsy (Cormier et al., 2010; McLaughlin et al., 2008). Lymphedema is mainly due to the loss or obstruction of lymphatic vessels caused by the operation, the impeded or blocked lymph return and retention in the tissue space, resulting in a series of pathological and clinical changes such as tissue swelling, chronic inflammation and even tissue fibrosis at relevant parts, resulting in secondary oedema of the affected upper limb (Kong & Yang, 2020). A systematic review of 3,764 patients showed that the prevalence of postoperative pain in BC patients was 29.8% (Wang et al., 2018). The occurrence and development of postoperative pain are related to many factors, such as surgical injury, age, psychosocial factors, genetic factors, neurosensitization and so on (Chen et al., 2016). The incidence of shoulder motion disorder after breast cancer surgery was 2% ~ 51% (McNeely et al., 2010). Postoperative patients' lack of exercise due to fear of pain and affecting wound recovery leads to the poor activity of upper limbs and shoulder joints, which often leads to limited activity and dysfunction of affected limbs (Malicka et al., 2009; Shamley et al., 2014).

BACKGROUND

2

As early as the 1990s, rehabilitation exercise was used after breast cancer surgery, and exercise proved to be beneficial for recovery (Zang, 1992). Currently, increasingly diversified rehabilitation exercises are being used. The methods include resistance exercise, shoulder and elbow exercise, lymphatic drainage, aerobic exercise, physiotherapy and massage, etc. Exercise is often used to promote recovery following breast cancer surgery. Some research suggests that aerobic exercise can improve the range of motion of the shoulder (Park, 2017; Shen, 2017; Zengin Alpozgen et al., 2017). This reveals the importance of aerobic exercise. But another study involving 28 researchers showed that it had no effect on improving shoulder range of motion (Loudon et al., 2016). In recent years, the resistance movement has been found to relieve pain. Although previous research demonstrated resistance movements are effective (Chen et al., 2019; Park, 2017), some studies revealed that such interventions have not achieved significant results (Ammitzbøll et al., 2020), researchers speculate that this may be affected by the insufficient intensity of resistance exercise interventions and differences exercise content also lead to different effects. Therefore, the intervention effect of different exercise types on postoperative complications of breast cancer is still unclear.

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) took the lead in formulating the first edition of exercise guidelines for cancer survivors (hereinafter referred to as the 2010 guideline) in 2010 (Schmitz et al., 2010). However, due to the lack of evidence, it was unable to provide special exercise prescriptions for specific cancers. In order to make up for the shortcomings of the 2010 guideline, ACSM launched the 2019 guideline in 2019 and gave targeted exercise suggestions for specific cancer‐related side effects. For example, for the improvement of lymphedema, the guidelines recommended resistance exercise, while for the improvement of sleep, the guidelines recommended aerobic exercise (Campbell et al., 2019). The 2019 guideline provides new ideas for our clinical work. First of all, since the guidelines are universal and not specific to specific diseases, the effectiveness and safety of exercise for breast cancer patients still need to be further verified. Secondly, there are a few kinds of cancer‐related side effects involved in the guidelines, and most of the exercise effects are still under exploration. The research question of this study is: what are the effective rehabilitation exercises for different complications of breast cancer patients after surgery? Therefore, we conducted a systematic evaluation and meta‐analysis to find out the effective exercise methods for different complications of breast cancer patients after surgery.

DESIGN

3

Systematic review and meta‐analysis.

METHODS

4

The protocol for this systematic review was registered on INPLASY (INPLASY2022100065) and is available in full on inplasy.com (https://doi.org/10.37766/inplasy2022.10.0065).

Search strategy

4.1

We searched Chinese and English literature from the inception of the databases to May 2020. China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform, VIP China Science and Technology Journal Database, China Biology Medicine, EMBASE and PubMed databases were searched. Combinations of breast cancer (‘breast tumor’, ‘breast carcinoma’, ‘mammary carcinoma’, ‘breast neoplasm’) and rehabilitation exercise (‘exercise’, ‘physical therapy’) were employed when screening abstracts/keywords of articles. Two researchers independently searched, read the title and abstract of the literature, read the full text of the preliminary included literature and extracted the data. In case of divergence, a third researcher was consulted.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

4.2

The inclusion criteria were formulated based on the PICOS (population, intervention, comparison, outcome and study design) framework. Participants (P): patients after breast cancer surgery; Intervention (I): used exercise intervention in the experimental group; Comparison (C): involved a comparison with no intervention or usual care; Outcomes (O): Studies resulted in the assessment of one or more of the following quantitative outcome indicators — pain, lymphedema, quality of life and functional status; Study design (S): randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Languages were restricted to English and Chinese. Exclusion criteria: (1) the results and intervention measures were not clear; (2) the quality of literature was lower than three points; (3) the publications were repeated and (4) there was no full text.

Data extraction

4.3

Data extraction was performed by one investigator and reviewed by another investigator. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third investigator until a consensus was reached. The following information was extracted from included studies: author, publication year, sample size, intervention measures, surgery methods, intervention intensity, intervention time and outcome indicators.

Quality of evidence

4.4

The modified Jadad scale (Jadad et al., 1996) was used to evaluate the quality of the included literature. The total score was 7 points, 1–3 points for low‐quality literature and 4–7 points for high‐quality literature. Low‐quality literature was excluded.

Statistical analysis

4.5

CMA2.0 software was used for meta‐analysis. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed by the I 2‐heterogeneity test. p < .10, I 2 ≥ 60%, indicating high heterogeneity, and a random effect model was selected; p ≥ .10, I 2 < 60%, indicating low heterogeneity, a fixed effect model was selected to draw the tforest map. We perform the funnel plot or Egger's test to evaluate publication bias.

RESULTS

5

Study selection

5.1

Figure 1 shows a flow diagram of the literature search and selection processes. We detected 2,069 related articles. EndNote™ software identified 892 repeated publications, which were deleted. Another 1,059 articles did not meet the inclusion criteria. An additional 18 publications either did not contain the full text, did not indicate specific measures, were unrelated to the research, or had unlimited indicators. After further literature quality evaluation, 68 low‐quality reports were excluded. Data from 32 papers were extracted, among which 15 papers could not be analysed by meta‐analysis because of inconsistent intervention measures or evaluation indicators. Finally, we included 17 papers for meta‐analysis. The basic characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1. The Jadad scores of the included studies were all above 4; thus, the articles were all the high quality (Table 2).

Effects of exercise on pain

5.2

Five studies (Chen et al., 2019; Harder et al., 2015; Loudon et al., 2014; Park, 2017; Zengin Alpozgen et al., 2017) examined the effects of exercise on pain; there were 142 participants in experimental groups, and 145 participants were in control groups. The assessment tool for pain varied and included visual analogue (VAS) 0–10 scale or VAS 0–100 scale. Thus, we converted all the scales to a 0–10‐point scale. Four studies (Chen et al., 2019; Harder et al., 2015; Park, 2017; Zengin Alpozgen et al., 2017) examined showed that aerobic exercise significantly reduced pain (MD = −1.043, 95% CI = −1.290 to −0.795, p = .000, I 2 = 40.655; Figure 2). Only two studies (Loudon et al., 2014; Park, 2017) failed to show a statistically significant difference in pain with resistance exercise (MD = −0.466, 95% CI = −1.729 to 0.797, p = .470, I 2 = 95.954; Figure 2).

Effects of exercise on the range of motion (ROM)

5.3

Effect of aerobic exercise on the range of motion

5.3.1

Four reports (Loudon et al., 2016; Park, 2017; Shen, 2017; Zengin Alpozgen et al., 2017) describe the effects of aerobic exercise on the range of motion. There were 95 participants in experimental groups, and 92 participants were in control groups. The range of motion of the shoulder includes shoulder abduction range, shoulder joint flexion range, shoulder extension range, shoulder internal rotation range and shoulder external rotation range. Each aspect needs to be analysed separately. The studies showed that aerobic exercise improved the shoulder internal rotation range (MD = 3.868, 95% CI = 2.151–5.585, p = .000, I 2 = 0.000; Figure 3) and shoulder flexion range (MD = 3.398, 95% CI = 1.556–5.240, p = .000, I 2 = 0.000; Figure 3). However, there was no significant difference between aerobic exercise and control groups in the range of motion for shoulder abduction (MD = 4.432, 95% CI = −3.185 to 12.050, p = .254, I 2 = 88.877; Figure 4), shoulder external rotation (MD = 4.873, 95% CI = −4.856 to 14.602, p = .326, I 2 = 87.916; Figure 4) and shoulder extension (MD = 4.256, 95% CI = −1.801 to 10.330, p = .168, I 2 = 95.457; Figure 4).

Effect of shoulder and elbow exercise on the range of motion

5.4

We included only two randomized controlled trials (Kilbreath et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2005), but the sample size was large. There were 221 participants in the shoulder and elbow exercise groups and 139 participants in the control groups. We could analyse only the shoulder abduction range and the shoulder external rotation range. Shoulder and elbow exercise significantly improve shoulder external rotation range (MD = 2.691, 95% CI = 2.389–2.992, p = .000, I 2 = 0.000; Figure 5). However, there was no significant difference in the shoulder abduction range (MD = 10.353, 95% CI = −19.535 to 40.242, p = .497, I 2 = 99.294; Figure 5).

Effects of exercise on upper extremity disability and symptoms

5.5

There were five randomized controlled trials (Harder et al., 2015; Hayes et al., 2013; Li, 2019; Shen, 2017; Zengin Alpozgen et al., 2017) that investigated the effects of upper extremity disability and symptoms. The disabilities of the arm, shoulder and hand (DASH) score were used to evaluate the impact on upper extremity disability and symptoms, and total scores ranged from 0–100; a high score indicated greater disability. Two trials (Harder et al., 2015; Li, 2019) assessed resistance exercise, and four trials (Harder et al., 2015; Hayes et al., 2013; Shen, 2017; Zengin Alpozgen et al., 2017) evaluated aerobic exercise. In all, there were 259 participants in the experimental groups and 184 participants in the control groups. Resistance exercise (MD = −4.094, 95% CI = −7.901 to −0.286, p = .035, I 2 = 55.502; Figure 6) and aerobic exercise (MD = −5.231, 95% CI = − 8.028 to −2.434, p = .000, I 2 = 0.000; Figure 6) both improved upper extremity ability and lessened symptoms.

Effects of exercise on arm lymphedema

5.6

Effect of manual lymphatic drainage and aerobic exercise on the volume of arm lymphedema

5.6.1

Four studies (Loudon et al., 2016; Pasyar et al., 2019; Tambour et al., 2018; Williams et al., 2002) assessed the volume of arm lymphedema. Compared with the control group, manual lymphatic drainage (MD = − 22.628, 95% CI = −67.940 to 22.684, p = .328, I 2 = 35.519; Figure 7) and aerobic exercise (MD = 9.935, 95% CI = −54.043 to 73.913, p = .761, I 2 = 0.000; Figure 7) did not show a significant difference.

Effect of shoulder and elbow exercise on incidence of arm lymphedema

5.6.2

In four studies (Kilbreath et al., 2012; Todd et al., 2008; Torres Lacomba et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2005), the effects of shoulder and elbow exercise on incidence of arm lymphedema were compared with no exercise. There were 338 participants in the shoulder and elbow exercise group and 254 participants in the control group. Patients in the shoulder and elbow exercise group were less likely to experience arm lymphedema (RR = 0.343, 95% CI = 0.207–0.569, p = .000, I 2 = 12.846; Figure 7).

Effects of exercise on muscle strength

5.7

We included three studies (He et al., 2011; Loudon et al., 2014; Zengin Alpozgen et al., 2017) to evaluate the effects of aerobic exercise on muscle strength. These studies included 84 participants in the shoulder and elbow exercise group and 78 participants in the control group. Aerobic exercise was found to improve muscle strength during flexion (MD = 1.076, 95% CI = 0.330–1.823, p = .005, I 2 = 26.046; Figure 8). Two of these three studies (Loudon et al., 2014; Zengin Alpozgen et al., 2017) showed that aerobic exercise could improve abduction muscle strength (MD = 0.991, 95% CI = 0.134–1.849, p = .023, I 2 = 38.964; Figure 8).

DISCUSSION

6

Postoperative complications of breast cancer have a high incidence. Many exercise approaches have been used to reduce the impact of complications. In this systematic review and meta‐analysis, we evaluated the effectiveness of different exercise approaches on postoperative complications of breast cancer. The systematic review included 17 randomized controlled studies. The methods used to evaluate postoperative complications of breast cancer were diverse among the studies and included VAS, ROM, DASH and muscle strength. The choice of exercise depends on the type of complication, and the different exercises may have certain advantages.

Reduce postoperative pain

6.1

Aerobic exercise can reduce the intensity of pain. There are many reasons for postoperative pain from breast cancer surgery. Age and BMI may be risk factors(van Helmond et al., 2017). Of course, age cannot be subject to intervention, but it is possible to reduce the BMI of patients. Kelley et al. reported that Aerobic exercise can reduce BMI (Kelley et al., 2017); thus, patients with appropriate aerobic exercise after surgery can reduce postoperative pain by reducing BMI. In addition, when performing aerobic exercise, the pituitary gland secretes the opioid neuropeptide endorphin (Grossman & Sutton, 1985), in which β‐endorphin has a variety of functions, such as pain relief and anti‐inflammatory effects(Shrihari, 2019). Therefore, clinicians can guide patients with pain from breast cancer surgery to perform aerobic exercise, particularly patients with high BMI. In addition, Paolucci et al. reported that shoulder and elbow movement is equally effective for pain relief (Paolucci et al., 2021); however, no similar study or meta‐analysis had been conducted to confirm this report. Meta‐analysis shows that resistance exercise has no effect on pain. That is, anti‐resistance exercise is safe for patients with pain, and it will not increase the risk of pain.

Improve the range of motion of the shoulder joint

6.2

Aerobic exercise can improve the range of motion of shoulder flexion and internal rotation, and shoulder and elbow exercise can improve the range of motion of shoulder external rotation. According to the results of our meta‐analysis, aerobic exercise combined with shoulder elbow movement can improve the range of motion of shoulder joints.

Reduce upper limb dysfunction

6.3

Both resistance exercise and aerobic exercise can improve upper limb dysfunction. Pain is the main factor of upper limb dysfunction in breast cancer patients (De Groef et al., 2017). Aerobic exercise can reduce pain. When the pain is relieved, the patient dares to move his upper limbs, so it can improve the symptoms of upper limb dysfunction. Anti‐resistance exercise will not increase the intensity of pain. In addition, resistance exercise can prevent muscle atrophy (Oates et al., 2010), prevent muscle mass decline, and improve upper limb function. For patients with pain and upper limb dysfunction, we recommend aerobic exercise because aerobic exercise can reduce pain and improve upper limb function. Patients with simple upper limb dysfunction can choose an aerobic exercise or resistance exercise according to preference. By allowing patients to choose, we can improve the enthusiasm of patients to complete the exercise program.

Reduce arm lymphedema

6.4

Manual lymphatic drainage and aerobic exercise do not contribute to reducing oedema volume. Shoulder and elbow movement can reduce the incidence of lymphedema. Therefore, for patients who have not developed lymphedema after surgery, targeted shoulder and elbow movement can be performed early to prevent lymphedema; prevention is the key to reducing the occurrence of lymphedema (Heller et al., 2019). The 2019 Guideline suggests that the lymphedema of cancer patients should be alleviated through resistance exercise (Campbell et al., 2019). However, this study cannot verify the effect of resistance exercise because there is not enough high‐quality literature. It is suggested that relevant studies can compare the effect of resistance exercise and resistance plus shoulder and elbow exercise on lymphedema.

Enhance muscle strength

6.5

This conclusion is different from the recommendations of the 2019 Guideline. The 2019 Guideline suggests increasing the muscle strength of cancer patients through resistance exercise, while the effect of aerobic exercise or aerobic plus resistance exercise remains to be verified (Campbell et al., 2019). The results of this study provide favourable evidence to support that aerobic exercise can improve the muscle strength of cancer patients. However, only three randomized controlled trials were included in this study, and more studies are needed to verify this view in the later stage.

LIMITATIONS

7

Although a large number of publications were included in this study, there were still 15 articles that could not be analysed because of inconsistent intervention measures or evaluation indicators.

CONCLUSIONS

8

To prevent lymphedema, shoulder and elbow exercise should be chosen first. When patients have pain, upper limb dysfunction and low muscle strength, aerobic exercise can solve these problems. The best way to improve the range of motion of the shoulder joint is aerobic exercise combined with shoulder and elbow exercise. If the patient has only upper limb dysfunction, it can be solved by aerobic exercise or resistance exercise alone. In sum, according to the different postoperative complications, different intervention measures and targeted rehabilitation programs can be put forward. This review and meta‐analysis confirm that the benefits of exercise after breast cancer surgery depend on the type of exercise and the particular medical complication. Our findings can be used to guide clinical care. Because of differences in exercise methods, the time after surgery when exercise is initiated, and the duration of exercise differ among researchers, it is necessary to refine and standardize exercise programs.

## INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignant tumour in women (Sung et al., 2021). According to the WHO in 2018, 11.6% of the number of new cancers worldwide were breast cancer (Bray et al., 2018). Breast cancer accounts for 13% of all cancer patients aged 15–49 (Mattiuzzi & Lippi, 2019). Early screening, diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer standardization reduce the incidence rate of breast cancer‐related mortality and raise the 5‐year survival rate. The overall 5‐year survival rates of stage I, II and III patients were 98%, 92% and 75% respectively (DeSantis et al., 2019). Therefore, the number of breast cancer survivors will continue to increase.

Radical surgery (Bogach et al., 2022) supplemented by chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy and other therapies (Bhat et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020) is the main treatment for BC. Radical surgery for BC primarily includes BC standard radical surgery (Halsted surgery), BC modified radical surgery, simple mastectomy, breast‐conserving surgery, axillary lymph node dissection and sentinel lymph node biopsy (Grebić et al., 2021). Although the treatment of BC has developed from extensive radical resection to less invasive treatment, the postoperative complications still, mainly including lymphedema, pain, upper limb dysfunction, etc., which seriously affect patient quality of life (QOL) (Dams et al., 2022; Ghanei Gheshlagh et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022). According to studies the incidence of lymphedema is 4.4%–32.5% (Zou et al., 2018) and it is closely associated with axillary lymph node dissection and anterior lymph node biopsy (Cormier et al., 2010; McLaughlin et al., 2008). Lymphedema is mainly due to the loss or obstruction of lymphatic vessels caused by the operation, the impeded or blocked lymph return and retention in the tissue space, resulting in a series of pathological and clinical changes such as tissue swelling, chronic inflammation and even tissue fibrosis at relevant parts, resulting in secondary oedema of the affected upper limb (Kong & Yang, 2020). A systematic review of 3,764 patients showed that the prevalence of postoperative pain in BC patients was 29.8% (Wang et al., 2018). The occurrence and development of postoperative pain are related to many factors, such as surgical injury, age, psychosocial factors, genetic factors, neurosensitization and so on (Chen et al., 2016). The incidence of shoulder motion disorder after breast cancer surgery was 2% ~ 51% (McNeely et al., 2010). Postoperative patients' lack of exercise due to fear of pain and affecting wound recovery leads to the poor activity of upper limbs and shoulder joints, which often leads to limited activity and dysfunction of affected limbs (Malicka et al., 2009; Shamley et al., 2014).

## BACKGROUND

As early as the 1990s, rehabilitation exercise was used after breast cancer surgery, and exercise proved to be beneficial for recovery (Zang, 1992). Currently, increasingly diversified rehabilitation exercises are being used. The methods include resistance exercise, shoulder and elbow exercise, lymphatic drainage, aerobic exercise, physiotherapy and massage, etc. Exercise is often used to promote recovery following breast cancer surgery. Some research suggests that aerobic exercise can improve the range of motion of the shoulder (Park, 2017; Shen, 2017; Zengin Alpozgen et al., 2017). This reveals the importance of aerobic exercise. But another study involving 28 researchers showed that it had no effect on improving shoulder range of motion (Loudon et al., 2016). In recent years, the resistance movement has been found to relieve pain. Although previous research demonstrated resistance movements are effective (Chen et al., 2019; Park, 2017), some studies revealed that such interventions have not achieved significant results (Ammitzbøll et al., 2020), researchers speculate that this may be affected by the insufficient intensity of resistance exercise interventions and differences exercise content also lead to different effects. Therefore, the intervention effect of different exercise types on postoperative complications of breast cancer is still unclear.

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) took the lead in formulating the first edition of exercise guidelines for cancer survivors (hereinafter referred to as the 2010 guideline) in 2010 (Schmitz et al., 2010). However, due to the lack of evidence, it was unable to provide special exercise prescriptions for specific cancers. In order to make up for the shortcomings of the 2010 guideline, ACSM launched the 2019 guideline in 2019 and gave targeted exercise suggestions for specific cancer‐related side effects. For example, for the improvement of lymphedema, the guidelines recommended resistance exercise, while for the improvement of sleep, the guidelines recommended aerobic exercise (Campbell et al., 2019). The 2019 guideline provides new ideas for our clinical work. First of all, since the guidelines are universal and not specific to specific diseases, the effectiveness and safety of exercise for breast cancer patients still need to be further verified. Secondly, there are a few kinds of cancer‐related side effects involved in the guidelines, and most of the exercise effects are still under exploration. The research question of this study is: what are the effective rehabilitation exercises for different complications of breast cancer patients after surgery? Therefore, we conducted a systematic evaluation and meta‐analysis to find out the effective exercise methods for different complications of breast cancer patients after surgery.

## DESIGN

Systematic review and meta‐analysis.
## METHODS

The protocol for this systematic review was registered on INPLASY (INPLASY2022100065) and is available in full on inplasy.com (https://doi.org/10.37766/inplasy2022.10.0065).

### Search strategy

We searched Chinese and English literature from the inception of the databases to May 2020. China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform, VIP China Science and Technology Journal Database, China Biology Medicine, EMBASE and PubMed databases were searched. Combinations of breast cancer (‘breast tumor’, ‘breast carcinoma’, ‘mammary carcinoma’, ‘breast neoplasm’) and rehabilitation exercise (‘exercise’, ‘physical therapy’) were employed when screening abstracts/keywords of articles. Two researchers independently searched, read the title and abstract of the literature, read the full text of the preliminary included literature and extracted the data. In case of divergence, a third researcher was consulted.

### Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were formulated based on the PICOS (population, intervention, comparison, outcome and study design) framework. Participants (P): patients after breast cancer surgery; Intervention (I): used exercise intervention in the experimental group; Comparison (C): involved a comparison with no intervention or usual care; Outcomes (O): Studies resulted in the assessment of one or more of the following quantitative outcome indicators — pain, lymphedema, quality of life and functional status; Study design (S): randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Languages were restricted to English and Chinese. Exclusion criteria: (1) the results and intervention measures were not clear; (2) the quality of literature was lower than three points; (3) the publications were repeated and (4) there was no full text.

### Data extraction

Data extraction was performed by one investigator and reviewed by another investigator. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third investigator until a consensus was reached. The following information was extracted from included studies: author, publication year, sample size, intervention measures, surgery methods, intervention intensity, intervention time and outcome indicators.

### Quality of evidence

The modified Jadad scale (Jadad et al., 1996) was used to evaluate the quality of the included literature. The total score was 7 points, 1–3 points for low‐quality literature and 4–7 points for high‐quality literature. Low‐quality literature was excluded.

### Statistical analysis

CMA2.0 software was used for meta‐analysis. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed by the I 2‐heterogeneity test. p < .10, I 2 ≥ 60%, indicating high heterogeneity, and a random effect model was selected; p ≥ .10, I 2 < 60%, indicating low heterogeneity, a fixed effect model was selected to draw the tforest map. We perform the funnel plot or Egger's test to evaluate publication bias.
