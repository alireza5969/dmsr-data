Surgical Management of Bilateral Vocal Fold Paralysis in Children: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Ryan Kendall Thorpe[1]

Sohit Paul Kanotra[1][2]

[1.] Department of Otolaryngology – Head and Neck Surgery, University of Iowa

[2.] University of Iowa Stead Family Children's Hospital

Abstract

OBJECTIVES:

To examine and compare the outcomes of various glottic widening surgeries (GWS) for initial management of bilateral vocal fold paralysis (BVFP) in children, the outcomes of different GWS in children who underwent initial tracheostomy, and the rate of decannulation in children who underwent tracheostomy alone versus tracheostomy followed by GWS.

DATA SOURCES:

PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane libraries, and Embase were searched following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines on September 9th, 2019 with no date restriction.

REVIEW METHODS:

Articles focusing on GWS or tracheostomy for initial management of BVFP were included. Articles describing patients who received no surgical intervention for BVFP were excluded.

RESULTS:

5,989 articles were reviewed. 67 articles met inclusion criteria. 240 patients were incorporated into the analysis. Patients who underwent primary GWS had an eventual tracheostomy rate of 6.0% (5/83). There were no statistically significant differences in the rate of tracheostomy, reoperation, or mortality between cricoid split, suture lateralization, and cordectomy/cordotomy. Patients who underwent primary tracheostomy failed to achieve decannulation in 36.9% (58/157) of cases. Decannulation was more likely in tracheostomized children who received GWS compared to those who did not (odds ratio = 6.336, p = <0.0001).

CONCLUSIONS:

Most children who undergo primary GWS for BVFP avoid tracheostomy or reoperation. These data demonstrated no differences in surgical outcomes between the most common GWS for BVFP. For children who receive a tracheostomy as their first intervention for BVFP, GWS is associated with a significantly improved rate of decannulation.

INTRODUCTION:

1.

RATIONALE:

1.1

Bilateral vocal fold paralysis (BVFP) is a rare cause of respiratory distress in children. Clinical presentation is variable, ranging from mild inspiratory stridor to catastrophic airway compromise.1 The natural history of BVFP depends on its etiology, including trauma, neurological disorders such as Arnold-Chiari malformation, hydrocephalus and cerebral palsy, hypoxia, iatrogenic causes (e.g., related to intubation or surgery), or idiopathic/unknown.

Pediatric BVFP is most commonly iatrogenic or idiopathic in origin, with a favorable outcome occurring in the majority of such cases.1-3 In a long term study of 102 pediatric patients with vocal cord paralysis1, 28 were identified with idiopathic vocal cord paralysis, of which 18 (64%) recovered within a range of 6 months to 11 years while 26 had iatrogenic vocal fold paralysis, of which 12 (46%) recovered within 5 years. Historically, the rate of tracheostomy in neonates with BVFP has been reported to be 50-90%.4-6 Though tracheostomy remains an essential component of management for BVFP in children with acute airway compromise and decompensation, it carries a high incidence of serious complications including pneumothorax, accidental decannulation, and laryngeal/tracheal stenosis.7 During the mid-1990’s, management of BVFP in children began to favor less invasive interventions to provide an adequate airway for ventilation while minimizing adverse effects such as aspiration and dysphonia.8

New techniques in airway surgery have been developed and refined over the past several decades, offering additional treatment modalities for pediatric otolaryngologists in the management of BVFP.9 These include injection of botulinum toxin (Botox) to the cricothyroid muscle and surgical intervention, such as arytenoidectomy, cordotomy, laterofixation, recurrent laryngeal nerve reinnervation and cricoid split with costal cartilage grafting.10 Though these surgeries generally result in permanent alterations to the patient’s laryngeal anatomy and do not restore normal vocal fold function, complications are less commonly seen and less severe than those seen with tracheostomy. To date, no study has demonstrated superiority of one technique over another. The multitude of surgical options reflect an absence of consensus regarding treatment.

Surgery for pediatric BVFP has previously been studied as part of a systematic review and meta-analysis in 2002.11 However, this study only examined patients who had already received a tracheostomy. With the development of novel surgical techniques and the focus on avoiding tracheostomy as initial management of pediatric BVFP, a re-examination of the literature is needed. Our objectives were to examine and compare the outcomes of various glottic widening surgeries (GWS) as the initial management of BVFP in children, the outcomes of different GWS in children who underwent tracheostomy as an initial procedure, and the rate of decannulation in children who underwent tracheostomy alone for BVFP compared with tracheostomy followed by GWS.

METHODS:

2.

The literature search for this study was based on the protocols described in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines and statements.12 Studies were identified via review by the two authors by searching the PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane library, and Embase databases from their inception to September 9th, 2019, and any disagreements in potential studies were resolved by consensus. A librarian assisted in the planning and development of terms for the computerized search (Table 1). The title and the abstract were screened to determine eligibility based on predefined inclusion criteria, which are outlined in detail below. Clearly irrelevant studies were excluded at this stage. The references of the articles were examined for additional studies that could be included in this analysis.

INCLUSION AND EXCLUSION CRITERIA:

2.1

Inclusion criteria: 1. All primary research studies, including case reports, case series, retrospective studies, and prospective studies examining outcomes of surgery for management of BVFP. 2. The study must have included data on the pediatric population (defined as <18 years of age). 3. Post-operative follow-up of the patients must have been available. 4. The study must have been reported in the English language. 5. The study was not a duplicate study or a study from the same data set.

Exclusion criteria: 1. Letters, quizzes, or other review articles. 2. Studies which only reviewed unilateral vocal fold paralysis. 3. Articles in which previously tracheostomized patients versus non-tracheostomized patients, adult versus pediatric patients, or unilateral versus bilateral vocal fold paralysis patients could not be distinguished.

DATA EXTRACTION SHEET:

2.2

After identification of eligible articles, the authors developed a data extraction sheet and pilot-tested it on 5 of the articles and refined it accordingly. RKT extracted the following data, and SPK checked it. We extracted author information, hospital type, study design, sample size, patient age at diagnosis of BVFP, patient age at initial surgery for BVFP, etiology of BVFP, reason for airway surgery, type of initial airway surgery, additional airway findings, any additional procedures performed, duration of follow-up, and postoperative outcomes, including necessity of tracheostomy, operative revision, or mortality. Outcome measures were the need for tracheostomy (or decannulation if tracheostomy was performed first), need for revision surgery, and mortality. In cases where an article included both eligible and ineligible patients, only information regarding the eligible patients was collected.

QUALITY OF ARTICLES:

2.3

Assessment of level of evidence for each identified article was performed with the Oxford Center for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels (OCEBM).13 This scoring system was used to report overall scores from highest level of evidence (1) to lowest level of evidence (5) for each article that met inclusion criteria.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS:

2.4

To test for differences in tracheostomy or decannulation rate, surgical revision rate, and mortality rate between different types of GWS, we used the generalized linear mixed modeling (GLMM) framework with a logit link. A random effect model was included to account for study clustering. This analysis was conducted using SAS 9.4 software (Cary, NC). To test for differences in decannulation rates amongst tracheostomized patients who underwent no further airway intervention versus those who underwent GWS after tracheostomy, we used Fisher’s exact test, conducted with GraphPad Prism 8.4.2 software (San Diego, CA).

RESULTS:

3.

5,989 articles were identified based on initial review, with 1,891 articles from PubMed, 3,455 from Web of Science, 305 from the Cochrane library, and 338 from Embase. 67 valid studies were identified.3,4,10,14-77 A full list of these studies along with their characteristics are recorded in Supplemental Table S1 and S2. An overview of our methodology is described in Figure 1. Of the studies included, all were either case reports (23/67) or case series (44/67), resulting in an OCEBM level of 4 for all studies. A total of 240 patients were included.

BILATERAL VOCAL FOLD PARALYSIS CAUSES, PRESENTATION, AND INITIAL INTERVENTION:

3.1.

Etiology of the BVFP was reported for 224 patients, of which 131 (58.5%) were idiopathic/unknown, 61 (27.2%) were due to a neuromuscular or neurologic disorder, 12 (5.4%) resulted from iatrogenic injuries to the bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerves (secondary to cardiac, esophageal, or upper airway surgery), 7 (3.1%) were due to birth trauma, 7 (3.1%) were due to prolonged intubation, 5 (2.2%) resulted from direct trauma to the vocal cords, and 1 (0.4%) was related to the administration of vincristine.

For their initial airway intervention, 157/240 patients (65.4%) underwent tracheostomy and 83 patients (34.6%) underwent GWS. Indications for initial airway intervention were reported for 143 patients, of which 62 (43.4%) had progressive respiratory distress, 56 (39.2%) failed extubation, 13 (9.1%) had persistent stridor, 7 (4.9%) had failure to thrive, 2 (1.4%) had a tracheostomy performed immediately after iatrogenic injury to the bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerves, 2 (1.4%) were unable to be intubated and subsequently underwent emergency tracheostomy, and 1 (0.7%) had recurrent apneas at night. These findings are summarized and stratified by initial airway intervention in Table 2.

PATIENTS WHO UNDERWENT PRIMARY GLOTTIC WIDENING SURGERY:

3.2

A total of 83 patients underwent GWS as their first airway intervention for BVFP. 26/83 patients (31.3%) underwent cricoid split, 26 (31.3%) underwent suture lateralization, 24 (28.9%) underwent cordectomy/cordotomy, 5 (6.0%) underwent Botox injection into the cricothyroid muscle, and 2 (2.4%) underwent arytenoidectomy. In one of the patients described, arytenoidectomy was performed at the same time as cordectomy.78 The three surgical groups included in the statistical analysis for the primary GWS group were cricoid split, suture lateralization, and cordectomy/cordotomy. Statistical analysis via GLMM amongst patients who underwent GWS as their first airway intervention for BVFP revealed no statistically significant differences in tracheostomy rate (p = 0.98), reoperation rate (p = 0.28), or mortality (p = 0.38) between cricoid split, suture lateralization, and cordectomy/cordotomy.

The overall rate of tracheostomy in the primary GWS group was 6.0% (5/83). 14 (16.9%) patients underwent revision surgery of the same type. Mortality occurred in 6 patients (7.2%) who underwent primary GWS. One death occurred 2 months after the initial airway surgery and was unrelated to the surgery; further details were not reported.79 Another death occurred in a patient who underwent revision surgery later followed by a tracheostomy, though details of the cause of death were not reported.23 Two other patients died between one and four months after their airway surgery after being placed on comfort measures for severe neurological conditions.18,77 One other death occurred as a result of neurological degeneration.24 Lastly, another death occurred in a patient who eventually required a tracheostomy for continued respiratory distress; this patient had co-morbid microcephaly and hypotrophy.23 Follow-up ranged from 18 days to 192 months (mean of 35.6 months).

PATIENTS WHO UNDERWENT PRIMARY TRACHEOSTOMY:

3.3

Of the 157 patients who underwent tracheostomy as their first airway intervention, 71 had no further airway interventions. 86 patients underwent GWS after tracheostomy. Of these, 40/86 (46.5%) underwent arytenoidectomy, 26 (30.2%) underwent cordectomy/cordotomy, 10 (11.6%) underwent cricoid split, 6 (7.0%) underwent bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve reinnervation, and 4 (4.7%) underwent suture lateralization.

Analysis of the pooled data of the 157 tracheostomized patients revealed that 99 (63.1%) were ultimately decannulated. Amongst the 71 patients who received tracheostomy as their only airway intervention for BVFP, decannulation occurred in 29 (40.8%, mean follow-up duration: 42.3 months). Amongst the 86 patients who received tracheostomy for BVFP followed by GWS, decannulation occurred in 70 (81.4%, mean follow-up duration 47.0 months). Death occurred in 3/157 tracheostomized patients (1.9%), one who died from aspiration pneumonia and enlargement of his brain tumor, one from cardiac arrhythmia, and one due to an unspecified reason.49,51,66 The three surgical groups included in the statistical analysis for the primary tracheostomy group were cricoid split, arytenoidectomy, and cordectomy/cordotomy.

Statistical analysis via GLMM amongst patients who underwent tracheostomy as their first airway intervention for BVFP followed by eventual GWS revealed no statistically significant differences in decannulation rate (p = 0.85) or reoperation rate (p = 0.24) between cricoid split, arytenoidectomy, and cordectomy/cordotomy. No patient deaths were reported for these groups. These findings are summarized in Table 3.

Statistical analysis via Fisher’s exact test comparing decannulation rates amongst patients who underwent tracheostomy as their only airway intervention for BVFP versus those who underwent tracheostomy followed by GWS revealed a statistically significant higher decannulation rate for the patients who underwent tracheostomy followed by GWS (odds ratio 6.336, p = <0.0001).

DISCUSSION

4.

Bilateral vocal fold paralysis is a rare condition with a variable natural history. Since a large percentage of patients will undergo spontaneous recovery, temporizing surgeries to open the glottic aperture have become favored over more invasive and morbid procedures such as tracheostomy in patients with respiratory distress or failure to thrive. This systematic review presents the current body of knowledge for the initial surgical management of BVFP in children. These data will be useful for clinicians in planning treatment for the child with BVFP and for counseling parents on expected outcomes.

After a comprehensive review of the literature, no statistically significant differences are identified in the rate of tracheostomy, reoperation, or mortality between the three most common GWS procedures in children without tracheostomy (cricoid split, suture lateralization, and cordectomy/cordotomy), as well as the three most common GWS in children with tracheostomy (cricoid split, arytenoidectomy, and cordectomy/cordotomy). Most children in the primary GWS group avoided a tracheostomy, which was only reported in 5/83 (6.0%) patients. Amongst those who received tracheostomy as their first airway intervention for BVFP, there was a statistically significant improvement in decannulation rate for children who received GWS afterward. These data suggest that GWS should be offered to all tracheostomized children with BVFP who fail decannulation, though superiority of one GWS technique over another was not demonstrated.

Patients from the included studies underwent either tracheostomy, cricoid split, suture lateralization, cordectomy/cordotomy, arytenoidectomy, bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve reinnervation, or botulinum toxin injection into the cricothyroid muscle as their initial treatment. This is consistent with the contemporary surgical therapies described in the literature for pediatric BVFP.80 Arytenoidectomy was only performed as initial surgical management in 3 of the 83 patients (3.6%) who underwent primary GWS compared with 40 of the 157 patients (25.5%) who underwent primary tracheostomy. This is consistent with its typical use in decannulation of previously tracheostomized children rather than as an initial airway procedure. Botulinum toxin injection into the cricothyroid muscle was utilized in 5 patients in one included case series, all of whom had favorable outcomes. While this technique has been described in a larger case series with promising results in adults, there was an insufficient number of cases using this technique in children to be included in our analysis.81 Owing to the complexity of the procedure, bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve reinnervation of the posterior cricoarytenoid muscle is seldom performed for treatment of BVFP.9 This technique was only utilized in one case series of 6 children who were previously tracheostomized, of which half failed to be decannulated.

The mortality rate in this cohort was 9/240 (3.8%.) None of these deaths were related to airway compromise. This reflects the high medical complexity of children with BVFP, who often have severe comorbid conditions. This is similar to pediatric tracheostomy, in which only a small percentage of postoperative patient deaths are directly attributable to the tracheostomy itself.82

We utilized a broad range of medical subject headings (MeSH) and other search terms in 4 databases in order to capture all reported cases in the literature. As with other reviews of surgical intervention for rare clinical entities, data was limited to retrospective case reports or case series, thus all received an OCEBM level of evidence of 4. Prospective multicentric trials comparing different surgical modalities will be needed to conclusively identify optimal management for this condition in children with and without a tracheostomy.

There were several limitations to our study. There was significant heterogeneity in study design and reporting of results, a consequence of the observational nature of the included studies. Duration of follow-up was not able to be determined for individual patients in 5 studies, and follow-up otherwise ranged from 1 week to 420 months in the remaining studies. Additionally, our review did not include patients with BVFP who received no intervention. Thus, patients with BVFP that were minimally symptomatic (not requiring any intervention) or died from other co-morbid syndromes before receiving any intervention were not included in the analysis. These minimally symptomatic patients should be included in future prospective trials to determine their rate of eventual intervention and tracheostomy. Data regarding voice and swallowing outcomes were not reported in most of the included studies, which would be an important primary endpoint in any future trial addressing surgical management of BVFP. Until more robust evidence is published, choice of surgical technique will continue to depend on a discussion with the child’s parents, surgeon preference and training, and available resources. Nonetheless, this systematic review adds important knowledge regarding the emerging treatment options for BVFP in children. This will serve to guide physicians in counselling patients and their families as well as making treatment decisions.

CONCLUSION

5.

Most children who undergo suture lateralization, cricoid split, or cordectomy/cordotomy as first line treatment for BVFP avoid tracheostomy or reoperation. These data demonstrated no differences in surgical outcomes between the most common surgical options for BVFP in patients who underwent GWS as their first airway intervention as well as those who underwent tracheostomy first. Prospective trials are needed to further examine outcomes in these patients to determine optimal surgical technique. Amongst children who receive a tracheostomy as their first intervention for BVFP, GWS is associated with a significantly improved rate of decannulation.
