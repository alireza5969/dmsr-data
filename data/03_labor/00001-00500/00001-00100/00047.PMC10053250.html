Abstract

This systematic review evaluated the clinical effectiveness and safety of subcutaneous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (S‐ICD) in patients at an increased risk of sudden cardiac death and with an ICD indication for primary or secondary prevention. A systematic literature search was conducted in four databases (Medline via Ovid, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and HTA‐INAHTA). Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and controlled observational studies with ≥100 S‐ICD patients and a low to moderate risk of bias were eligible for inclusion. The studies' quality and the available evidence's strength were assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias tool, the ROBINS‐I tool, and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. One RCT, a post hoc analysis of the RCT (n = 849) and four controlled observational studies (n = 7149) were included. The quality of the available evidence was graded as low to very low, except for the primary composite endpoint of the RCT, which was rated as moderate quality. After 4 years, the RCT showed that S‐ICD was non‐inferior to TV‐ICD regarding the composite endpoint of inappropriate shocks and device‐related complications (68 [15.1%] vs. 68 [15.7%], hazard ratio [HR] 0.99, 95% confidence interval [CI] [0.71, 1.39], non‐inferiority margin 1.45, P = 0.001). The RCT and two observational studies reported statistically significantly fewer lead complications in S‐ICD patients (after 4 years: 1.4% vs. 6.6%, HR 0.24, 95% CI [0.10, 0.54]; after 3 years: 0.3% vs. 2.3%, P = 0.03; and after 5 years: 0.8% vs. 11.5%, P = 0.03). Identified evidence about appropriate and inappropriate shocks was inconclusive: The RCT detected statistically significantly more appropriate shocks in patients with S‐ICD (83 [19.2%] vs. 57 [11.5%], HR 1.52, 95% CI [1.08, 2.12], P = 0.02), whereas one observational study showed a statistically significantly lower rate in the S‐ICD group (9.9%, 95% CI [7.0, 13.9], vs. 13.9%, 95% CI [10.8, 17.8], P = 0.003). Regarding inappropriate shocks, one observational study reported statistically significantly higher rates in the S‐ICD cohort (11.9% vs. 7.5%, P = 0.007), whereas the RCT and two other observational studies did not detect a statistically significant difference between the treatment groups (P > 0.05). None of the included studies showed a statistically significant difference in overall mortality and shock efficacy between patients with S‐ICD and TV‐ICD (P > 0.05). The available evidence is insufficient to show the superiority of S‐ICD compared with TV‐ICD, hindering the widespread use of the technology. Results of the recently completed ATLAS trial are to be awaited, and the anticipated role of the S‐ICD needs to be clearly formulated.

Introduction

According to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guideline (2021), a high proportion of deaths among patients with heart failure occur suddenly and unexpectedly.1, 2 An implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) is deemed to detect and terminate life‐threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmias.3 In contrast to the established transvenous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (TV‐ICD), the subcutaneous counterpart (S‐ICD) places the lead subcutaneously, that is, directly under the skin, leaving the heart and vascular system untouched. The implantation of S‐ICD can prevent short‐ and long‐term complications associated with the transvenous lead. These complications involve pneumothorax and cardiac perforation in the short term, as well as lead fracture, lead dysfunction, lead endocarditis, and venous thrombosis in the longer term.3, 4

The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guideline 2021/20221, 2, 5 and the American Heart Association (AHA), American College of Cardiology (ACC), and Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) Guideline for Management of Patients With Ventricular Arrhythmias and the Prevention of Sudden Cardiac Death 20173 recommend S‐ICDs in patients with an ICD indication when pacing therapy for bradycardia support, cardiac resynchronization, or anti‐tachycardia pacing (ATP) is not needed (ESC: Class IIa, Level C). Further, the S‐ICD may be considered a valuable alternative to the TV‐ICD system when venous access is difficult, after removing a TV‐ICD for infections or in younger adult patients with a long‐term need for ICD therapy (ESC: Class IIa, Level C; AHA/ACC/ATP: Class I, Level B/Class IIa, Level B).

In Austria, since 2018, the S‐ICD has been reimbursed with restriction, supported by the evidence synthesis conducted by the Ludwig Boltzmann Institute for HTA (LBI‐HTA) that showed a lower risk for lead complications in patients treated with S‐ICD.6 In light of recently published clinical studies, it was uncertain whether the broad adoption as an unconditionally reimbursable hospital procedure is supported by clinical evidence. Hence, the Austrian Institute for Health Technology Assessment (AIHTA, former: Ludwig Boltzmann Institute for HTA) conducted an updated systematic review (SR) on the use of S‐ICD as part of the annual evaluation of the Austrian hospital benefit catalogue.7, 8 This article aims to summarize the evidence of the effectiveness and safety of S‐ICDs compared with conventional TV‐ICDs based on this evidence.9

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement10, 11 and the Core Model for the production of Rapid Relative Effectiveness Assessments (Version 4.2) of the European Network for Health Technology Assessment (EUnetHTA)12 were utilized as reporting standards.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted on 24 November 2021 using four databases (Medline via Ovid, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and HTA‐INAHTA). The search was limited to the period December 2017 until December 2021, updating the existing SR from 2018, and to articles published in English or German. In addition, hand searches in reference lists of other SRs13, 14, 15 were carried out. On 29 November 2021, three clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov; WHO‐ICTRP; EU Clinical Trials) were searched to identify ongoing and unpublished studies.

Selection criteria

Comparative studies assessing the effectiveness and safety of S‐ICDs compared with TV‐ICDs in adult patients at increased risk of sudden cardiac death and with an ICD indication for primary or secondary prevention were eligible for inclusion. RCTs, as well as retrospective and prospective controlled observational studies with more than 100 S‐ICD patients and a low to moderate risk of bias (RoB), were considered. The relevance of the selected effectiveness and safety outcomes was rated according to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) guidelines.16 All‐cause mortality, appropriate ICD shocks, shock efficacy, inappropriate shocks and device‐ and lead‐related complications were critical for informing an evidence‐based reimbursement decision. Other outcomes were considered important (hospital re‐admission and quality of life) and relevant (appropriate and inappropriate ATP). Table 1 presents the eligibility criteria in more detail.

Data extraction, quality appraisal, and analysis

Relevant data were extracted using established data extraction tables. One author (S. W.) extracted the data, and another author (G. G.) controlled it. Two authors (S. W. and G. G.) independently assessed the quality of the included studies by utilizing the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool17 for RCTs and the ROBINS‐I tool18 for controlled observational studies. The evidence was qualitatively synthesized, and the strength of evidence was assessed by the GRADE approach.16 All discrepancies were resolved by consensus or involving a third researcher (B. W. and C. W.). Solely, the results of the critical outcomes are described in this article. Results of the other noncritical outcomes can be found in the original HTA report9 or requested from the authors.

Results

Search results

The updated systematic and hand searches yielded 557 records after de‐duplication. The abstract screening revealed that 459 records did not meet the inclusion criteria, resulting in 98 full‐text reviews. Following the full‐text review, 92 full texts were excluded for reasons. One RCT (in two publications) and further four observational studies (of which two were newly identified by the update search) were eligible for the qualitative synthesis of the evidence. Characteristics of the included studies

One RCT19 and a post hoc analysis of the RCT20 with 849 enrolled patients and four propensity score‐matched observational studies21, 22, 23, 24 with 7149 patients evaluated the use of S‐ICD compared with TV‐ICD for primary or secondary prevention in patients at increased risk of sudden cardiac death. The RCT was powered to test the non‐inferiority of S‐ICD over TV‐ICD in terms of a primary composite endpoint of inappropriate ICD shocks and device‐related complications (non‐inferiority margin: 1.45).19 The primary endpoints of the second publication of the RCT (a post hoc analysis) were appropriate ICD therapy and first shock efficacy.20 Three observational studies with control21, 22, 24 were powered for the primary endpoint device‐related complications. The fourth controlled observational study23 defined a primary composite endpoint of any complication recorded in the hospital. The median follow‐up of the RCT was approximately 4 years, during which 18 patients in the S‐ICD (4.3%) and 11 patients in the TV‐ICD group (2.7%) switched to the other treatment group.19 The median follow‐up periods in the controlled observational studies ranged from the in‐hospital stay23 to 72 months (6 years).24 All included studies enrolled patients older than 18 with an AHA/ACC/HRS Class I or IIa ICD indication for primary or secondary prevention. In contrast, patients with an indication for either bradycardia pacing, biventricular pacing, or another unsuitability for S‐ICD implantation were excluded. One controlled observational study21 also enrolled patients with a previous ICD implantation; however, this affected only 14.0% in the S‐ICD and 11.0% in the TV‐ICD cohort; thus, the study was still considered. In all studies, most of the enrolled patients were male, and the patients in the controlled observational studies were slightly younger than those in the RCT. However, no statistically significant gender and age balance differences were reported.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 In the RCT19 and three controlled observational studies,22, 23, 24 the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was between 30% and 40% in both treatment arms, while in one observational study,21 the LVEF was higher with around 50% in both cohorts. Quality of the studies and the certainty of the available evidence

The results of the RCT19, 20 were ranked as having some concerns (primary analysis) and high RoB (post hoc analysis) primarily due to the non‐inferiority design (clinically debatable threshold of 1.45) and due to the sponsoring of the study by the S‐ICD manufacturer. Across the six controlled observational studies, two from the existing SR from 201821, 23 and two new ones22, 24 were ranked as having a moderate RoB. The main reasons for downgrading were the missed blinding of the outcome assessors in some studies and the sponsoring by the S‐ICD manufacturer.22 The entire RoB assessment is depicted in the original report9 or can be requested from the authors. Critical effectiveness and safety outcomes of S‐ICD compared with TV‐ICD

Effectiveness

Overall mortality

Low‐quality evidence was found for overall mortality up to 6 years (1 RCT, n = 894, and 4 controlled observational studies, n = 7140). None of the studies defined the endpoint as a primary outcome. None of the studies showed a statistically significant difference in overall mortality between patients with S‐ICD or TV‐ICD independent of the follow‐up.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24

Appropriate ICD shocks

Low‐quality inconclusive evidence was found for appropriate shocks (1 RCT, n = 849, and 2 controlled observational studies, n = 1042). The endpoint was defined as a primary outcome in any study. The RCT19 detected a statistically significant difference between the S‐ICD and the TV‐ICD group after 4 years, with more appropriate shocks appearing in patients with S‐ICD (83 [19.2%] vs. 57 [11.5%], hazard ratio [HR] 1.52, 95% confidence interval [CI] [1.08, 2.12], P = 0.02). In contrast, one of the two controlled observational studies22 showed a statistically significantly lower appropriate shock rate in the S‐ICD compared with the TV‐ICD cohort after 3 years (9.9%, 95% CI [7.0, 13.9], vs. 13.9%, 95% CI [10.8, 17.8], P = 0.003). The other controlled observational study21 could not detect a statistically significant difference in appropriate shocks between the cohorts after five years.

Shock efficacy

Low‐quality evidence was found for the outcome shock efficacy for up to 4 years (1 post hoc analysis, n = 849, and 1 controlled observational study, n = 782). In the post hoc analysis of the RCT,20 shock efficacy was defined as a primary endpoint. None of the two studies detected a statistically significant or numerical difference in shock efficacy between patients with S‐ICD or TV‐ICD independent of the follow‐up.20, 22

Safety

Composite endpoint of inappropriate shocks and device‐related complications

Moderate‐quality evidence was found for the primary composite endpoint of inappropriate shocks and device‐related complications (1 RCT, n = 849). After 4 years, the RCT showed that the S‐ICD is non‐inferior compared with the TV‐ICD concerning this composite endpoint (68 [15.1%] vs. 68 [15.7%], HR 0.99, CI 95% [0.71, 1.39], non‐inferiority margin 1.45, P = 0.001), but not superior (P = 0.95).19

Inappropriate ICD shocks

Low‐quality inconclusive evidence was found for inappropriate shocks (1 RCT, n = 849, and 3 controlled observational studies, n = 1380). In the RCT, inappropriate shocks were one component of the primary composite endpoint,19 while it was a secondary outcome in the controlled observational studies.21, 22, 24 Only one controlled observational study22 reported a statistically significant difference in inappropriate shocks after 3 years, with higher inappropriate shock rates observed in the S‐ICD cohort (11.9% vs. 7.5%, P = 0.007). In contrast, the RCT19 and two other controlled observational studies21, 24 did not detect a statistically significant difference between the treatment groups. The RCT19 and one controlled observational study21 reported slightly more inappropriate shocks in the S‐ICD than in the TV‐ICD group after 4 and 5 years. After 6 years, the other controlled observational study19 showed more inappropriate shocks in the TV‐ICD cohort.

Device‐ and lead‐related complications

Low‐quality inconclusive evidence was also found for device‐ and lead‐related complications (1 RCT, n = 849, and 3 controlled observational studies, n = 1380). All studies were powered for device‐ and lead‐related complications as a primary outcome. Only one controlled observational study24 reported a statistically significant difference in device‐ and lead‐related complications after 6 years, with slightly more complications occurring in the S‐ICD cohort (3 [1.8%] vs. 17 [0.1%], odds ratio [OR] 0.16, 95% CI [0.05, 0.56], P = 0.001). In contrast, the RCT19 and two other controlled observational studies21, 22 did not detect a statistically significant difference between the treatment groups independent of the follow‐up. Although the RCT19 and one controlled observational study21 reported slightly more device‐related complications in patients with TV‐ICD after 4 and 5 years, respectively, in the other controlled observational study,22 more device‐related complications occurred in the S‐ICD cohort after 3 years.

Lead‐related complications

Moderate to low‐quality evidence was found for lead‐related complications (1 RCT, n = 849, and 3 controlled observational studies, n = 1380). The RCT19 and two controlled observational studies21, 22 identified a statistically significant reduction in lead‐related complication in patients with S‐ICD after four (1.4% vs. 6.6%, HR 0.24, 95% CI 0.10–0.54), three (0.3% vs. 2.3%, P = 0.03), and five (0.8% vs. 11.5%, P = 0.03) years, respectively. The third controlled observational study24 did not detect a statistically significant difference in lead failures and lead dislodgements between the cohorts after 6 years.

Discussion

The main finding of this SR is that moderate evidence from the RCT showed non‐inferiority within the margin of 1.45 for the S‐ICD in terms of a composite outcome of inappropriate shocks and device‐related complications and superiority in terms of lead‐related complications compared with the conventional TV‐ICD in patients with an increased risk of sudden cardiac death and an ICD indication for primary and secondary prevention with no need for pacing. The benefit of the S‐ICD regarding reduced lead‐related complications might be even underestimated in the RCT, as the mean follow‐up of 4 years was relatively short and lead complications tend to appear in patients with a TV‐ICD later in the follow‐up.

Moreover, in the RCT and controlled observational studies, no statistically significant differences were found in the effectiveness outcomes overall mortality and shock efficacy, and inconsistent results were reported for appropriate ICD shocks.

Concerning further outcomes (see Table 4), most included studies did not detect a statistically significant difference in rehospitalizations between patients with S‐ICD and TV‐ICD. Only one controlled observational study24 showed a statistically significant difference in complication‐related rehospitalizations after 6 years, with fewer rehospitalizations per patient in the S‐ICD cohort. None of the included studies reported on the impact of the S‐ICD on patient quality of life.

The present SR complements less recent SRs. Our review followed more stringent methodological steps to assess the quality of the included evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) scheme.16 In addition, the present SR only considered high‐ to moderate‐quality‐controlled observational studies next to randomized evidence. In contrast, the two identified SRs also included observational studies of lower quality. One systematic review and meta‐analysis from 202114 identified the same RCT and 12 observational studies comprising 9073 patients that directly compared clinical outcomes and complications between patients with S‐ICD and TV‐ICD. The authors concluded that for patients with an ICD indication without the need for pacing, TV‐ICD and S‐ICD are comparable in terms of the composite of clinically relevant device‐related complications and inappropriate shocks. Another systematic review and meta‐analysis from 202113 included 26 studies that examined 7542 patients with S‐ICD and 5400 patients with TV‐ICD. The results showed that patients with S‐ICD had a statistically significantly lower incidence of lead‐related complications. In contrast to the results of the present SR, the review also observed a statistically significant reduction in all‐cause mortality in the S‐ICD group. However, even if the higher all‐cause mortality rate of the S‐ICD arm (+23%) in the PRETORIAN trial could not reach statistical significance at 49 months, claims of the S‐ICD being associated with a lower mortality rate compared with the TV‐ICD requires further preferably randomized evidence.

With respect to the studies included in the present SR, concerns exist regarding the non‐inferiority results of the composite endpoint of inappropriate shocks and device‐related complications. First, the single components of the endpoint could be expected to go in opposite directions and thus might have biased the trial towards reaching non‐inferiority: It seems logical that S‐ICD has fewer device‐related complications but higher rates of inappropriate shocks due to impaired sensing.25 Second, the same weight was given to severe consequences of inappropriate high‐voltage shocks and possible less severe device‐related complications, which might have also biased the results towards non‐inferiority.26

Appropriate shocks usually occur more frequently in patients with S‐ICDs, as S‐ICDs are incapable of delivering ATPs, while TV‐ICDs can terminate a tachycardic arrhythmia in a way preferred by patients (without a shock, only by ATP). However, evidence from clinical practice showed that both ‘appropriate’ (as deemed by the programmer of the individual device) and inappropriate ATPs could also accelerate a cardiac arrhythmia and thus necessitate a shock. In the worst case, an ATP could accelerate tachycardia, which would otherwise have terminated spontaneously and would also have had no haemodynamic consequence.27, 28 None of the included studies evaluated these uncertainties about the clinical value of ATP in a structured manner. Further evidence is needed in this regard.

The reduction in lead‐related complications with S‐ICD can be explained by the lacking contact with cardiac structures and the absence of transvenous lead implantation.25 Short‐term complications, such as pneumothorax or local re‐bleeding, are associated with hospitalization, and longer term complications, such as lead endocarditis, can lead to significant morbidity and mortality.29, 30 Therefore, S‐ICD might be a treatment option for patients with an increased risk of bacteraemia and endocarditis. Furthermore, S‐ICD is recommended for patients with complex or impossible venous access and might also represent a plausible option for specific clinical situations in which decade‐long retention of an ICD is likely and desirable, particularly in children.1, 2, 3 However, the present SR does not include studies evaluating the effectiveness and safety of S‐ICD in children.

The results of this SR should be viewed in the context of its limitations. Although controlled observational studies are more prone to internal validity concerns when compared with randomized trials, they were considered additionally; we carefully selected observational studies in line with Cochrane methodology to mitigate concerns31: We defined pre‐specified design features (i.e., n = > 100 S‐ICD recipients) and applied the principle of best available evidence (i.e., exclusion of studies with a high RoB). Consequently, the results of large single‐arm registry studies were excluded from this SR. For example, the S‐ICD Post Approval Study (S‐ICD PAS) with 1637 de novo patients (mean age 52 years, 68.5% men, mean LVEF of 32.0%, 42.9% ischaemic disease) from 86 US centres reported a S‐ICD complication‐free rate of 92.5% and an appropriate shock rate of 5.3%.32, 33 Another example presents the extended Experience from the long‐term Italian S‐ICD (ELISIR) registry study with 1254 patients (median age 52.0 years, 77.6% men, 30.9% ischaemic disease). The study observed S‐ICD complications in 9.3% of the patients with 127 device‐related complications over a mean follow‐up of 23.2 months.34 The results of another large registry study, the Understanding Outcomes With the S‐ICD in Primary Prevention Patients With Low Ejection Fraction (UNTOUCHED) trial with 1111 patients (mean age 55.8 years, 74.7% men, mean LVEF 26.4%) showed that the rate of inappropriate shocks observed in the PRAETORIAN trial19 could be further reduced by using newer S‐ICD generations.35, 36 Finally, a recently published US‐American registry study with 16 063 enrolled patients (mean age 72.6 years, 71.6% men, mean LVEF 28.3%) concluded that the risk of death, device re‐operation, device removal for infection, device re‐operation without infection, and cardiovascular and all‐cause re‐admission were similar among S‐ICD and TV‐ICD recipients.37 These results from noncomparable studies align with our SR results; however, they need to be confirmed by further randomized studies.

Conclusions

The available evidence is insufficient to show superiority of S‐ICD compared with TV‐ICD, hindering the widespread use of the technology. Nevertheless, S‐ICD may be beneficial for specific indications, as it is deemed non‐inferior to TV‐ICD in terms of a composite endpoint of inappropriate shocks and device‐related complications and comes along with statistically significantly fewer lead‐related complications. Results of the recently completed ATLAS trial37 are to be awaited to shed more light on the comparative effectiveness and safety of S‐ICD. Further, the anticipated role of the S‐ICD should be clearly formulated (e.g., narrow indication versus broad replacement).

## Introduction

According to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guideline (2021), a high proportion of deaths among patients with heart failure occur suddenly and unexpectedly.1, 2 An implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) is deemed to detect and terminate life‐threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmias.3 In contrast to the established transvenous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (TV‐ICD), the subcutaneous counterpart (S‐ICD) places the lead subcutaneously, that is, directly under the skin, leaving the heart and vascular system untouched. The implantation of S‐ICD can prevent short‐ and long‐term complications associated with the transvenous lead. These complications involve pneumothorax and cardiac perforation in the short term, as well as lead fracture, lead dysfunction, lead endocarditis, and venous thrombosis in the longer term.3, 4

The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guideline 2021/20221, 2, 5 and the American Heart Association (AHA), American College of Cardiology (ACC), and Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) Guideline for Management of Patients With Ventricular Arrhythmias and the Prevention of Sudden Cardiac Death 20173 recommend S‐ICDs in patients with an ICD indication when pacing therapy for bradycardia support, cardiac resynchronization, or anti‐tachycardia pacing (ATP) is not needed (ESC: Class IIa, Level C). Further, the S‐ICD may be considered a valuable alternative to the TV‐ICD system when venous access is difficult, after removing a TV‐ICD for infections or in younger adult patients with a long‐term need for ICD therapy (ESC: Class IIa, Level C; AHA/ACC/ATP: Class I, Level B/Class IIa, Level B).

In Austria, since 2018, the S‐ICD has been reimbursed with restriction, supported by the evidence synthesis conducted by the Ludwig Boltzmann Institute for HTA (LBI‐HTA) that showed a lower risk for lead complications in patients treated with S‐ICD.6 In light of recently published clinical studies, it was uncertain whether the broad adoption as an unconditionally reimbursable hospital procedure is supported by clinical evidence. Hence, the Austrian Institute for Health Technology Assessment (AIHTA, former: Ludwig Boltzmann Institute for HTA) conducted an updated systematic review (SR) on the use of S‐ICD as part of the annual evaluation of the Austrian hospital benefit catalogue.7, 8 This article aims to summarize the evidence of the effectiveness and safety of S‐ICDs compared with conventional TV‐ICDs based on this evidence.9
## Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement10, 11 and the Core Model for the production of Rapid Relative Effectiveness Assessments (Version 4.2) of the European Network for Health Technology Assessment (EUnetHTA)12 were utilized as reporting standards.

### Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted on 24 November 2021 using four databases (Medline via Ovid, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and HTA‐INAHTA). The search was limited to the period December 2017 until December 2021, updating the existing SR from 2018, and to articles published in English or German. In addition, hand searches in reference lists of other SRs13, 14, 15 were carried out. On 29 November 2021, three clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov; WHO‐ICTRP; EU Clinical Trials) were searched to identify ongoing and unpublished studies.

### Selection criteria

Comparative studies assessing the effectiveness and safety of S‐ICDs compared with TV‐ICDs in adult patients at increased risk of sudden cardiac death and with an ICD indication for primary or secondary prevention were eligible for inclusion. RCTs, as well as retrospective and prospective controlled observational studies with more than 100 S‐ICD patients and a low to moderate risk of bias (RoB), were considered. The relevance of the selected effectiveness and safety outcomes was rated according to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) guidelines.16 All‐cause mortality, appropriate ICD shocks, shock efficacy, inappropriate shocks and device‐ and lead‐related complications were critical for informing an evidence‐based reimbursement decision. Other outcomes were considered important (hospital re‐admission and quality of life) and relevant (appropriate and inappropriate ATP). Table 1 presents the eligibility criteria in more detail.

### Data extraction, quality appraisal, and analysis

Relevant data were extracted using established data extraction tables. One author (S. W.) extracted the data, and another author (G. G.) controlled it. Two authors (S. W. and G. G.) independently assessed the quality of the included studies by utilizing the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool17 for RCTs and the ROBINS‐I tool18 for controlled observational studies. The evidence was qualitatively synthesized, and the strength of evidence was assessed by the GRADE approach.16 All discrepancies were resolved by consensus or involving a third researcher (B. W. and C. W.). Solely, the results of the critical outcomes are described in this article. Results of the other noncritical outcomes can be found in the original HTA report9 or requested from the authors.
