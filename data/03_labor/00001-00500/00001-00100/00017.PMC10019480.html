Abstract

INTRODUCTION

Phantom limb pain (PLP) after amputation is a frequent entity that conditions the life of those who suffer it. Current treatment methods are not sufficiently effective for PLP management. We aim to analyze the clinical application of transcranial direct current (tDCS) in people with amputation suffering from PLP.

EVIDENCE ACQUISITION

The following databases were consulted in September 2021: MEDLINE, EMBASE, The Web of Science, PEDro, SCOPUS and SciELO. Randomized controlled trials investigating the use of tDCS in people with amputation undergoing PLP were selected. Demographic data, type and cause of amputation, time since amputation, stimulation parameters, and outcomes were extracted.

EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

Six articles were included in this review (seven studies were considered because one study performed two individual protocols). All included studies evaluated PLP; six evaluated the phantom limb sensations (PLS) and two evaluated the psychiatric disorders. In all included studies the intensity and frequency of PLP was reduced, in three PLS were reduced, and in none study psychiatric symptoms were modified.

CONCLUSIONS

Anodic tDCS over the contralateral M1 to the affected limb, with an intensity of 1-2 mA, for 15-20 minutes seems to significantly reduce PLP in people with amputation. Single-session treatment could modify PLP intensity for hours, and multi-session treatment could modify PLP for months. Limited evidence suggests that PLS and psychiatric disorders should be treated with different PLP electrode placements. Further studies with larger sample size and longer follow-up times are needed to establish the priority of tDCS application in the PLP management.

Introduction

Phantom limb pain (PLP) is a chronic pain condition perceived in a limb that no longer exists. This sensation is reported by people who have undergone limb amputation. PLP is a serious public health problem that affects the physical, psychological, and functional health of people with amputation, significantly impairing their quality of life.1 After limb amputation, 50-80% of patients experience phantom limb sensations (PLS),2, 3 which people with amputation describe as kinesthetic, length, volume, or other spatial sensations of the amputated limb. Up to 87% of people with amputation experience PLP,4 described as sharp, stabbing, electrical, dull, squeezing, cramping pain, as distinguished from stump pain, which is sharp, often stabbing, localized to the stump, and usually aggravated by pressure.5

Current pharmacological, behavioral, and rehabilitative treatment options for PLP are not entirely effective, probably because of the involvement of some psychological factors such as depression, anxiety, tension, and stress.6 The relationship between these factors and PLP is even greater than in other chronic pain conditions.7, 8 Given the lack of efficacy of standard treatment in a large number of patients, surgical alternatives have been investigated.9 Among the most studied options is the surgical destruction of nerve endings, such as rhizotomy, which despite demonstrating a high initial rate of pain relief, is often associated with adverse symptoms and a high rate of recurrent pain in the long term, being limited to very few patients.9 Other non-destructive surgical treatment options, such as nerve blocks, have gained interest due to lower associated adverse effects.10

In recent years and given the pathophysiological relationship of PLP with maladaptive plastic cortical reorganization after amputation,11 neuromodulation techniques have emerged as effective treatment options. Invasive neuromodulation techniques, such as deep brain stimulation or epidural electrical stimulation, have been investigated and show a long-term effectiveness in pain reduction of 25-50%.12-14

Non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) techniques are presented as an alternative that significantly reduces the risks of invasive neuromodulation techniques and can be an effective outpatient treatment. NIBS is an effective treatment technique for chronic pain, which could induce changes in maladaptive neuroplasticity and potentiate descending inhibitory pathways,15 producing pain relief and a decreased PLS.16 Several types of NIBS have been used for this purpose, the most used being repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS). These NIBS techniques have been useful in the treatment of some pain conditions, such as neuropathic pain after spinal cord injury,17 certain types of chronic pain,18 or the pain present in fibromyalgia.19 As they are also effective in psychiatric disorders such as anxiety and depression, they are potentially useful in PLP because if its association with these conditions.20

There are previous reviews on the efficacy of PLP treatment using NIBS,3, 5, 8, 21 the most current being the systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Pacheco et al. in 2020,3 in which only two tDCS studies are included; these reviews analyze different NIBS techniques, but none include only studies on tDCS despite being the most clinically affordable tool compared to other NIBS, so a specific review is needed to compile all the information on the use of this technique as an approach to PLP.

Evidence acquisition

Design

This review was reported following the PRISMA recommendations for reporting systematic reviews22 (Supplementary Digital Material 1: Supplementary Table I). This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO with the registration number: CRD42021278468

Search strategy and database

The following databases were searched on September 2021: MEDLINE, EMBASE, The Web of Science, PEDro, SCOPUS and SciELO. Several keywords were used about pathology, and the tDCS, combined with Boolean operators (complete search strategy in Supplementary Digital Material 2: Supplementary Table II).

Screening process and eligibility criteria

The titles and abstracts of the studies obtained from the literature search in the different databases were analyzed by two different researchers according to the following inclusion-exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher. Inclusion criteria: Eligible studies had to be randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reporting the level or severity of PLP (continuous or categorical data). PLP had to be defined as a sensation of shooting, stabbing, tightness, throbbing and burning or paresthesia or any other pain sensation in an extremity that no longer existed with a pain Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score of at least 4 out of 10. Exclusion criteria: Studies that included patients with a diagnosis of complex regional pain syndrome, or any pathology that could alter the course of PLP (diagnosis of cancer, immunological disorders, renal failure requiring dialysis treatment, etc.) were excluded.

Data extraction

Standardized methodology was used to extract data from studies that met the inclusion criteria. Data on the first author, year of publication, design, number of patients, demographic data, type and cause of amputation, time since amputation, stimulation parameters, outcomes and results were extracted.

Assessment of risk of bias (methodological quality)

To analyze the methodological quality of each individual study, the Oxford quality scoring system was used.23 This scale includes items related to randomization, masking, and the description of the losses to follow-up, with the highest score (highest methodological quality) being 5 and the lowest score (lowest methodological quality) being 0. To analyze the risk of bias of each individual study, the RoB-2 (the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials)24 was used. It is a valid tool that evaluates domains related to random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other bias. Each item may be classified as a risk of bias that is “high risk”, “low risk”, or “some concerns.” The entire methodological process was carried out by two different researchers, and any discrepancy was resolved by a third researcher.

Data synthesis and analysis

Studies with variables that were compared with others, including their baseline, post, and follow-up measurements, were included in the qualitative analysis. Outcomes related to the PLP were established as the primary outcome (postintervention outcome or mean differences [preintervention - postintervention difference]), and those related to PLS and psychiatric disorders such as depression or anxiety were established as secondary outcomes. Results were divided into subgroups for further analysis: polarity (anode versus cathode versus bilateral tDCS), tDCS intensity (<2 mA versus 2 mA), and treatment sessions (single session versus several sessions), amputation level (upper limb versus lower limb). Data on patient characteristics, interventions, and outcomes were tabulated and a narrative synthesis was performed. Pooling of RCT data by meta-analysis was explored but was not possible due to heterogeneity of outcome data.

Evidence synthesis

Studies selection

A total of 171 studies were retrieved. Duplicate studies were eliminated, leaving a total of 75 studies, on which a critical reading of the title and abstract was carried out. After the first screening, there was a total of 10 studies, which were obtained and read in full text together with three studies included through reading the bibliography of two systematic reviews. Finally, 6 studies25-30 were included in the review after performing a second screening, with a total of 211 subjects (138 men/73 women). The study by Bolognini et al.26 was considered two different studies because the authors carried out two different interventions with independent protocols. From this point on, the data were processed as if they were 7 studies. The whole screening process is shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

Quality assessment

Regarding methodological quality, of the seven included studies, 6 studies25, 26, 28-30 showed “high” (5 points) methodological quality according to the Oxford quality scoring system, and one study21 showed a methodological quality rated as “moderate” (score of 3) (Supplementary Digital Material 3: Supplementary Table III). All studies were double-blind. Regarding the risk of bias, four studies showed low risk in all measured domains25, 26, 29 (selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other bias). The other three studies27, 28, 30 showed some concerns regarding blinding of participants and personnel (Figure 2).

Subject and studies

The years of completion of the studies span from 2013 to 2022. The design of the included studies was RCT, all being double blind. Among the studies, 5 performed a crossover RCT.25-27, 29 Of the of 211 subjects (138 men/73 women), the mean age was 49.51 ranged between 21 and 82 years. The cause of the amputation was the following: traumatic, 32; diabetes, 21; ischemic, 21; cancer, 5. Data on amputation type from the study conducted by Gunduz et al. 202128 were not available. All studies included subjects with single-sided amputation, four studies included subjects with lower limb amputation,26, 28, 30 two with upper limb amputation,25, 29 and one study included subjects with both lower and upper limb amputations.30 Among the studies with lower limb amputation, 81 subjects had above-knee amputation and 79 had below-knee amputation. Among the studies with upper limb amputation, 16 subjects had above-elbow amputation and 14 below-elbow amputation. The time since amputation was 69.48 months; 40% of the subjects included in the study by Segal et al.30 had been amputated for less than 1 month at the time of the study and 60% of the subjects for more than 1 month. All included studies had a mean PLP greater than 4 in the VAS of pain, being 4 to 627, 29 in two studies and greater than 6 in 3 studies.25, 28, 30 The studies by Bolognini et al.26 did not show mean baseline PLP.

Treatment

Regarding treatment, 5 studies applied tDCS,25-27, 29 and two applied tDCS combined with mirror therapy.28, 30 All included studies applied anodal tDCS, five placed the anode over the primary motor cortex contralateral to the affected limb,26, 28-30 one placed the anode over the cerebellum,25 and one placed the anode over the parietal cortex contralateral to the affected limb.26 One study applied anodic and cathodic stimulation over the parietal cortex at different times.26 Current intensity ranged from 1 to 2 mA, being 1 mA in one study,29 1.5 mA in two studies27, 30 and 2 mA in four studies.25, 26, 28 The current density (intensity/cm2 of the electrode) was 0.028 in one study,29 0.04 in one study,30 0.043 in one study27 and 0.057 in four studies.25, 26, 28 Regarding stimulation time, four studies applied tDCS for 20 minutes,21, 28-30 and three studies applied it for 15 minutes.26, 27 The number of sessions was 1 in three studies,26, 29 5 in two studies25, 26 and 10 in two studies.28, 30

Measurement tools

PLP were measured in all the included studies by the intensity; three studies also assessed the frequency of PLP episodes27, 29, 30 and pain paroxysms.25, 27 The measurement tools used in relation to PLP were as follows: six used VAS for PLP intensity,25-28, 30 and one used the short pain questionnaire.29 PLS were measured in six studies25-28, 30 as sensations other than PLP and residual limb pain. The included studies used VAS for phantom limb movements, phantom non-painful sensations, and residual stump pain other than PLP25-28 and the Sensory subscale of the Short Form McGill Pain Questionnaire.30

Psychiatric disorders were assessed in two studies.27, 28 All these studies used the Beck Depression Inventory,27, 28 and one also used the Beck Anxiety Inventory.27

Intensity and frequency of PLP

Pain intensity and paroxysms decreased after application of anodic tDCS in all25-30 but one26 of the included studies. The studies that observed significant improvements applied different stimulation protocols: current intensity and current density (1 mA,29 1.5 mA27, 30 or 2 mA;25, 26, 28 0.028 mA/cm2, 29 0.04 mA/cm2, 30 0.043 mA/cm2, 27 or 0.057 mA/cm2),25, 26, 28 stimulation time (20 min25, 28-30 or 15 min)26, 27 and number of sessions (1,26, 29 525, 27 or 10).28, 30 The intensity of PLP decreased in subjects with unilateral upper25, 27, 29 and lower26-28, 30 extremity amputation, regardless of level and months since amputation. Baseline PLP was not a limiting factor in these changes, as subjects with a mean PLP of 4 to 626, 29 and above 625, 28, 30 significantly improved in a similar way.

The study that did not achieve significant changes in PLP26 applied anodic and cathodic stimulation over the parietal cortex with an intensity of 2 mA (current density or 0.057 mA/cm2), for 15 min in one anodic and one cathodic session. Mean VAS pain reduction was included in three studies, being 5,30 2.5,28 and 1.6.25

Data from PLP frequency could be pooled from 3 studies.26, 29, 30 In these studies, a reduction in the frequency of PLP episodes was observed after treatment with tDCS alone26 or in combination with mirror therapy.29, 30 When applied in combination with mirror therapy, the real stimulation group improved significantly compared to the sham stimulation group.29, 30 The studies that evaluated the frequency of PLP applied anodic stimulation with an intensity of 129 mA or 1.5 mA,27, 30 for 1527 or 20 minutes29, 30 and performed a total of 1,29 527 and 10 sessions.30 PLP frequency decreased in subjects with unilateral upper25, 27, 29 and lower extremity27, 28, 30 amputation, regardless of the level and months since amputation.

PLS

PLS were analyzed by six studies.25-28, 30 In three of these studies,25, 26 PLS improved after application of tDCS. The studies that showed significant improvements applied anodic stimulation over the M1 contralateral to the painful limb25, 26 for 2025 or 15 minutes,26 in 126 or 5 sessions25 with an intensity of 2 mA,25, 26 and cathodic stimulation over the parietal cortex contralateral to the pain26 (1 session, 2 mA, 15 minutes). Improvements were observed in both lower26 and upper limb25 amputations.

The study by Bolognini et al. in 201326 that applied tDCS over the parietal cortex showed significant improvements in PLS only after application of cathodal stimulation, but not after anodal stimulation. The study by Bolognini et al. in 2015,27 showed no significant improvement over PLS but significant improvement over PLP. The study by Gunduz et al.28 showed no significant improvements in PLS but significant improvement over PLP after applying 10 sessions of 2 mA anodal tDCS for 20 min combined with mirror therapy. The study by Segal et al.30 showed no significant improvements in PLS but significant improvement over PLP after applying 10 sessions of 1.5 mA anodal tDCS for 20 min combined with mirror therapy.

Psychiatric disorders

Psychiatric disorders were assessed in two studies.27, 28 Both studies showed no significant improvement over the intervention groups in terms of depression and anxiety, only a slight improvement in the depression aspect has been described in the study by Bolognini et al.26

Discussion

The aim of this review was to analyze the applicability of tDCS in the treatment of PLP after amputation. It seems that the use of tDCS should be a treatment of choice in the presence of PLP in subjects with lower or upper limb amputation. The strongest evidence is presented by tDCS alone or combined with mirror therapy when anodal stimulation of 1-2 mA is applied over the M1 cortex contralateral to the amputated limb for 15-20 minutes. The duration of PLP decrease seems to depend on the number of sessions, thus, single session protocols achieve immediate relief of PLP, and several sessions achieve more prolonged effects. As for PLS and psychiatric disorders such as depression or anxiety, they seem to be independent of PLP and should be treated with different stimulation protocols. These results should be taken with caution, as the tDCS technique presents a high interindividual variability of response, and protocols remain highly variable. In the following, the effect of tDCS in the management of people with upper and lower limb amputation with PLP will be analyzed according to subject characteristics and stimulation parameters.

Intensity of PLP

In all studies, included subjects had PLP with a mean greater than 4.6 in the VAS. All included studies significantly reduced the intensity of PLP, regardless of PLP baseline and chronicity, after applying anodic tDCS over the M1 contralateral to the affected limb or bilaterally over the cerebellum, finding different results after applying anodal or cathodal stimulation over the contralateral parietal cortex. Among the studies that indicated the mean pain reduction after tDCS application, this was greater than 2.3 in VAS in two studies,28, 30 and less than 2.3 (1.6) in a single study.25 It is considered that 2.3 is the minimum clinically important change for subjects suffering both chronic and acute pain,31, 32 being related to a reduction in medication intake, although these values depend on baseline pain and present great heterogeneity in the methods used to obtain them.

There was a difference between single-session and multi-session protocols, with the number of sessions being an important factor in achieving long-term PLP reduction. Studies that applied a single session, achieved a significant improvement in PLP relief of a few hours;26, 29 studies that applied multiple sessions,25, 27, 28, 30 achieved significant improvements in a reduction of approximately 40-50% in PLP that could last up to a couple of months after stimulation.30

Compared with other NIBS, tDCS appears to be superior to rTMS in reducing PLP in these patients, according to the results of the meta-analysis by Pacheco et al.,3 although they only analyzed 2 studies.

PLS

PLS is usually coupled with PLP, but it could be an independent factor in terms of tDCS treatment, as they respond differently to PLP. There were studies in which PLP improved significantly, whereas PLS did not.26, 28, 30 The study by Bolognini et al. in 201326 applied cathodal and anodal stimulation on the parietal cortex; cathodal stimulation significantly improved PLS compared to anodal stimulation. The same occurred in the study by Bocci et al.25 when bilateral anodic stimulation was applied over the cerebellum, which significantly improved compared to sham.

Most of the included studies applied anodic stimulation over area M1 and did not show significant improvements, except for one study.26 Therefore, PLS, even if present together with PLP, seems to be treated with a different electrode placement.

Psychiatric disorders

Psychiatric symptoms, especially depression and anxiety, are commonly present in people with amputation, being related to different factors, such as the acceptance of the new condition, or the disability produced by the loss of a limb. These factors may influence the onset of PLP and may aggravate it, just as pain may aggravate psychiatric symptomatology. However, the brain mechanisms involved in both processes involve different cortical areas.6 Within the present review, two studies included assessment measures around psychiatric disorders, namely depression27, 28 and anxiety.27 The results were not very significant, with a slight improvement for depression in the study by Bolognini et al.27 In several studies in which the main objective was to evaluate the effect on depression, tDCS has shown positive effects in reducing depression with different protocols,34 with the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) being the main treatment area, as in the study by Padberg et al.35 Other NIBS techniques, such as rTMS, have also been shown to reduce depression when applied over the left DLPFC.36, 37 Thus, psychiatric disorders seem to be independent of PLP in people with amputation and should be treated with different protocols than PLP even though they are two closely related phenomena. Left DLPFC could be the electrode placement of choice when people with amputation suffer from psychiatric symptoms combined with M1 area stimulation for the treatment of PLP.

Treatment parameters

The most applied stimulation in the treatment of PLP was anodic stimulation,25-30 although cathodic stimulation has also been applied.26 Anodic stimulation was useful in the reduction of PLP, like the reduction of neuropathic pain in other pathologies, such as spinal cord injury.38 Due to this fact, anodic stimulation could be a treatment of choice in people with amputation, since it also provides positive results in improvements in the control of myoelectric prostheses.39, 40 Studies that have tested the use of other NIBS, such as rTMS, have found that inhibitory41, 42 and excitatory4 protocols have a positive effect on PLP reduction, although this is still poorly studied.43 The studies included in this review have focused on the primary motor cortex as the area of stimulation.26-30 The cerebellum25 and parietal cortex26 were also evaluated by two studies, with the parietal cortex being the area that did not show significant reductions in pain. These areas correspond to the most studied cortical areas in terms of the application of tDCS focused on pain reduction, being the M1 area and the prefrontal cortex (in some studies combined with behavioral interventions) the areas that have shown the greatest effectiveness.44 The parietal cortex has not been studied by many authors and does not seem to be a target area of stimulation for pain reduction; however, some authors consider that the cerebellum could be an area of interest45 due to its relationship with sensory-motor integration related to analgesic process46 and in behavioral responses to nociceptive stimulation. Stimulation of the cerebellum has been shown to modulate painful sensation in humans probably by interfering with the inhibitory tone exerted by the cerebellum over cortical areas.47, 48

In other disorders that often present with neuropathic pain, such as multiple sclerosis, stimulation has been applied with promising results on the left DLPFC, which could be an area of interest in people with amputation as it shows significant improvements in pain and psychiatric disorders such as major depression,49 which are closely related in the PLP. The applied current intensities ranged between 1mA and 2mA, ranging the current density from 0.028 to 0.057 mA/cm2. Regarding the protocol applied, the intensity, stimulation time and number of sessions were similar to the most commonly used protocols in terms of neuropathic pain.50 The most recommended parameters to reduce PLP seem to be the following: anodic stimulation on the M1 contralateral to the affected limb, with an intensity ranging from 1 to 2 mA, for 15-20 minutes, in multi-session protocols, from 5 to 10. In chronic pain, the most recommended protocols include several treatment sessions to achieve a prolonged analgesic effect over time.22 The combined treatment of tDCS and mirror therapy was applied in two recent studies,28, 30 being previously proposed by Pinto et al.51 The combined treatment significantly reduced pain intensity, with a robust and long-lasting analgesic effect,28, 30 being superior to mirror therapy alone or combined with sham stimulation. This fact could change the viewpoint of current treatment options for PLP in people with amputation, which have been based on pharmacological interventions with gabapentin, amitriptyline, tricyclic antidepressants, morphine, or ketamine,52 and rehabilitation, including techniques such as mirror therapy or motor imagery. A current review concluded that mirror therapy should not be taken as the first treatment option in the management of PLP in people with amputation,53 so tDCS could be a treatment of choice combined with pharmacological treatment. The tDCS allows its use in combination with treatment strategies that have demonstrated efficacy in reducing pain and can the analgesic effect produced by these therapies. Thus, tDCS presents the possibility of using new treatment targets in people suffering from PLP. Therapies that could be combined with tDCS for the improvement of PLP that have been shown to be effective in other types of pain are: exercise,54 transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, peripheral electrical stimulation, and intramuscular electrical stimulation.55 To this end, tDCS should be extensively studied to know the best protocol option in each patient.

Side effects of intervention

The studies reported minor adverse effects after the procedure, such as headache, neck pain, scalp pain, scalp burning, reddened skin, drowsiness, difficulty concentrating, or mood changes.26-30 No adverse reactions were mentioned in the remaining reports.

Methodological quality and risk of bias

Regarding the assessment of methodological quality, all but one of the studies included in the review27 obtained the maximum score in the Oxford quality scoring system; therefore, they are studies of high methodological quality. The study that obtained a moderate score was because they did not reflect how they did the randomization. Regarding the risk of bias assessment, four studies showed a low risk in the risk of bias tool of Cochrane Library criteria, while two studies28, 30 showed some concerns in the risk of implementation bias because they could not blind the application of covered mirror therapy. In addition, one study showed some concerns because it did not mention random sequence generation and allocation concealment.27

Strengths and limitations of the study

We present a solid systematic review in which we analyzed the existing literature on the management of PLP in people with amputation using tDCS. We were able to extract a precise idea of the effectiveness and the protocol to be applied. This review has a good methodological quality, being composed only of RCTs of high methodological quality and low risk of bias. The main limitation found was the low number of studies and sample size, as well as the heterogeneity of outcome measures and availability of statistical results appropriate for meta-analysis.

Conclusions

The evidence provided by the included studies suggests that anodic tDCS over the M1 contralateral to the affected limb, with an intensity of 1-2 mA, for 15-20 minutes appears to significantly reduce PLP in people with amputation. Single-session treatment could modify PLP intensity for hours, and multi-session treatment could reduce PLP intensity and frequency for months. Limited evidence suggests that PLS and psychiatric disorders should be treated with different PLP electrode placements. Further studies with larger sample size and longer follow-up times are needed to establish the priority of application of tDCS and the possibility of combination with other therapies in the presence of PLP in people with amputation.
