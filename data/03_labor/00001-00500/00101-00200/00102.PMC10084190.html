Abstract

Objective

Acromegaly is a condition characterized by an overproduction of growth hormone which infers high morbidity and mortality if left untreated. The objective of this review is to analyse and appraise the current evidence for the generalized use of preoperative medications and the various surgical approaches as described in the literature.

Design

A thorough search from MEDLINE via PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library has been performed which identified a total of 37 papers.

Conclusion

The preoperative use of somatostatin receptor agonists (SAs) in acromegaly is a controversial topic with current guidelines suggesting against their generalized routine use. Most authors noticed an insignificant long‐term remission of acromegaly when given SAs compared with nil preoperative therapy, except for invasive macroadenomas as SAs have been found to reduce the tumour volume and aid towards the total resection of the adenoma. Furthermore, according to the evidence available, endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery is the optimum method for hypophysectomy in terms of its remission and safety profile.

INTRODUCTION

1

The clinical features observed in patients with acromegaly result from both an overproduction of growth hormone (GH) but also from the consequent overproduction of the insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF1) and from the physical enlargement of the pituitary gland. This condition substantially affects the quality of life of patients and increases morbidity and mortality risk.1

Preoperative medical treatment may be employed to either achieve symptom relief or reduction in the size of the pituitary adenoma to aid in the surgical removal of the gland, or to mitigate any intra‐ and postoperative risks, or all of the above. There is a difficulty in establishing an evidence base that is reliable and of high quality because of the rarity and variability of these tumours. The current guidelines by the Endocrine Society suggest against the generalized routine use of presurgical medical treatment except in very specific cases.2 The only preoperative treatments currently available are SAs (in particular octreotide and lanreotide), however, their use is limited to patients with severe nasopharyngeal thickening causing sleep apnoea or severe heart failure.3 Following removal of the pituitary gland, the patients must have their IGF1 and GH levels measured. The soonest this should be done to establish the nadir value to assess for remission is at 12 weeks following the completion of surgery or later. Earlier measurements, however, can be of value. The regression of plasma IGF1 takes a long time to be accomplished due to the longer half‐life in comparison with GH and due to the bound IGF1 on IGF‐binding proteins. SAs—if administered perioperatively—can have a carry‐on effect following the surgery due to their long half‐life; thus they may still have an impact on residual somatotroph cells and early blood tests could provide inaccurate reassurance of cure.4

According to the current guidelines of the Endocrine Society, the first‐line treatment for acromegaly is transsphenoidal surgery (TSS).2 However, there are alternative options for the treatment of acromegaly including primary medical treatment, transcranial surgery, and radiation therapy. Treatment should be personalized to allow for an optimum rate of remission with low rates of postoperative morbidity. Surgical procedures could be carried out using a microscope or endoscope.5 An older and now rarely used approach for pituitary surgery is via the transcranial route. This might still be used in an extremely large tumour with extensive invasion of nearby structures like the cavernous sinus and a large suprasellar component.

This article will focus on analyzing the evidence for the preoperative medical therapy on patients with acromegaly and on the various surgical approaches described in the literature.

METHODS

2

Search strategy

2.1

A thorough search of the online literature was conducted up to and including the 14th of June 2021 in the following databases: MEDLINE via PubMed, Cochrane library, and EMBASE. Google Scholar was also extensively used. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) system was used for standardization.6 All the references from the identified papers were also screened to identify potentially eligible studies.

The search strategy used to identify papers relating to Acromegaly was: (“Acromegaly” OR “GH secreting” OR “GH adenoma”) AND (“Preoperative” OR “Presurgical” OR “Perioperative” OR “Anaesth*” OR “Anesth*”).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

2.2

This systematic review included papers that (1) were original studies, (2) written in the English Language, (3) published during or after the year 2000, (4) included only adult patients, (5) discussed preoperative medical treatment of acromegaly OR discussed a surgical approach for acromegaly, and (6) included at least one of the outcomes of interest, which are outlined in the next section. Therefore, we excluded all case reports, case series, editorials, letters to the editor, and animal studies. We have also excluded all papers dating from before the year 2000. This is because medical and surgical care of patients has substantially changed since the 1990s.7 Furthermore, all papers not specifically discussing patients with acromegaly were excluded. Papers were therefore excluded if they focussed in general on patients undergoing hypophysectomy without specifying the underlying disease in those patients.

Quantitative measures of interest

2.3

The outcomes we were interested in discussing were, first, the intra‐ and postoperative morbidity of patients by looking at the complications during or shortly after surgery. Second, we were interested in mortality during or shortly after the surgical procedure. Third, we were interested at the short‐ or long‐term remission of patients. A remission was considered as short‐term if the last biochemical investigations for remission were conducted less than or equal to a period of 6 months after the surgical procedure, the patients did not exhibit any clinical features of recurrence and they did not receive any adjuvant treatment before their last biochemical investigation. A remission was considered long‐term if the last biochemical investigations for remission were conducted more than 6 months after the surgical procedure, the patients did not exhibit any clinical features of recurrence and they did not receive any adjuvant treatment before their last biochemical investigation. All papers, independent of their remission timeline, were included in the review. If a article included the remission rates for a series of dates throughout time, the last/most recent was taken into consideration.

Quality assessment of studies included

2.4

We used the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB‐2) tool to assess the quality of the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS) to assess the quality of the included non‐RCTs.8, 9 The ROB‐2 tool scale ranges from “high bias”, “low bias”, or “unclear bias”. The maximum score for a cohort study according to the NOS scale is 9 stars, whereas the maximum score for a cross‐sectional study is 8 stars. Articles which were defined with 0–3 stars are said to be at high risk of bias, those with 4–6 stars are of intermediate risk of bias, and those with 7–8/9 stars are of low risk of bias.

RESULTS

3

Our search strategy identified 607 articles. After screening the title and abstract of those papers, we excluded 538 articles as they did not meet inclusion criteria for our systematic review. Therefore, we reviewed 69 full‐text articles for the acromegaly patients. Out of these papers, 37 articles covering acromegaly patients were included in the qualitative synthesis of this systematic review. Synthesis of results

3.1

The quantitative outcomes of interest regarding the preoperative medical therapy and surgical approach of acromegaly patients were analyzed.

The studies included regarding preoperative medical therapy were four studies comparing the preoperative medical therapy using octreotide versus nil medical therapy, two studies comparing the use of lanreotide versus nil medical therapy, five studies comparing the use of either lanreotide or octreotide vs nil medical therapy, and three studies which were observational/cross‐sectional and non‐comparative. Analysis of the preoperative medical management of patients with acromegaly

3.3

There are two consensus criteria for remission. Within the articles selected for this portion of the review, 12/14 (85.7%) of articles characterized remission according to the 2000 consensus criteria [normal IGF1 level adjusted for age and gender AND GH level following OGTT < 1 μg/L OR random baseline GH level <2.5 μg/L], whereas 2/14 (14.3%) characterized remission according to the 2010 consensus criteria [normal IGF1 level adjusted for age and gender AND GH level following OGTT < 0.4 μg/L OR random baseline GH level <1 μg/L].10, 11

The analysis used for the determinacy of remission was the last biochemical assessment during follow‐up before the initiation of other postoperative medical or radiotherapeutic regimes. We divided patients into two groups. Those who were last assessed before or equal to 6 months post‐surgically (short‐term remission) and those who were last assessed more than 6 months post‐surgically (long‐term remission). Short‐term remission was assessed in 10/14 (71.4%) of papers, while long‐term remission was assessed in 4/14 (28.6%) of papers.

As illustrated in Figure 2, five articles had a statistically significant difference between the two comparative groups.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Lanreotide might be less efficacious than octreotide as all papers comparing octreotide to nil medical therapy had significant results. It is interesting also to note that six comparative articles did not find any significant differences in the overall rates of remission.

Most of the articles included in this review subdivided the remission of patients following surgery into patients with microadenomas and patients with macroadenomas. Carlsen 2008 had a significant difference in the remission of macroadenomas between the group taking SAs versus the group having nil medical treatment, equivalent to 50% versus 16%, respectively (p = .017).17 This was similar to Korkmaz 2019, who found a significant difference for macroadenomas too (p = .02).14 Regarding the microadenomas, only one article found a significant difference between the octreotide/lanreotide group versus the nil medical therapy group; 100% remission versus 62.1% remission respectively (p = .037). It was interesting to note that the same article, however, did not produce a significant difference in the macroadenoma patients (p = .39).18

In five of the articles a multivariate analysis was performed to analyse the factors which significantly (p < .05) reduced the overall rates of remission.12, 16, 19, 20, 21 Of those papers, they all agreed that a cavernous sinus invasion (i.e., Knosp criteria score III and IV) significantly reduced remission. Thus, all articles investigating the effects of SAs on invasive macroadenomas uniformly found a significant difference in remission compared to that of the group getting nil preoperative medical therapy. Duan 2017 found a 43.2% versus 17.6% remission between the groups, respectively (p = .004).18 Korkmaz 2019 found a 35.7% versus 4.8% remission, respectively (p = .002).14 Li 2012 found a 45.8% versus 20% remission, respectively (p < .05).13 Finally, Shen 2010 found a 42.1% versus 10% remission, respectively (p = .044).12 This indicates that SAs can aid towards volume reduction in vivo which can ultimately help for a total adenoma resection in cases of invasion of the cavernous sinus. Two of the articles demonstrated that 29% and 15.8% of patients taking SAs had a > 20% reduction of tumour volume, respectively.20, 21

Other factors shown by the multivariate analysis to significantly reduce remission are young age, high basal preoperative GH levels, high basal preoperative IGF1 levels, and large tumour volume.

Complications of patients were less extensively studied. Only two complications were found to be significant. Albarel 2018 found that 13 patients taking SAs pre‐operatively had long‐term hyponatremia compared with one patient having nil medical therapy (p < .01).16 Moreover, Shen 2010 showed that 10.5% of patients taking octreotide pre‐operatively had cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage postoperatively compared with 45.0% of patients taking nil medical therapy (p = .031).12 All other reported complications were found to be insignificant between the groups.

Apart from the two cases of postoperative complications mentioned above, all other major or minor complications following surgery were found to be statistically insignificant if compared to a group with nil preoperative treatment. Shen 2010 did not disclose the number of patients having postoperative complications apart from the patients having CSF leak as it was found significant. Moreover, the only article analysing the rates of intra‐operative complications is Losa 2019, however, like Shen 2010, it did not disclose the number of patients having intra‐operative complications as they were all insignificant between the two groups.12, 19

The highest rates of postoperative complications are hypopituitarism (33 patients) which was defined by all articles as patients having at least one new pituitary hormonal deficiency following surgery. Then this was followed by long‐term hyponatremia (20 patients), transient DI (14 patients), permanent DI (8 patients), CN palsies (I or III or IV) (7 patients), infections (excluding sinusitis) (6 patients), rhinorrhoea (6 patients), CSF leak (6 patients), epistaxis (5 patients), sinusitis (2 patients), haematoma (1 patient), and finally traumatic carotid cavernous fistula (1 patient). The majority of these complications were minor in nature and were almost immediately resolved. Finally, from all papers mentioning peri‐operative mortality, there was nil mortality amongst all centres where the research took place. We decided to exclude the number of patients having CSF leak from Shen 2010 as the number of patients having other complications in that particular study is unknown and would overestimate the results in the “CSF leak” column.

Analysis of the surgical approach to patients with acromegaly

3.4

Of the included articles in this section, 12/23 (52.2%) of the articles have used the 2000 criteria and 11/23 (47.8%) have used the 2010 criteria. Of the comparative studies included 4/5 (80%) use the 2010 criteria and only Yao 2021 uses the 2000 criteria.22

There are 17/23 (73.9%) of papers mentioning the long‐term remission of patients and 6/23 (26.1%) of the articles that have a short‐term remission. Of the comparative studies, 4/5 (80%) have a long‐term remission and Yao 2021 has a short‐term remission.

From the cross‐sectional studies discussing about remission, 11/17 (64.7%) regard endoscopic TSS, 4/17 (23.5%) regard microscopic TSS, and 2/17 (11.8) regard TSS without specifying the subtype.

Of the articles included, only 5/23 (21.7%) are comparative studies and nil are RCTs.22, 23, 24, 25, 26 The rest of the articles 18/23 (78.2%) are cross‐sectional/observational.

Figure 3 shows the amalgamated median rates of remission for patients undergoing the different sub‐types of TSS from all the cross‐sectional articles discussing about TSS. Each category is then subdivided into “overall”, “microadenoma”, and “macroadenoma” rates of remission. As illustrated in Figure 3, the median remission rate, independent of the subtype of TSS, is higher in patients with microadenoma compared to the overall and macroadenoma remission rates. In the “Microscopic TSS” and “TSS” groups, the macroadenoma remission rates are a bit higher than the overall. This is because some of the articles included only the overall remission rate or the macroadenoma remission rate, which could potentially underestimate the median overall remission or overestimate the median macroadenoma remission rates. The “TSS” group in Figure 3 refers to articles that did not distinguish between endoscopic and microscopic TSS.

The median remission rates are as follows: TSS 58% overall, 67% microadenomas, 60% macroadenomas; Endoscopic TSS 66.25% overall, 90.65% microadenomas, 61.2% macroadenomas; Microscopic TSS 57.7% overall, 81.8% microadenomas, 56% macroadenomas. Hence, our results point towards a better prognostic outcome with endoscopic TSS. This is verified via Fathalla 2015, who found a remission rate of 45.2% in endoscopic TSS compared with 34.7% in microscopic TSS.23 Nevertheless, this difference was found to be insignificant. Sarkar 2014 found a higher remission rate in the group having microscopic TSS compared with endoscopic TSS but again it was insignificant (p = .421).24 Starke 2013 did not disclose the exact remission rate percentages but were again insignificant between the groups. Consequently, even though there are some differences between the types of surgeries performed none is significantly better.

Transcranial surgery was more frequently performed before the introduction of TSS for the removal of the pituitary gland. However, it is rarely, if ever, performed the last couple of years. Yao 2021, is the only published paper after the year 2000 which compared TSS with transcranial surgery. It was a multicentre study that recruited 546 patients. The overall remission rate in the TSS group was 37.6% versus 21.4% in the transcranial group. Yet this was statistically insignificant (p > .05).22

Xie 2016 compares the use of endoscopic TSS with and without extra‐capsular resection.26 The results showed a significantly higher remission in patients having extra‐capsular resection versus no extra‐capsular resection (85.7% vs. 54.5%, respectively p < .05). Hence, according to the articles included in this review, the use of endoscopic TSS with extra‐capsular resection might infer the best prognosis.

Several of the included studies analyzed the risk factors which significantly (p < .05) reduced the remission via a multivariate analysis. All but three articles analyzing the predictors of remission showed that cavernous sinus invasion significantly reduced the rates of remission. It was interesting to see that 10 authors concluded that high basal preoperative GH levels significantly reduced remission but only two authors found the same regarding basal preoperative IGF1 levels. Furthermore, Yao 2021 found that even GH levels >28 μg/L were high enough to significantly alter prognosis.22 On the other hand, Anik 2017 found that >60 μg/L is considered to be “high” enough.27

Postoperative complications were described by most authors in these papers. Regarding the cohort studies comparing endoscopic vs microscopic TSS, Fathalla 2015 found significantly more transient DI in the endoscopic group compared with the microscopic group (34.7% vs. 17% p = .05 respectively) and more permanent DI cases (17.3% vs. 4.8% p = .04, respectively).23 Sarkar 2014 found the rates of hypopituitarism to be different between the groups. The endoscopic TSS group had lower rates of hypopituitarism compared to the microscopic TSS group (18.2% vs. 28.3% p = .029, respectively).24 Finally, Starke 2013 found only the self‐reported sinusitis to be different.25 Consequently, one cannot deduce the superiority of one surgical approach in terms of safety. When analysing the endoscopic TSS with and without extra‐capsular resection nil complications were significant; likewise, when comparing TSS with transcranial surgery.

The most frequent complications encountered in the cross‐sectional studies in articles analysing either patients undergoing endoscopic TSS or microscopic TSS are transient DI, hypopituitarism, CSF leak, and permanent DI. Hypopituitarism is defined by all papers as patients having at least one new pituitary hormonal deficiency following surgery. The median percentage of these complications calculated from all the cross‐sectional studies is shown in Figure 4. As illustrated in Figure 4, the rates of all complications are slightly higher in the microscopic TSS group of patients. The biggest difference is seen in the levels of postoperative hypopituitarism between microscopic TSS (9.3%) versus endoscopic TSS (5%). Median proportion of patients experiencing transient DI was 9.9% in the microscopic TSS cohort versus 9% in the endoscopic TSS cohort. Median proportion of patients experiencing CSF leak was 8.3% in the microscopic TSS cohort versus 6% in the endoscopic TSS cohort. Finally, permanent DI, which had the lowest incidence, occurred in median 2.45% of patients in the microscopic TSS cohort versus 2.35% in the endoscopic TSS cohort.

Quality assessment

3.5

In the included articles about the preoperative medical therapy of the patients with acromegaly, five articles were RCTs, six articles were cohort studies, and three articles were cross‐sectional studies. Whereas, about the surgical approach papers of the acromegaly patients, nil were RCTs, five articles were cohort studies and 18 articles were cross‐sectional studies.

All the RCTs were of overall low risk of bias. From the cohort studies, 10/11 (90.9%) were of low risk of bias, and 1/11 (9.1%) were of intermediate risk of bias. From the cross‐sectional studies, 5/22 (22.7%) were of low risk of bias, and 17/22 (77.3%) were of intermediate risk of bias. The analysis of risk of bias per article is found in Appendix 1.

DISCUSSION

4

Critiques of the studies concerning the preoperative medical management of patients with acromegaly

4.1

It was noteworthy to see that more articles comparing octreotide to placebo produced significant results compared with articles comparing lanreotide to placebo. However, no study in the literature compares lanreotide to octreotide face‐to‐face, for the preoperative therapy of patients with acromegaly. Furthermore, another conclusion that confidently can be drawn is the efficacy of SAs on invasive macroadenomas. This is because all papers which compared cohorts taking SAs versus cohorts taking placebo in invasive macroadenomas, found a statistically significant higher rate of remission for the cohorts taking SAs. Second, the majority of studies doing multivariate analysis, found that Knosp criteria III or IV correlated to significantly (p < .05) fewer remissions, meanwhile SAs can reduce the volume of tumours which aids towards a total resection of the adenoma.20, 21

Due to the two available consensus criteria for remission of patients with acromegaly, not all papers were uniform in the characterization of remission. The majority of papers have characterized remission according to the "less strict" 2000 consensus criteria, whereas only two articles characterized remission according to the “more strict” 2010 consensus criteria. Therefore, publications that have defined remission according to the 2010 consensus criteria, might have underestimated remission percentages in comparison with the other available studies.16, 19

The majority of articles in this section have only provided a short‐term remission for their patients. It might be considered that even in statistically significant differences between short‐term remission of groups, the results are unconvincing as SAs have a carry‐over effect after the surgery. Hence, the high rates of remission in the group of patients taking SAs might not be clinically translated in the long run. Albarel 2018 is the only article whose last follow‐up was more than 6 months postoperatively and had significant results when comparing SAs versus nil medical therapy, encouraging the theory of a carry‐over effect of SAs. To add to this, it is the only article assessing remission according to the 2010 criteria that had significant differences, hence this makes us wonder about the quality of the other articles since if they might have assessed their patients according to the 2010 criteria, some might not have produced significant results again.16

A limitation of this review is the equal weighting given to papers independent of their quality of evidence. Most articles are retrospective studies which are susceptible to selection bias and only 4/14 (28.6%) are prospective studies, with only Fougner 2014 being a prospective study assessing the long‐term remission of patients. Fougner 2014 did not produce any significance in the remission of patients.29

According to two meta‐analyses, nil found a significantly better long‐term biochemical remission of acromegaly when taking SAs.30, 31 One of which found an odds ratio (OR) of 1.49 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.95–2.32 p = .08) and the other found a RR of 1.03 (95% CI 0.86–1.24). On the other hand, meta‐analyses showed a statistically significant higher short‐term biochemical remission of patients when given preoperative SAs with an OR of 2.07 (95% CI 1.50–2.87 p < .00001), and RR of 2.15 (95% CI 1.39–3.33), respectively30, 32; which is in agreement with three of the studies included in this review.12, 14, 15

Critiques of the studies concerning the surgical approach to patients with acromegaly

4.2

The online published literature on the topic of surgical approach differs from that available for preoperative medical therapy for acromegaly and how it impacts morbidity and mortality/remission. In general, it encompasses articles that are of far lower quality as the majority of articles are cross‐sectional/observational which discuss particular surgical approaches without comparing them to a different group. Only 4/23 (17.4%) of the papers provided a comparator and none of which are prospective which again infers the risk of selection bias in a retrospective study. Patient characteristics could potentially differ between centres and this can influence surgical outcomes.

One advantage of the papers in this section is the fact that most papers look at long‐term remission and almost half of the studies have adopted the 2010 consensus criteria. Consequently, applicability to current clinical practice is high and the results can be translated into real clinical scenarios.

It was important to find that according to the cross‐sectional studies endoscopic TSS might be better than microscopic TSS in terms of remission and safety. The results from our systematic review regarding remission match other meta‐analyses available that compare endoscopic to microscopic TSS. Three meta‐analyses found that endoscopic TSS correlated to a higher rate of gross tumour removal and hence higher remission than microscopic TSS (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.11–2.08 p = .009) and (OR 1.655, 95% CI 1.131–2.421 p = .01).33, 34, 35 However, other meta‐analyses did not find any significant differences in the postoperative complications between endoscopic and microscopic TSS.35, 36

The insignificant difference in the remission rates provided by Yao 2021 might be invalid. This is because transcranial surgery is now chosen when a patient has a large invasive adenoma which would reduce the future remission rates anyway. Consequently, due to the possible variability in the confounding variables, we cannot deduce a certain answer from these results. Unfortunately, Yao 2021 has not provided whether the confounding variables were controlled‐for or not.22

Regarding the paper by Xie 2016 analysing the use of extra‐capsular resection, even though the results of the remission rates were significant, we have to bear in mind that this is the only article in the literature analysing this effect. Hence, it is not uncommon for a different author to find completely opposite results. The percentage error in Xie 2016s results might be high due to the small sample size in his cohorts (sum of 43 patients only). Hence, this might not be applicable to current clinical practise.26

When comparing the safety profile of microscopic TSS versus endoscopic TSS, we need to consider the discrepancy in the number of studies analysing each type of surgery. There are far fewer papers analysing the microscopic TSS compared with endoscopic TSS.

Finally, no articles published in the literature discusses a particular anaesthetic management consideration that can impact morbidity, mortality, or disease control. The only available studies discuss about the anesthetic approach of all patients undergoing hypophysectomy without specifying which diseases the patients had. Hence, we cannot distinguish whether those effects apply to acromegaly patients.

CONCLUSION

5

This systematic review thoroughly summarizes the available literature regarding the perioperative management of acromegaly and it includes all articles with the available preoperative medical therapies and surgical approaches. Due to the rarity of this condition, there is a difficulty in establishing an evidence base that is reliable and of high quality. Nonetheless, the literature analysing the treatment of this endocrine condition is expanding which aids toward a treatment that can be individualized according to the patient's signs, symptoms, concerns, and expectations. Most authors established conclusions that agree with the current guidelines of Endocrine Society which suggest against the generalized use of SAs for acromegaly. However, the guidelines should be reviewed to account for patients with macroadenomas as sometimes the preoperative use of SAs for these patients can prove beneficial. Furthermore, according to the evidence available, endoscopic TSS infers a higher remission rate compared with microscopic TSS with a better postoperative safety profile in terms of complications following the completion of surgery.

## INTRODUCTION

The clinical features observed in patients with acromegaly result from both an overproduction of growth hormone (GH) but also from the consequent overproduction of the insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF1) and from the physical enlargement of the pituitary gland. This condition substantially affects the quality of life of patients and increases morbidity and mortality risk.1

Preoperative medical treatment may be employed to either achieve symptom relief or reduction in the size of the pituitary adenoma to aid in the surgical removal of the gland, or to mitigate any intra‐ and postoperative risks, or all of the above. There is a difficulty in establishing an evidence base that is reliable and of high quality because of the rarity and variability of these tumours. The current guidelines by the Endocrine Society suggest against the generalized routine use of presurgical medical treatment except in very specific cases.2 The only preoperative treatments currently available are SAs (in particular octreotide and lanreotide), however, their use is limited to patients with severe nasopharyngeal thickening causing sleep apnoea or severe heart failure.3 Following removal of the pituitary gland, the patients must have their IGF1 and GH levels measured. The soonest this should be done to establish the nadir value to assess for remission is at 12 weeks following the completion of surgery or later. Earlier measurements, however, can be of value. The regression of plasma IGF1 takes a long time to be accomplished due to the longer half‐life in comparison with GH and due to the bound IGF1 on IGF‐binding proteins. SAs—if administered perioperatively—can have a carry‐on effect following the surgery due to their long half‐life; thus they may still have an impact on residual somatotroph cells and early blood tests could provide inaccurate reassurance of cure.4

According to the current guidelines of the Endocrine Society, the first‐line treatment for acromegaly is transsphenoidal surgery (TSS).2 However, there are alternative options for the treatment of acromegaly including primary medical treatment, transcranial surgery, and radiation therapy. Treatment should be personalized to allow for an optimum rate of remission with low rates of postoperative morbidity. Surgical procedures could be carried out using a microscope or endoscope.5 An older and now rarely used approach for pituitary surgery is via the transcranial route. This might still be used in an extremely large tumour with extensive invasion of nearby structures like the cavernous sinus and a large suprasellar component.

This article will focus on analyzing the evidence for the preoperative medical therapy on patients with acromegaly and on the various surgical approaches described in the literature.
## METHODS

### Search strategy

A thorough search of the online literature was conducted up to and including the 14th of June 2021 in the following databases: MEDLINE via PubMed, Cochrane library, and EMBASE. Google Scholar was also extensively used. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) system was used for standardization.6 All the references from the identified papers were also screened to identify potentially eligible studies.

The search strategy used to identify papers relating to Acromegaly was: (“Acromegaly” OR “GH secreting” OR “GH adenoma”) AND (“Preoperative” OR “Presurgical” OR “Perioperative” OR “Anaesth*” OR “Anesth*”).

### Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This systematic review included papers that (1) were original studies, (2) written in the English Language, (3) published during or after the year 2000, (4) included only adult patients, (5) discussed preoperative medical treatment of acromegaly OR discussed a surgical approach for acromegaly, and (6) included at least one of the outcomes of interest, which are outlined in the next section. Therefore, we excluded all case reports, case series, editorials, letters to the editor, and animal studies. We have also excluded all papers dating from before the year 2000. This is because medical and surgical care of patients has substantially changed since the 1990s.7 Furthermore, all papers not specifically discussing patients with acromegaly were excluded. Papers were therefore excluded if they focussed in general on patients undergoing hypophysectomy without specifying the underlying disease in those patients.

### Quantitative measures of interest

The outcomes we were interested in discussing were, first, the intra‐ and postoperative morbidity of patients by looking at the complications during or shortly after surgery. Second, we were interested in mortality during or shortly after the surgical procedure. Third, we were interested at the short‐ or long‐term remission of patients. A remission was considered as short‐term if the last biochemical investigations for remission were conducted less than or equal to a period of 6 months after the surgical procedure, the patients did not exhibit any clinical features of recurrence and they did not receive any adjuvant treatment before their last biochemical investigation. A remission was considered long‐term if the last biochemical investigations for remission were conducted more than 6 months after the surgical procedure, the patients did not exhibit any clinical features of recurrence and they did not receive any adjuvant treatment before their last biochemical investigation. All papers, independent of their remission timeline, were included in the review. If a article included the remission rates for a series of dates throughout time, the last/most recent was taken into consideration.

### Quality assessment of studies included

We used the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB‐2) tool to assess the quality of the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS) to assess the quality of the included non‐RCTs.8, 9 The ROB‐2 tool scale ranges from “high bias”, “low bias”, or “unclear bias”. The maximum score for a cohort study according to the NOS scale is 9 stars, whereas the maximum score for a cross‐sectional study is 8 stars. Articles which were defined with 0–3 stars are said to be at high risk of bias, those with 4–6 stars are of intermediate risk of bias, and those with 7–8/9 stars are of low risk of bias.
