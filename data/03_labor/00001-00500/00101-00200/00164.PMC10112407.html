Higher- versus Lower-Dose Corticosteroids for Severe to Critical COVID-19: A Systematic Review and Dose–Response Meta-analysis

Abstract

Rationale

Corticosteroids are standard of care for patients with severe coronavirus disease (COVID-19). However, the optimal dose is uncertain.

Objectives

To compare higher doses of corticosteroids with lower doses in patients with COVID-19.

Methods

We searched MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, MedRxiv, and Web of Science from inception to August 1, 2022, for trials that randomized patients with severe-to-critical COVID-19 to corticosteroids, standard care, or placebo. Reviewers, working in duplicate, screened references, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias using a modified version of the Cochrane risk of bias 2.0 tool. We performed a dose–response meta-analysis and used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to assess the certainty of evidence. We present our results both in relative risk and absolute risk difference per 1,000, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

We included 20 trials, with 10,155 patients. We show that, compared with lower-dose corticosteroids, higher-dose corticosteroids probably reduce mortality (absolute risk difference, 14 fewer deaths per 1,000 [95% CI, 26 fewer to 2 fewer]; moderate certainty) and may reduce the need for mechanical ventilation (absolute risk difference, 11.6 fewer per 1,000 [95% CI, 23.2 fewer to 6.9 more]; low certainty). The effect of corticosteroids on nosocomial infections is uncertain (16.7 fewer infections per 1,000 [95% CI, 5.4 fewer to 25.0 fewer]; very low certainty).

Conclusions

Relatively higher doses of corticosteroids may be beneficial in patients with severe-to-critical COVID-19 and may not increase the risk of nosocomial infections.

As of November 15, 2022, there have been >640 million cumulative cases of coronavirus disease (COVID-19) worldwide and >6.6 million deaths (1). In an attempt to improve outcomes for patients with COVID-19, investigators have, with varying results, repurposed drugs originally intended for other indications. There is compelling evidence that corticosteroids, IL-6 inhibitors, and Janus kinase inhibitor (JAK) inhibitors improve outcomes for patients with severe-to-critical COVID-19 (2).

Since the publication of the RECOVERY corticosteroid trial, which showed a reduction in mortality in severe-to-critical COVID-19, corticosteroids have become standard of care, due in part to their accessibility and low cost (3). Consistent with what was used in RECOVERY, the typical dose prescribed is 6 mg of dexamethasone for 10 days (a cumulative intended dose of 60 mg) (3). However, increasing evidence suggests that higher-dose corticosteroids may be superior to lower doses in reducing mortality, as described in the recent COVID-STEROIDS-2 study and the subsequent secondary Bayesian analysis (4, 5).

We present a systematic review and dose–response meta-analysis comparing relatively higher- versus lower-dose corticosteroids for severe-to-critical COVID-19.

Methods

We registered a protocol on Open Science Framework (osf.io/2n6qw). eProtocol presents the registered protocol (6).

Search Strategy

We developed a search strategy with the help of an experienced medical librarian. We searched MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, MedRxiv, and Web of Science from inception to January 7, 2022, with an update on August 1, 2022. Table E1 in the data supplement presents the search strategy, and the supplemental methods present further details on the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Eligibility Criteria

We included all trials that randomized adult patients (⩾18 yr old) with probable, suspected, or confirmed severe COVID-19 to corticosteroids versus standard care or placebo or to alternative doses of corticosteroids. We used World Health Organization definitions of disease severity (data supplement). For trials that reported on patients with mixed severity, we extracted subgroup data on patients with severe-to-critical COVID-19, when reported. When subgroup data were not reported, we only included trials with mixed severity if patients predominantly (>80%) had severe-to-critical disease.

Study Selection

We excluded trials that reported only on patients with nonsevere disease, trials in which mild disease made up ⩾20% of the trial sample and that did not report subgroup data on patients with severe-to-critical disease, and trials that compared interventions that were not corticosteroids with other interventions or standard care or placebo.

Statistically, we treated corticosteroid dosing as a continuous variable and compared arbitrary relatively higher-dose corticosteroids with lower-dose corticosteroids. We did this by choosing doses that have been typically compared in clinical trials (i.e., 12 mg vs. 6 mg). Two reviewers, after training and calibration exercises to ensure sufficient agreement, worked independently and in duplicate to screen titles and abstracts of search records and subsequently the full texts of records determined potentially eligible at the title and abstract screening stage. Reviewers resolved discrepancies by discussion or, when necessary, by third party adjudication.

Data Collection

Two reviewers, after calibration exercises to ensure sufficient agreement, worked independently and in duplicate to extract data from eligible trials. Reviewers resolved discrepancies by discussion or, when necessary, by third party adjudication. The data supplement presents further detail on data extraction.

Risks of Bias

Two reviewers, working independently and in duplicate, assessed risk of bias using a revision of the Cochrane tool for assessing risk of bias in randomized trials (RoB 2.0). We resolved discrepancies by discussion and, when necessary, with adjudication by a third party. Table E2 and the data supplement present more detail on the risk of bias tool and methods.

Statistical Methods

We conducted random-effects dose–response meta-analysis with the restricted maximum likelihood heterogeneity estimator using methods proposed by Orsini and colleagues (7) and Greenland and Longnecker (8). Dose–response meta-analysis estimates the association between doses of an exposure and the relative risk or mean difference of an outcome. We calculated the cumulative intended dose of corticosteroids administered during the trial by multiplying the administered dose by the duration (i.e., dexamethasone 6 mg/d for 10 d = 60 mg total dose) and used this number in the analysis. We then present the data as daily dosing for a 10-day period (reflecting current practice) to improve clinical understanding of the results.

To address whether results are driven by cumulative exposure to corticosteroids or the duration of exposure, for the outcomes of mortality, mechanical ventilation, and infections, we performed prespecified random-effects meta-regression and included the duration of treatment with corticosteroids as a moderator. We anticipated that if duration of exposure rather than cumulative exposure was important, we would see larger effects in the analysis of duration rather than cumulative dose. We used corticosteroid conversions to convert other types of corticosteroids to dexamethasone equivalents (9–11).

We summarized the effects of interventions using relative risk (RR) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) for dichotomous outcomes and mean difference with 95% CI for continuous outcomes. To facilitate interpretation for dichotomous outcomes, we calculated absolute risk differences per 1,000 patients and corresponding 95% CIs (12–14). We estimated the risk of mortality from recent epidemiological reports from Ontario, Canada to be 20%. We estimated the risk of invasive mechanical ventilation to be 11.6% from large cohort studies (15–17). For risk of nosocomial infections, we calculated the median risk in the standard care and placebo arms of the included trials.

For all outcomes, we present the data as continuous outcome per 60 mg of dexamethasone equivalent (then converted to daily dosing/10 d). Importantly, this is the intended dose, not the actual dose administered, as many die before they can complete the course and receive less than the intended dose. Although the outcomes are presented as continuous per increment increase in corticosteroid dose, we present our results comparing 12 mg/d for 10 days of dexamethasone equivalent with 6 mg/d for 10 days of dexamethasone equivalent, representing the range of doses most commonly considered in trials that have head-to-head comparisons (i.e., RECOVERY administered 6 mg/d for 10 d and COVID-STEROIDS-2 administered 12 mg/d for 10 d) (18). We present the results as such to improve clinical utility and understanding but does not restrict the analysis to these two comparisons between any two doses that can be derived by using the linear assumptions from the analysis, within the studied doses (0–160 mg). Importantly, although we established a linear association, it is not valid for doses greater than the studied range. From here onward, when we refer to “higher dose” we are referring to 12 mg/d for 10 days, and “lower dose” refers to 6 mg/d for 10 days.

We also performed two prespecified sensitivity analyses for mortality, restricting the analysis first to patients who were only on supplementary oxygen at baseline and second to patients who were receiving noninvasive and invasive mechanical ventilation at baseline. We performed one post hoc sensitivity analysis, investigating whether there were more frequent infections in trials predominately enrolling patients who required mechanical ventilation at baseline versus those who did not.

We performed all analyses using the dosresmeta and meta packages in R (version 4.03, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) (19, 20). The data and the code for generating the results presented in this manuscript are available on Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/PRUQJ).

As per protocol, we performed a network meta-analysis in addition to our dose–response analysis. We present the methods of this analysis in eProtocol, and more detail on our statistical analyses are presented in the data supplement.

Certainty of the Evidence

For all outcomes, reviewers, working independently and in duplicate, assessed the certainty (quality) of the evidence using the GRADE approach. We judged the certainty for each outcome as high, moderate, low, or very low, based on considerations of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

To make judgements regarding imprecision, we used a minimally contextualized approach, which considers only whether confidence intervals include a minimally important effect and does not consider the magnitude of plausible effects, captured by confidence intervals (21).

We describe results using guidance from the GRADE Working Group, based on the certainty of evidence and the magnitude of the effect (e.g., corticosteroids reduce mortality [high certainty], corticosteroids probably reduce mortality [moderate certainty], corticosteroids may reduce mortality [low certainty], and the effect of corticosteroids on mortality is very uncertain [very low certainty]) (22). More detail is presented in the data supplement.

Results

Our search identified 4,929 unique records. We describe 21 eligible trials including 10,155 patients (3, 4, 23–38). We excluded one trial from the meta-analysis that did not report on any outcomes of interest (37). Of the studies in which we contacted trial authors (4, 30, 34, 36, 38), we received a response from one trial (4). Data from two trials were collected from a prospective meta-analysis (27, 39, 40). Figure E1 presents more details on inclusion and exclusion process.

Trial Characteristics

A majority (75%) of trials were published in peer-reviewed journals, and all trials were published in English. Sixty-four percent of recruited subjects were male. The median age of patients was 61 years old (interquartile range, 58.6–64.2 yr). All patients were on oxygen therapy, and approximately one-fifth of the patients were mechanically ventilated at randomization. Six trials included patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) only (25, 27, 28, 31, 35, 37), four included patients with mixed ICU and non-ICU admissions (4, 24, 26, 36), three trials included no patients in the ICU at baseline (23, 29, 30), and six trials did not report baseline admission status (3, 32–34, 38, 39).

Ten trials reported on dexamethasone, eight on methylprednisolone, and three on hydrocortisone. Seven trials randomized patients to corticosteroids with ⩽6 mg dexamethasone equivalent/d for 10 days or standard care or placebo, eight trials randomized patients to >6 mg/d, and seven trials compared head-to-head corticosteroid doses (4, 30–32, 36, 38, 41). Risk of Bias

Table E4 presents our risk of bias ratings for all outcomes. Four trials out of 17 were at high or probably high risk of bias (25, 33, 34, 36), primarily due to bias arising from the randomization process, and the remainder were rated at low risk of bias.

Mortality

Eighteen trials, with 8,438 patients and 2,442 deaths, reported on mortality with a median follow-up of 28 days (3, 4, 23–28, 30–36, 41, 43). Eleven trials (6,313 patients) compared corticosteroids with standard care or placebo, and seven trials (2,125 patients) compared different doses of corticosteroids.

The dose–response meta-analysis shows that higher-dose corticosteroids probably reduce mortality more than lower dose (RR, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.85–0.98]) (moderate certainty). Table 2 presents additional details, including absolute risks and corresponding CIs.

Mechanical Ventilation

Twelve trials reported on the need for invasive mechanical ventilation, including 5,173 patients with 546 events, with a median follow-up of 12.5 days (3, 23, 24, 27, 28, 30, 32–36, 38). Eight trials (4,662 patients) compared corticosteroids with standard care or placebo, and four trials (511 patients) compared different doses of corticosteroids.

The dose–response meta-analysis shows that higher-dose corticosteroids may reduce the risk of mechanical ventilation compared with low-dose corticosteroids (RR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.87–1.03]; low certainty).

Duration of Invasive Mechanical Ventilation

Four trials, including 411 patients, reported on duration of mechanical ventilation (31, 35, 36, 38). One trial (299 patients) compared corticosteroids with standard care or placebo, and three trials (125 patients) compared different doses of corticosteroids.

The dose–response meta-analysis found that higher-dose corticosteroids may reduce the duration of mechanical ventilation compared with lower-dose corticosteroids (mean difference, 2.6 fewer days [95% CI, 6.1 fewer to 0.8 more days]; low certainty).

Duration of Hospitalization

Nine trials reported on the duration of hospitalization, including 7,489 patients. Five trials (7,041 patients) compared corticosteroids with standard or placebo and four trials (448 patients) compared different doses of corticosteroids.

Our dose–response meta-analysis found that higher-dose corticosteroids may have no benefit compared with lower-dose corticosteroids for reducing the duration of hospitalization (mean difference, 0.34 more days [95% CI, 0.74 fewer to 1.46 more days]; low certainty).

Nosocomial Infections

Nine trials reported on the risk of infections, including 2,311 patients with 568 reported infections, with a median follow-up of 28 days (4, 23, 24, 28, 29, 33, 35, 36, 38). More detail on separate types of infections is presented in the data supplement.

The dose–response meta-analysis found that higher-dose corticosteroids have an uncertain effect on nosocomial infection compared with low dose (RR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.83–0.97]; very low certainty).

Sensitivity Analyses

When we restricted the analysis of mortality to patients who were not receiving invasive mechanical ventilation at baseline, we found a larger reduction in mortality with corticosteroids. Figures E21–E26, as well as Table E7 and the data supplement, present more detail.

We also performed a subgroup analysis for risk of bias for higher versus lower corticosteroid use for trials that report higher versus lower doses. We found a statistically significant subgroup effect, with a statistically significant reduction in mortality in the low risk of bias subgroup (RR, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.74–0.99]) compared with the high risk of bias group (RR, 2.12 [95% CI, 1.06–4.23]). Only one trial was rated at high risk of bias and produced significantly discordant results with the remaining six other trials. Figure 2 presents the forest plot.

Discussion

Main Findings

We present the most up-to-date and comprehensive systematic review of the effectiveness of corticosteroids, both at low and high doses, for severe-to-critical COVID-19. We also establish the dose–response relationship between corticosteroids and clinically important outcomes, including nosocomial infections (44). We show that relatively higher-dose corticosteroids probably reduce mortality and may reduce the need for mechanical ventilation compared with lower-dose corticosteroids.

These results were pronounced among patients who were only receiving oxygen at baseline, with a larger effect size and less heterogeneity among those receiving supplemental oxygen therapy and not mechanically ventilated. These results suggest that treating patients with less severe disease with higher-dose dexamethasone may be more beneficial than treating patients who are mechanically ventilated—contrary to some guidelines that suggest using high-dose corticosteroids only in critical COVID-19 infections (45).

Although possible reduction or no difference in infections with relatively higher-dose corticosteroids may seem counterintuitive, because corticosteroids have immunosuppressive properties, these results may be explained by a reduction in the need for and duration of mechanical ventilation, which increases the risk of developing ventilator-associated pneumonia in COVID-19 (46). However, the certainty of the evidence precludes strong claims about the risk of infections in higher-dose corticosteroids, and clinicians need to consider the clinical scenario when making decisions concerning the use of higher-dose corticosteroids.

In Relation to Other Findings

The effectiveness of corticosteroids in severe-to-critical COVID-19 has been extensively studied in trials—the largest of which was RECOVERY, an open-label adaptive platform trial including 6,425 patients, showing an absolute risk reduction in mortality of 2.8% (3). Other trials, however, were smaller, and most did not show statistically significant reductions in mortality or other important outcomes. Therefore, the dose used in RECOVERY (6 mg of dexamethasone for 10 d) has become standard (47). Most trials studying corticosteroids in COVID-19, however, randomized patients to higher equivalent doses of corticosteroids than RECOVERY. Furthermore, successful trials that investigated corticosteroids in non-COVID acute respiratory distress syndrome used much higher dexamethasone doses than current practice for COVID-19 (48). Until now, meta-analyses have pooled these trials without attention to the dose of corticosteroids, thus obfuscating the true effect of higher-dose regimens (27, 49, 50). Before the present analysis, there has not been a comprehensive review of the optimal dosing of corticosteroids in severe-to-critical COVID-19 infection. This question is of particular importance, as corticosteroids are one of the most accessible and effective medications for severe-to-critical COVID-19.

Seven trials have directly compared higher- versus lower-dose corticosteroids (4, 30–32, 36, 38, 41). The largest of these was COVID-STEROIDS-2, which randomized 1,000 patients to either 12 or 6 mg of dexamethasone (5). Although there was no statistically significant benefit for higher- versus lower-dose corticosteroids across outcomes, the point estimate favored the higher dose. Furthermore, a prespecified Bayesian analysis of the COVID-STEROIDS2 trial further supports the use of relatively higher-dose regimens (5).

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this systematic review include a comprehensive search for eligible trials, the application of state-of-the-art methods to establish the dose–response relationships between COVID-19 and clinically important outcomes, and use of the most up-to-date GRADE methods to assess the certainty of evidence. We also performed sensitivity analyses to address whether duration of treatment for corticosteroids was driving the relationship rather than dose and to test whether results were consistent in patients receiving supplementary oxygen and mechanical ventilation at baseline.

We did not have important information for duration of hospitalization, including stratification by survivors. Although we were able to perform subgroup analysis for patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation versus oxygen only, there may be important differences when comparing these varying types of oxygen-only modalities, especially noninvasive modalities such as High flow nasal canula (HFNC), continuous positive airway pressure, and Bilevel ventilation. Future reviews and trials may be able to answer these questions.

Furthermore, although we did not believe there was important indirectness in the disease population to warrant rating down the certainty of the evidence, the changing landscape of the pandemic (i.e., vaccination status, early antiviral treatment) may change the overall effectiveness of corticosteroids in severe-to-critical COVID-19 infection (51). Future trials and real-world observational data are needed to determine the effect, if any, of these interventions on corticosteroids in this phenotype.

We also identified important heterogeneity in the results, particularly for the outcomes duration of mechanical ventilation and hospitalization This is, however, to be expected, because we anticipate factors in addition to dose to also influence trial results (e.g., baseline severity of patients). The COVID-STEROID trial, for example, reported increased mortality with corticosteroids, but it investigated very low–dose hydrocortisone and only included 3% of its planned sample size (26). Although Toroghi and colleagues reported harm with higher-dose corticosteroids, the trial investigated higher doses than are typically administered in clinical practice (240 mg dexamethasone), had a low sample size, and was at high risk of bias (36). Despite such inconsistencies, the pooled effect demonstrates efficacy, and the dose response suggests greater benefit with high-dose corticosteroids.

Novel Findings and Impact

Corticosteroids remain standard of care for the treatment of severe-to-critical COVID-19 because of their effectiveness, accessibility, and low cost—particularly for low- and middle-income countries. Despite increasing vaccination rates and the introduction of effective antiviral therapy for early disease, as of November 12, 2022, there are >20,000 patients in the hospital and 2,618 patients admitted to the ICU in the United States alone (52). Therefore, effective therapy for patients with severe-to-critical COVID-19 remains a priority. Corticosteroids are among the most effective and widely available treatments globally. Guidance on proper dosing for this life-saving medication is imperative. However, international and national guidelines, including the World Health Organization, have not addressed optimal dosing of corticosteroids (45). Our review suggests that a relatively higher-dose regimen of corticosteroids probably confers a larger mortality benefit than smaller-dose regimens. The RECOVERY trial, a large platform trial, is currently investigating the effects of higher-dose corticosteroids and will likely provide higher-certainty evidence addressing this issue (53). More recently, investigators announced that they recommended stopping recruitment of patients who did not require oxygen or only required simple oxygen at the time of randomization because of safety concerns. This result is not unexpected, as the original RECOVERY dexamethasone trial showed potential harm in patients not receiving oxygen (17.8% vs. 14.0% in the dexamethasone vs. standard care arms). Although this announcement included patients not receiving oxygen or patients on simple oxygen, the details have not been published. There are too many unknown variables to interpret the results of the trial thus far.

Until the results of this trial are published, clinicians and evidence users should consider using higher-dose regimens of corticosteroids, given the moderate certainty evidence that it reduces mortality based on the best available data. The advantage of the present analysis is that we can provide moderate certainty evidence now for a relatively available medication.

Conclusions

Compared with lower-dose corticosteroids, higher-dose corticosteroids probably reduce mortality and may reduce mechanical ventilation. Clinicians and guideline developers should consider benefits of treatment with higher doses of corticosteroids.

## (untitled)

As of November 15, 2022, there have been >640 million cumulative cases of coronavirus disease (COVID-19) worldwide and >6.6 million deaths (1). In an attempt to improve outcomes for patients with COVID-19, investigators have, with varying results, repurposed drugs originally intended for other indications. There is compelling evidence that corticosteroids, IL-6 inhibitors, and Janus kinase inhibitor (JAK) inhibitors improve outcomes for patients with severe-to-critical COVID-19 (2).

Since the publication of the RECOVERY corticosteroid trial, which showed a reduction in mortality in severe-to-critical COVID-19, corticosteroids have become standard of care, due in part to their accessibility and low cost (3). Consistent with what was used in RECOVERY, the typical dose prescribed is 6 mg of dexamethasone for 10 days (a cumulative intended dose of 60 mg) (3). However, increasing evidence suggests that higher-dose corticosteroids may be superior to lower doses in reducing mortality, as described in the recent COVID-STEROIDS-2 study and the subsequent secondary Bayesian analysis (4, 5).

We present a systematic review and dose–response meta-analysis comparing relatively higher- versus lower-dose corticosteroids for severe-to-critical COVID-19.
## Methods

We registered a protocol on Open Science Framework (osf.io/2n6qw). eProtocol presents the registered protocol (6).

### Search Strategy

We developed a search strategy with the help of an experienced medical librarian. We searched MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, MedRxiv, and Web of Science from inception to January 7, 2022, with an update on August 1, 2022. Table E1 in the data supplement presents the search strategy, and the supplemental methods present further details on the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

### Eligibility Criteria

We included all trials that randomized adult patients (⩾18 yr old) with probable, suspected, or confirmed severe COVID-19 to corticosteroids versus standard care or placebo or to alternative doses of corticosteroids. We used World Health Organization definitions of disease severity (data supplement). For trials that reported on patients with mixed severity, we extracted subgroup data on patients with severe-to-critical COVID-19, when reported. When subgroup data were not reported, we only included trials with mixed severity if patients predominantly (>80%) had severe-to-critical disease.

### Study Selection

We excluded trials that reported only on patients with nonsevere disease, trials in which mild disease made up ⩾20% of the trial sample and that did not report subgroup data on patients with severe-to-critical disease, and trials that compared interventions that were not corticosteroids with other interventions or standard care or placebo.

Statistically, we treated corticosteroid dosing as a continuous variable and compared arbitrary relatively higher-dose corticosteroids with lower-dose corticosteroids. We did this by choosing doses that have been typically compared in clinical trials (i.e., 12 mg vs. 6 mg). Two reviewers, after training and calibration exercises to ensure sufficient agreement, worked independently and in duplicate to screen titles and abstracts of search records and subsequently the full texts of records determined potentially eligible at the title and abstract screening stage. Reviewers resolved discrepancies by discussion or, when necessary, by third party adjudication.

### Data Collection

Two reviewers, after calibration exercises to ensure sufficient agreement, worked independently and in duplicate to extract data from eligible trials. Reviewers resolved discrepancies by discussion or, when necessary, by third party adjudication. The data supplement presents further detail on data extraction.

### Risks of Bias

Two reviewers, working independently and in duplicate, assessed risk of bias using a revision of the Cochrane tool for assessing risk of bias in randomized trials (RoB 2.0). We resolved discrepancies by discussion and, when necessary, with adjudication by a third party. Table E2 and the data supplement present more detail on the risk of bias tool and methods.

### Statistical Methods

We conducted random-effects dose–response meta-analysis with the restricted maximum likelihood heterogeneity estimator using methods proposed by Orsini and colleagues (7) and Greenland and Longnecker (8). Dose–response meta-analysis estimates the association between doses of an exposure and the relative risk or mean difference of an outcome. We calculated the cumulative intended dose of corticosteroids administered during the trial by multiplying the administered dose by the duration (i.e., dexamethasone 6 mg/d for 10 d = 60 mg total dose) and used this number in the analysis. We then present the data as daily dosing for a 10-day period (reflecting current practice) to improve clinical understanding of the results.

To address whether results are driven by cumulative exposure to corticosteroids or the duration of exposure, for the outcomes of mortality, mechanical ventilation, and infections, we performed prespecified random-effects meta-regression and included the duration of treatment with corticosteroids as a moderator. We anticipated that if duration of exposure rather than cumulative exposure was important, we would see larger effects in the analysis of duration rather than cumulative dose. We used corticosteroid conversions to convert other types of corticosteroids to dexamethasone equivalents (9–11).

We summarized the effects of interventions using relative risk (RR) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) for dichotomous outcomes and mean difference with 95% CI for continuous outcomes. To facilitate interpretation for dichotomous outcomes, we calculated absolute risk differences per 1,000 patients and corresponding 95% CIs (12–14). We estimated the risk of mortality from recent epidemiological reports from Ontario, Canada to be 20%. We estimated the risk of invasive mechanical ventilation to be 11.6% from large cohort studies (15–17). For risk of nosocomial infections, we calculated the median risk in the standard care and placebo arms of the included trials.

For all outcomes, we present the data as continuous outcome per 60 mg of dexamethasone equivalent (then converted to daily dosing/10 d). Importantly, this is the intended dose, not the actual dose administered, as many die before they can complete the course and receive less than the intended dose. Although the outcomes are presented as continuous per increment increase in corticosteroid dose, we present our results comparing 12 mg/d for 10 days of dexamethasone equivalent with 6 mg/d for 10 days of dexamethasone equivalent, representing the range of doses most commonly considered in trials that have head-to-head comparisons (i.e., RECOVERY administered 6 mg/d for 10 d and COVID-STEROIDS-2 administered 12 mg/d for 10 d) (18). We present the results as such to improve clinical utility and understanding but does not restrict the analysis to these two comparisons between any two doses that can be derived by using the linear assumptions from the analysis, within the studied doses (0–160 mg). Importantly, although we established a linear association, it is not valid for doses greater than the studied range. From here onward, when we refer to “higher dose” we are referring to 12 mg/d for 10 days, and “lower dose” refers to 6 mg/d for 10 days.

We also performed two prespecified sensitivity analyses for mortality, restricting the analysis first to patients who were only on supplementary oxygen at baseline and second to patients who were receiving noninvasive and invasive mechanical ventilation at baseline. We performed one post hoc sensitivity analysis, investigating whether there were more frequent infections in trials predominately enrolling patients who required mechanical ventilation at baseline versus those who did not.

We performed all analyses using the dosresmeta and meta packages in R (version 4.03, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) (19, 20). The data and the code for generating the results presented in this manuscript are available on Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/PRUQJ).

As per protocol, we performed a network meta-analysis in addition to our dose–response analysis. We present the methods of this analysis in eProtocol, and more detail on our statistical analyses are presented in the data supplement.

### Certainty of the Evidence

For all outcomes, reviewers, working independently and in duplicate, assessed the certainty (quality) of the evidence using the GRADE approach. We judged the certainty for each outcome as high, moderate, low, or very low, based on considerations of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

To make judgements regarding imprecision, we used a minimally contextualized approach, which considers only whether confidence intervals include a minimally important effect and does not consider the magnitude of plausible effects, captured by confidence intervals (21).

We describe results using guidance from the GRADE Working Group, based on the certainty of evidence and the magnitude of the effect (e.g., corticosteroids reduce mortality [high certainty], corticosteroids probably reduce mortality [moderate certainty], corticosteroids may reduce mortality [low certainty], and the effect of corticosteroids on mortality is very uncertain [very low certainty]) (22). More detail is presented in the data supplement.
