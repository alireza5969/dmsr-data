Yield of Surveillance Colonoscopy in Older Adults with a History of Polyps: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Abstract

Background

The benefit of surveillance colonoscopy in older adults is not well described.

Aims

To quantify the detection of colorectal cancer (CRC) and advanced polyps during surveillance colonoscopy in older adults with a history of colon polyps.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review (MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase) for all published studies through May 2020 in adults age > 70 undergoing surveillance colonoscopy. The main outcome was CRC and advanced polyps detection. We performed meta-analysis to pool results by age (>70 vs. 50–70).

Results

The search identified 6239 studies, of which 569 underwent full-text review and 64 data abstraction, of which 19 were included. The risk of detecting CRC (N = 11) was higher in those >70 compared to 50–70 (risk ratio 1.5 (95% CI 1.1–2.2); risk difference 0.8% (95% CI −0.2%–1.8%)). Similarly, the risk of detecting advanced polyps (N = 8) was higher in those >70 compared to 50–70 (risk ratio 1.3 (95% CI 1.2–1.3), risk difference 2.7% (95% CI 1.3%–4.0%)). Most studies did not stratify results by baseline polyp risk.

Conclusions

The detection of CRC and advanced polyps during surveillance colonoscopy in older individuals was higher than in younger controls; however, the absolute risk increase for both was small. These differences must be weighed against competing medical problems and limited life expectancy in older adults when making decisions about surveillance colonoscopy. More primary data on the risks of CRC and advanced polyps accounting for number of past colonoscopies, prior polyp risk, and duration of time since last polyp are needed.

Introduction

Patients with a history of colorectal polyps are recommended to undergo surveillance colonoscopy because of their increased risk of colorectal cancer (CRC). In older adults (age 70 and above), surveillance is the most common indication for colonoscopy [1]. While the US Multi-Society Task Force on CRC provides recommendations on how frequently to perform surveillance colonoscopy, there is currently little guidance on the upper age at which surveillance can be reasonably stopped [2, 3].

Given the aging population in the USA and the prevalence of colon polyps (i.e., more than half of older adults have been told they have had a colon polyp at some point in the past) [4, 5], the question of when surveillance colonoscopy can be reasonably stopped is an increasingly relevant and important issue. It is well described that the potential harms from colonoscopy, including heart attacks, stroke, aspiration pneumonia, bleeding, infection, perforation of the colon, splenic laceration, and death [6–9], increase with age, with an estimated combined risk of 28 out of 1000 in those 75 and older [10–12]. This further strengthens the imperative to understand the benefit of continued surveillance in older adults. Most of the published literature on the yield of surveillance colonoscopy has focused on patients age 50–75, or those younger than 50, excluding older adults [13, 14]. Quantification of the incidence of detecting clinically meaningful findings during surveillance colonoscopy in older adults could help inform guidance around the appropriateness of stopping surveillance by age. Therefore, the aim of our study was to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis on the incidence of detection of CRC and advanced polyps during surveillance colonoscopy in adults age 70 and older with a history of colon polyps.

Methods

Study Design

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Literature Search

Prior to beginning the study, we registered the study protocol with PROSPERO [15]. We conducted a literature search in MEDLINE (Ovid), Cochrane Library (Wiley), Web of Science (Clarivate Analytics), and Embase (Ovid). We also searched conference proceedings for the prior two years, Google Scholar (first 100 results), and references of book chapters and reviews. We limited our search to English language results and included all results through May 2020. Our search strategy included database specific subject headings and keywords related to the concepts of history of polyps, colonoscopy, and age 65 and older (Supplemental Table 1).

Two authors (S.H. and G.W.) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of potential studies using Rayyan QCRI software [16]. Each reviewer was blinded to the inclusion and exclusion decisions of the other until all potential studies were reviewed by both individuals. Randomized control trials and prospective and retrospective observational studies that contained surveillance colonoscopy data for patients age > 70 with a history of colon polyps were considered. After independent review, the reviewers compared lists of included studies and resolved discrepancies through discussion and consensus. After the title/abstract review process, the reviewers independently reviewed the full-text of potential studies to determine all the studies meeting the inclusion criteria for further analysis. Any discrepancies in review that were unable to be resolved through discussion and consensus went to a third author (A.C.) for resolution. References of all included studies were reviewed to determine if additional studies were missed in the original search. An external subject matter expert reviewed the final list of included studies to evaluate for potentially missing studies.

For studies with data derived from larger polyp prevention trials or multicenter registries, we assessed for the possibility of duplicate data and only included the study with the most robust data meeting inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Study Population

The population of interest was individuals older than 70 years with a history of colon polyps undergoing surveillance colonoscopy. Age was defined as the patient’s age at time of colonoscopy. If more than one colonoscopy per patient was reported, then the age at the first included colonoscopy was used. Patients had variable history of index polyps (e.g., non-advanced adenomas, advanced adenomas, serrated polyps) and lag time between colonoscopies.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies of colonoscopy performed for screening or symptoms were excluded. Studies with patients with incomplete polypectomy, personal history of CRC, inflammatory bowel disease or a genetic CRC syndrome, such as Lynch or familial adenomatous polyposis, were excluded.

Intervention

The intervention of interest was surveillance colonoscopy in patients age > 70.

Comparison

The comparison was surveillance colonoscopy in patients age 50–70.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the detection of CRC during surveillance colonoscopy. A secondary outcome was the detection of advanced polyps, defined as adenomas ≥10 mm in size or containing villous features or high-grade dysplasia, sessile serrated lesions ≥10 mm in size, sessile serrated lesions with any dysplasia, or traditional serrated polyp [17].

Data Extraction

We created a standardized data collection form for extraction of relevant information on study characteristics and outcomes. Two authors (S.H. and G.W.) independently collected and compared data from all included studies. The senior author (A.C.) resolved discrepancies in cases where a consensus was unable to be reached. We attempted to contact authors of studies in which data relevant to our study may have been collected but was not presented in a form usable for our analysis.

Risk of bias of each included study was assessed by two authors independently (S.H. and G.W.) using the Newcastle–Ottawa risk of bias tool developed for observational studies [18].

Analysis

We calculated the risk differences of both CRC and advanced polyps for older adults (over 70) compared to the younger adults (age 50–70) for all our included studies and stratified by baseline polyp risk where possible. For the subset of studies with available information, we calculated the risk differences of both CRC and advanced polyps for adults over 80 compared to the younger control group (age 50–70). We calculated the event rates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), p-values, and sample size and heterogeneity measures between age groups. We assumed random-effects in our analysis. RevMan 5.4 (Cochrane collaborative) was used for all analyses, including assessment for heterogeneity using the I2 statistic and generation of funnel plots to determine publication bias [19]. We also calculated risk ratios (RR) between the older and younger age groups for the CRC and advanced polyp endpoints using Mantel–Haenszel risk ratios with random-effects modeling. For the subset of studies that only included older adults without comparison to a younger group, we calculated the absolute risk of both CRC and advanced polyps.

We also performed sensitivity analyses, including jack knife analysis and exclusion of studies where age cutoffs did not align exactly, to evaluate contributors to heterogeneity and resulting changes in risk estimates.

Results

Study Selection

Our search strategy identified 4035 unique studies. After title and abstract screening, there were 569 studies eligible for full-text review, of which 64 were included for data abstraction. After exclusions (e.g., wrong outcome [20], wrong population [21], incomplete or unusable age stratification [22, 23], pooled screening/surveillance data [24, 25], lack of histology [26], case–control study design [27], presentation of adjusted odds without primary data, and lack of response from author for useable data), 19 studies were included for quantitative analysis (Fig. 1) [1, 28–45]. Of note, Martinez 2009 includes pooled data from 8 additional studies [46–53].

Study Characteristics

In total, there were 141,449 individuals over age 70, of which 33,533 (23.7%) were over age 80, and 232,990 individuals age 50 to 70. Table 1 shows the characteristics for the 19 included studies. Studies were conducted in several continents of the world, including North America (7), Europe (7), Asia (4), and Australia (1), and encompassed data from both prospective and retrospective cohorts, pooled analyses, and data from national and multicenter registries. Two studies (Atkin 2017, Park 2016) were restricted to individuals with elevated polyp risk, defined as ≥3 small adenomas or ≥1 advanced polyp; the remaining studies included a mix of baseline polyp risks or were not reported. Supplemental Table 2 shows how potential duplicate data were handled.

Primary Outcome: Detection of Colorectal Cancer (CRC)

Eleven studies contained data on the risk of detection CRC in individuals aged > 70 compared to individuals aged 50 to 70. The absolute risk of detecting CRC in the older group was 2.1% (1224 events; 57,469 participants) and 1.4% in the younger group (1366 events; 94,536 participants). The pooled risk difference for CRC among individuals >70 compared to 50–70 was 0.83% (95% CI −0.15%–1.8%, I2: 98%, p = 0.10, Table 2). The pooled risk ratio for CRC for individuals greater than 70 to the younger control was 1.5 (95% CI 1.1–2.2, I2: 86%, p = 0.01). Results did not differ meaningfully when evaluating studies with only high-risk baseline polyps (Atkin, Park) (pooled risk difference 1.34% (95% CI −1.70%–4.49%, I2: 95%, p = 0.39) compared to studies with mixed or unknown baseline polyps (pooled risk difference 0.71% (95% CI −0.38%–1.80%, I2: 98%, p = 0.20).

Six studies contained data on the comparison of CRC risk in individuals aged > 80 to younger control (ages 50 to 70). For individuals >80, the absolute risk was 2.3% (352 events; 15,216 participants) compared to 1.4% (1016 events; 70,411 participants) in the younger group. The CRC pooled risk difference was 1.6% (95% CI 0.40%–2.7%, I2: 85%, p = 0.008, Table 3). The CRC pooled risk ratio between these two groups was 2.0 (95% CI 1.4–2.9, I2: 81%, p < 0.001).

Six studies contained data on CRC risk in individuals aged >70 without a younger comparison group. The absolute risk of CRC was 0.6% (Supplemental Table 3).

Secondary Outcome: Detection of Advanced Polyps

Eight studies contained data on the comparison of advanced polyps risk in individuals aged > 70 and ages 50 to 70. The absolute risk in the older group was 10.3% (9112 events; 88,320 participants) compared to the younger group which was 8.2% (12,204 events; 148,829 participants). The advanced polyps pooled risk difference among individuals between these two groups was 2.7% (95% CI 1.3%–4.0%, I2: 78%, p < 0.001, Table 4). The pooled risk ratio for advanced polyps for > 70 compared to age 50–70 was 1.27 (95% CI 1.24–1.30, I2: 0%, p < 0.001). When evaluating studies with only high-risk baseline polyps (Atkin, Park), the pooled risk difference was higher at 5.45%% (95% CI 3.10%–7.79%, I2: 0%, p < 0.001) compared to studies with mixed or unknown baseline polyp risk 2.00% (95% CI 0.64%–3.35%, I2: 78%, p = 0.004).

Five studies contained data on the comparison of advanced polyps risk in individuals aged > 80 and individuals aged 50 to 70. For individuals > 80, the absolute risk of advanced polyps was 12.1% (2251 events; 18,636 participants) compared to 8.2% (12,055 events; 147,230 participants) for younger controls. The advanced polyps pooled risk difference was 5.8% (95% CI 2.6%–8.9%, I2: 41%, p < 0.001, Table 5). The advanced polyps pooled risk ratio between these two groups was 1.65 (95% CI 1.35–2.02, I2: 49%, p < 0.001).

Five studies contained data on advanced polyps in individuals aged > 70 without a younger comparison group. The absolute risk of individuals > 70 was 9.6% (Supplemental Table 4).

Sensitivity Analyses

Jack knife analysis for the outcomes of advanced adenomas for age > 80 showed that removing Symonds decreased heterogeneity (I2 = 41 to 0%; Supplemental Table 5a). We chose to keep Symonds in the main analysis because of the overall low I2. Jack knife analysis for the other outcomes (i.e., advanced adenomas age > 70, CRC age > 70, and CRC age > 80) did not reduce heterogeneity significantly (i.e., none dropped below 50%) (Supplemental Table 5b–d).

When removing studies with misaligned age group cutoffs, for the outcome of advanced adenomas for age > 80, heterogeneity increased (I2 = 41 to 67%) and the point estimate changed from 0.06 (95% CI 0.03–0.09) to 0.04 (95% CI 0.02–0.05) (Supplemental Table 6a). There were no clinically meaningful changes in heterogeneity or point estimates when removing studies with misaligned age groups for the other outcomes (i.e., advanced adenomas age > 70, CRC age > 70, and CRC age > 80) (Supplemental Tables 6b–d).

Quality Assessment and Publication Bias

Overall, the quality of studies ranged from fair to good with only one study rated as poor (Supplemental Table 7). There was no evidence of publication bias (Supplemental Fig. 1a–d).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of the incidence of CRC and advanced polyps in older adults undergoing surveillance for prior history of colon polyps, we found a very small increase in incidence of CRC (0.8% absolute risk difference) and a small increase in advanced polyps (2.7% absolute risk difference) in those aged > 70 compared to those age 50–70. This difference was larger when looking at the subgroup of individuals aged > 80.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis investigating the detection of CRC and advanced polyps during surveillance colonoscopy in older adults. Other literature has evaluated yield of surveillance by gender and race, but age as a primary predictor has not been studied comprehensively. Conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis was methodologically appropriate given the small numbers of older adults included in any single study. Of note, relevant characteristics of the study population within the included studies were often unspecified (e.g., number of prior colonoscopies, history of advanced or non-advanced polyps, duration of time since advanced polyp if applicable). While more primary studies on this topic are needed, the existing data may be all that we can synthesize since larger or more refined data collection on this specific age group are unlikely to be done in the near future.

The increase in CRC detection on surveillance among older adults compared to younger adults was very small. Likely for the majority of older adults, this very small increase in detection of CRC may not readily justify the burdens and risks of colonoscopy that also increase with age [8]. However, for a small percentage of older adults without competing co-morbidities and long life expectancy, detection of CRC on surveillance could lead to earlier treatment and improved outcomes that are life-extending.

The increase in detection of advanced polyps risk among older adults compared to younger adults was higher compared to the difference in CRC detection by age. This makes intuitive sense given how much more common advanced polyps are compared to CRC. Whether the detection of advanced polyps is a meaningful outcome in itself among older adults may be debatable. The presence of advanced polyps themselves does not directly cause harm, and only a small proportion will progress to become invasive cancer, a process estimated to take many years [54]. It is therefore possible that most older adults, especially if they have multiple co-morbidities, frailty, poor function or declining cognition, may not live long enough to realize the benefits of advanced polyp removal, making this increase in advanced polyp risk potentially less clinically meaningful.

To balance the potential benefit of colonoscopy in older adults, it is also important to consider the potential harms. The incidence and severity of harms with colonoscopy increase in older adults compared to younger adults [8, 55–59], with the risk in adults age 80–84 estimated to be 75% higher compared to the risk in those age 66–69 [8]. Competing risk of death from other causes, unrelated to development of CRC, is also important to consider as patients age and develop more significant co-morbidities. An evaluation of the causes of death in the years following screening or surveillance colonoscopy found a high rate (5.3%) of death within 2 years of colonoscopy [60]. In addition to older age (> 80), other associated risk factors for death included substance abuse, depression, malignancy, complicated diabetes, cirrhosis, and lung disease. Of note, that study had limitations inherent to its case control design and was overwhelmingly male (97%).

The strengths of our study include the rigorous and comprehensive search strategy. We were able to include data that from a very large number of patients (>140,000) from countries across several continents, increasing its generalizability. The focus on older adults is relevant and will become increasingly so with the aging US population and large burden of surveillance colonoscopy performed in older adults.

We acknowledge several limitations. The majority of the studies in our meta-analysis include data from patients with unknown prior polyp risk and other studies include a combination of non-advanced and advanced polyps at baseline or use varying definitions of advanced polyps. Furthermore, most of the data on colorectal cancer or advanced polyp outcomes were not stratified by baseline polyp risk. Therefore, we only knew the baseline risk of the polyps (non-advanced vs. advanced) for a minority of patients included in our analysis. Baseline polyps strongly predict an individual’s lifetime risk of CRC, with the risk of CRC in those with only non-advanced polyps estimated to approximate the risk of those without polyps [61, 62]. If the population of older adults included in this current study includes either more or less high-risk patients (i.e., patients with advanced adenoma at recent surveillance colonoscopy) in either the control or older population cohorts, this could skew our results. However, we know that only a minority of older adults have harbored advanced polyps in the past (estimated to be 10%), so it is likely that most older adults in this study have non-advanced polyps (i.e., 1–2 small adenomas) [38].

The included studies also had variable timing of surveillance. We captured the mean/median follow-up times for each study, when reported, but the variation in follow-up time could mean that patients developed outcomes outside of the study timeframe for those with shorter intervals and were therefore missed in our analysis. We were unable to account for the number of prior colonoscopies per individual, which could also affect interpretation of our results. We were also unable to account for other factors in CRC risk, such as family history or other environmental exposures such as smoking or diet, as these were not captured comprehensively or granularly in the included studies. Finally, there may have been variable quality of colonoscopy performance in the studies captured; however, this likely reflects the colonoscopy performance in real world settings.

In summary, in this systematic review and meta-analysis, we found that the risk of CRC and advanced polyps during surveillance colonoscopy among older adults with a prior history of polyps is slightly increased compared to younger controls, but overall remains small. Knowledge of these absolute and relative risks may help refine stopping parameters for surveillance in older adults, particularly those with co-morbidities or limited life expectancy where risks of colonoscopy may outweigh benefits. In addition, this information generated specific to this age group, if provided in an interpretable format that is understandable by people of all levels of educational attainment, may help inform shared decision-making between providers and patients about the appropriateness of ongoing surveillance colonoscopy.
