Effect of azithromycin on bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome in posttransplant recipients: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Background:

Bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) is a devastating complication that occurs after transplantation. Although azithromycin is currently used for the treatment of BOS, the evidence is sparse and controversial. The aim of this meta-analysis is to evaluate the effects of azithromycin on forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and patient’s survival.

Methods:

PubMed, Embase, Cochrane library, Web of Science databases, and the ClinicalTrials.gov registry were systematically searched from inception until December 2020 for relevant original research articles. Random-effects models were used to calculate pooled-effect estimates.

Results:

Searches identified 15 eligible studies involving 694 participants. For FEV1 (L), there was a significant increase after short-term (≤12 weeks; P = .00) and mid-term (12–24 weeks; P = .01) administration of azithromycin. For FEV1 (%) compared to baseline, there was a significant increase after short-term (≤12 weeks) administration of azithromycin (P = .02), while there were no statistically significant differences in the medium and long term. When pooled FEV1% was predicted, it exhibited a similar trend to FEV1 (%) compared to baseline. In addition, we discovered that azithromycin reduced the risk of death (hazard ratio = 0.26; 95% confidence interval = 0.17 to 0.40; P = .00) in patients with BOS post–lung transplantation.

Conclusions:

Azithromycin therapy is both effective and safe for lung function improvement in patients with posttransplant BOS after the short- and medium-term administration. Additionally, it has been demonstrated a significant survival benefit among patients with BOS post–lung transplant. Higher quality randomized controlled trials and more extensive prospective cohort studies are needed to confirm the effect of azithromycin on patients with posttransplant BOS.

1. Introduction

Bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) is a fatal noninfectious pulmonary complication that occurs most frequently in lung transplantation (LTx), hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), and recently reported renal transplantation.[1–3] BOS is characterized by progressive airflow obstruction (persistent decline in forced expiratory volume in 1 second [FEV1]), in the absence of acute rejection, infection, or other coexisting conditions, and obstruction of the physiology of BOS. The clinical symptoms are dyspnea, cough, fatigue, or fever, and the histological hallmark is bronchiolitis obliterans.[4] A study on the incidence and epidemiology of BOS after LTx from the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) showed that only 36.3% of LTx recipients survived and were free from BOS at 5 years posttransplantation, and almost all the survivors developed BOS at 10 years posttransplantation.[5] Also, the incidence of BOS in HSCT was 9.1%, while the mortality rate remained as high as 50%.[6] Overall, BOS has a significant impact on the long-term survival and quality of life of the recipients after transplantation.[7–9]

Potential therapeutic options for posttransplant BOS that have been tried incessantly, including adjustment of immunological strategies,[10,11] montelukast,[12–14] total lymphatic irradiation,[15–18] extracorporeal photopheresis,[16–18] and mesenchymal stem cells.[19,20] However, these strategies have, at best, reduced the rate of graft function decline associated with BOS, and could not arrest its progression. Azithromycin was first reported to halt or even reverse the decline in lung function in patients with BOS in 2003, which has generated enormous benefit for its innovative, well-tolerated, and inexpensive characters.[21] Subsequently, there have been numerous studies on the use of azithromycin in the therapy of posttransplant BOS.[22–36] To our knowledge, 2 previous meta-analyses were conducted on the use of azithromycin for posttransplant BOS, one of which in patients with post-LTx BOS with a mean follow-up of 7 months identified an 8.8% increase in FEV1 with azithromycin therapy (P < .001).[37] And another was in the post-HSCT BOS population, in which a mean increase in FEV1 of only 30 mL was observed (P = .82), and azithromycin for BOS did not obtain conclusive support.[38] And neither article discusses the length of the course of azithromycin for posttransplant BOS. Furthermore, the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation, American Thoracic Society, and European Respiratory Society (ISHLT/ATS/ERS) guidelines shed no clear light on the different courses of azithromycin for posttransplant BOS and its effectiveness.[4]

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of azithromycin treatment for posttransplant BOS patients at different lengths of course and to investigate the benefit of azithromycin on survival.

2. Methods

This systematic review was performed in strict compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis guidelines (see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, which shows Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis checklist, http://links.lww.com/MD/G861)[39] and was also registered in the PROSPERO international register of systematic reviews (CRD42021256019).

2.1. Search strategy

A comprehensive literature screening was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane library, Web of Science databases, and the ClinicalTrials.gov registry with the key terms “azithromycin” and “bronchiolitis obliterans” from inception to November 2020 without language or date restriction. A manual search was also performed of reference lists of included articles, and literature searches were complemented by perusing the reference lists of previous meta-analyses. Details can be found in the appendix (see Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/G861, which illustrates the search strategy).

2.2. Eligibility criteria

2.2.1. Participants.

Adult patients who underwent transplantation developed BOS, and BOS was diagnosed by the definitions of the ISHLT guidelines or the National Institutes of Health Graft Versus Host Disease Consensus guidelines[4,40–43]

2.2.2. Intervention.

Azithromycin or a combination of fluticasone, azithromycin, and montelukast (FAM) supplement of any form or dosage was administered.

2.2.3. Comparison.

Placebo group or no intervention.

2.2.4. Outcomes.

One or more of the following outcomes were assessed. The primary outcomes were changes in pulmonary function parameters before and after azithromycin administration, including the change in the absolute value of FEV1 (L), the percentage change in FEV1 (%) from baseline (at treatment initiation), and the change in predicted FEV1%. The second outcome included survival and adverse drug reactions.

2.2.5. Study design.

Randomized control trials, cohort studies, case series. There is no publication status and language restriction.

2.2.6. Exclusion criteria.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: conference abstract, azithromycin used for prevention, and comparable data could not be extracted.

Altogether, 2 independent reviewers performed literature screening, and each disagreement was discussed and, if required, was arbitrated by a third reviewer.

2.3. Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out independently by 2 authors using standardized, prepiloted data-extraction Microsoft Excel sheets and any discrepancies regarding the extraction of data were solved by consultation of the third reviewer. Where >1 publication of 1 study exists, reports were grouped together and the publication with the complete data was used in the analysis. Where relevant information was only published in earlier versions, these data were used. Extracted information included: first author, year of publication, region/country, study design, participant characteristics (age, sex, transplant type, BOS stage), sample size, treatment option, dosage and duration, primary and secondary outcomes, adverse events.

The primary outcomes are extracted at various time points respectively. According to the duration of use of azithromycin, it can be divided into short term (≤12 weeks), medium term (12–24 weeks), and long term (>24 weeks); when there are multiple time points in a certain period of time, the later time is selected. Where only graphs were provided, we selected the graph extraction tool to extract the required data.[44] Mean and standard deviation were used to represent the change in the primary outcome (FEV1) before and after the treatment of azithromycin. Where the data were not presented with mean and standard deviation, we made corresponding assumptions and converted the data.[45] Hazard ratio (HR) was used to assess survival, and data from multivariate analysis were extracted preferentially, if not, we could also extract the data from the univariate analysis or the data from Kaplan–Meier survival curves.[46]

2.4. Assessment for study quality

The method to access the methodological quality of the studies for systematic review obeyed the standard established by Cochrane Reviews. Two reviewers independently assessed the selected articles for methodological quality; any discrepancies were solved by the discussion with the third reviewer. Risk of bias in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was assessed for the following criteria using the Cochrane Risk of bias Tool.[47] The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the quality of the Cohort studies.[48] Quality Appraisal of Case Series Studies Checklist was used to assess the quality of case series (Institute of Health Economics).[49]

2.5. Statistical analysis

STATA Version 16 software package was used for statistical analysis. For continuous data, the weighted mean difference (WMD) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) are pooled. Changes of the pulmonary function parameter FEV1 with 12% or 200 ml increase from baseline, respectively, were considered clinically significant.[50] For dichotomous data, HR and its corresponding 95% CI are pooled, respectively. Pooled-effect estimates were calculated by a random-effects model, and the heterogeneity between studies was determined by the I2 test.[51] To determine possible clinical heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed that included type of transplantation and administration regimen (azithromycin vs FAM). Sensitivity analysis was performed to investigate the impact of individual studies on the pooled results by sequentially excluding individual studies and to check the robustness of these findings. For any meta-analysis, only a forest plot with ≥10 studies can produce a meaningful funnel plot.[52] Therefore, publication bias was not carried out in this paper. A 2-sided P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Study selection

A total of 1072 potential publications were identified, and after duplication was removed, 769 were available for screening of titles and abstracts. Of these, 41 full-text articles were screened for eligibility, resulting in the identification of 16 published articles.[21–36] An RCT study was excluded from the pooled analysis as it showed an estimated MD in FEV1 between treatments (azithromycin minus placebo), rather than the comparison of FEV1 value before and after azithromycin treatment. Therefore, this paper was used only in formulation of the discussion of this article.[24] Finally, 15 published articles were eligible, and 1 article that reported the effects of long-term use of azithromycin on survival was identified as meeting the criteria, although it was a post hoc analysis of an RCT study.[22] A flow diagram for each stage is shown in Figure 1.

3.3. Synthesis of results

3.3.1. Primary outcomes.

3.3.1.1. FEV1 (L).

For the changes in FEV1 per unit liter before and after azithromycin treatment, 7 studies[26,28,30,32,33,35,36] reported short term (≤12 weeks), 4 studies[21,28,30,36] assessed mid-term (12–24 weeks), and 3 studies[28,30,36] reported long term (>24 weeks), respectively. The results of the pooled analysis showed an increasing trend in FEV1 (L) between pre– and post–azithromycin treatment, specifically at the mid-term, where the pooled MD in FEV1 (L) was increased by 0.22 L (95% CI = 0.05 to 0.39; P = .01; I2 = 41.68%), manifesting considerable clinical significance. However, this improvement seemed not to be sustained (MD = 0.11 L; 95% CI = −0.39 to 0.62; P = .65; I2 = 91.00%), and the pooled MD in the long term tended to decrease compared to the mid-term, as shown in Figure 2.

3.3.1.2. FEV1 (%) compared to baseline (at treatment initiation).

Six studies[26,31–33,35,36] reported short-term (≤12 weeks) data, 3 studies[21,31,36] reported mid-term (12–24 weeks) data, and 2 studies[27,36] reported long-term data for the change in FEV1 (%) from baseline between pre– and post–azithromycin treatment. Pooled analysis showed a significant difference in FEV1 (%) change values between pre- and posttreatment of azithromycin for short term (MD: 10.65; 95% CI = 1.63–19.67; P = .02), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 86.40%). There was no significant difference in FEV1 (%) between mid- and long-term azithromycin treatment and pre–azithromycin treatment. The detailed data are shown in Figure 3.

3.3.1.3. FEV1% predicted.

Five studies[21,23,25,28,34] evaluated predicted FEV1 (%) change between pre– and post–azithromycin short-term (≤12 weeks) treatment, 3 studies[23,28,34] reported mid-term (12–24 weeks) data, and 2 studies[28,34] reported long-term (>24 weeks) data, respectively. Pooled analysis showed a significant increase in the predicted FEV1 (%) (MD: 2.99; 95% CI = 0.70–5.27; P = .01; I2 = 14.77%) between pre– and post–azithromycin short-term treatment. However, FEV1% predicted in the mid-term and long term after azithromycin treatment were not significantly different from those before azithromycin treatment, and the heterogeneity among studies were both high. The detailed data are shown in Figure 4.

3.3.2. Secondary outcomes.

3.3.2.1. Survival.

Four studies involving 436 participants reported death outcomes in the azithromycin group vs the nonazithromycin group (or placebo group) using time-to-event analyses, with a mean follow-up of 4.8 years (ranging from 1 to 9 years).[22,27–29] After pooling the HRs, the risk of death was significantly reduced with azithromycin treatment compared with nonazithromycin (HR = 0.26; 95% CI = 0.17–0.40; P = .00), with heterogeneity I2 = .00%. The result is shown in Figure 5.

Figure 5.

Forest plot for survival for azithromycin vs no azithromycin. CI = confidence interval, FEV1 = forced expiratory volume in 1 s, HR = hazard ratio.

3.3.2.2 . Safety.

Eleven studies provide information concerning the safety and tolerability of azithromycin.[21–26,28,30,31,33,35] Only 4 studies reported adverse side effects associated with azithromycin; 3 studies[26,28,31] of which were mainly gastrointestinal complaints (nausea, dyspepsia or colitis) when azithromycin was used alone, and 1 case was of laryngeal edema. The remaining study that reported adverse effects of the FAM combination included dyspepsia (n = 1), fever (n = 1), pain (n = 1), pulmonary infection (n = 6), cough (n = 1), dyspnea (n = 1), pneumonitis (n = 1), pneumothorax (n = 1), and other respiratory disorders (n = 2).[23]

3.3.2.3. Subgroup analysis.

Subgroup analysis of primary outcomes was carried out according to different transplant types (HSCT and LTx) and regimens (azithromycin and FAM). For outcome of FEV1 (L) and FEV1 (%) compared to baseline, subgroup analysis by transplant type demonstrated that the therapeutic effect of azithromycin was superior to post-HSCT transplant BOS for post-LTx BOS in the short term. As for the outcome of the predicted FEV1 (%) change in the short-term, only 1 study was included in the HSCT subgroup, so we cannot draw any conclusion from this analysis. In the mid-term of the change of the predicted FEV1 (%), the improvement of the subgroup LTx was greater than that of the subgroup HSCT without any statistical significance. For the outcome of predicted FEV1 (%) changes, subgroup analysis of administration regimens showed similar results to subgroup analysis of transplant types. 3.3.2.4. Additional analysis.

We performed sensitivity analyses for all primary outcomes to assess the robustness of our results by excluding each individual study sequentially. Sensitivity analysis of studies reporting long-term changes in both outcomes FEV1 (L) and FEV1 (%) compared to baseline showed that 1 study influenced the results of the statistical analysis.[36] The results of other sensitivity analyses were not materially altered, indicating that most of our analyses were reasonable and reliable. Details can be found in the appendix (see Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/G861, which presents the results of the sensitivity analysis).

4. Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the efficacy and safety of azithromycin for posttransplant BOS patients from the length of azithromycin administration using a meta-analysis. The overall results suggest that the short-term to mid-term (≤24 weeks) azithromycin treatment has a significant improvement in lung function in patients with posttransplant BOS; however, this improvement does not continue over time, that is, there is a downward trend in the long term. Meanwhile, azithromycin also showed some survival benefit and was well tolerated.

In our study, we observed significant improvements in pulmonary function in posttransplant BOS patients after short- and mid-term treatment with azithromycin. Consistent with our study, the superiority of azithromycin compared to placebo treatment in a 12-week RCT was also verified.[24] However, the pulmonary function did not improve continuously with the continued azithromycin treatment but showed a downward trend. One possible explanation is that long-term use of azithromycin can be resistant.[53] On the other hand, in term of long-term use of azithromycin for posttransplant BOS in relation to lung function, there are not enough data used for analysis.

Surprisingly, we discovered that azithromycin reduces the risk of death, which is restricted to patients with post-LTx BOS at present. An RCT on the prevention of decreased airflow after HSCT unexpectedly found an increase in hematologic recurrence in the azithromycin group.[54] And, 1 study exposure to azithromycin in patients with BOS after HSCT was associated with an increased risk of new subsequent neoplasm.[55] Hence, we are not arbitrarily extrapolating this survival benefit to other transplant types, and the long-term use of azithromycin for both prophylaxis and therapy requires cautious deliberation.

Subgroup analyses indicated that the effectiveness of azithromycin in improving pulmonary function was slightly different in patients with BOS after different transplant types. It is possible that the mechanisms of BOS occurring posttransplant differ; in the case of HSCT, it may be a form of pulmonary manifestation of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), while in the case of LTx, there may be more complex mechanisms such as allograft injury, microbiological and environmental factors, etc.[4,56–58] Although the type of transplantation in this article relates only to LTx and HSCT, there are reported effectiveness of azithromycin in improving pulmonary function for both post–liver transplantation BOS and post–renal transplantation BOS.[3,59] Hence, we can consider short- to mid-term azithromycin administration as first-line therapy for patients with posttransplant BOS.

Furthermore, subgroup analysis of the 2 treatment regimens was performed in this study, and in terms of results, the short-term showed that the FAM regimen was superior to azithromycin alone, while the medium-term showed the opposite results. However, unfortunately, the FAM regimen is used only in patients with post-HSCT BOS. Several studies have also positively evaluated the effectiveness of fluticasone[60] alone and montelukast[12–14] alone in patients with BOS post-LTx. However, it is difficult to be conclusive whether the FAM regimen or azithromycin alone is more beneficial, and further in-depth studies are needed in the future.

In addition, the proportion of patients with improved pulmonary function after treatment with azithromycin was 49.7%, as we counted for all included studies in the article. Because of nonuniform definitions of azithromycin responders and nonresponders in various studies, this result may be biased; nevertheless, the effectiveness of azithromycin in improving pulmonary function in some posttransplant BOS patients is indisputable. The precise mechanism of azithromycin use in response to posttransplant BOS is as yet still speculated. The possible mechanisms associated with azithromycin treatment of posttransplant BOS are chiefly anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties.[27,31,32,59,61–64] There are also antibacterial properties of azithromycin that have been referred to, but most studies do not support this mechanism,[28,32,34,36,65] perhaps because the dose of azithromycin used for posttransplant BOS is not sufficient to achieve an antibacterial effect. There are also studies hypothesizing that azithromycin may have another possible mechanism to reduce gastroesophageal reflux and subsequent aspiration,[31,34] but there is no straightforward evidence to support this. Future studies are to be designed taking the authentic mechanism of azithromycin into consideration.

Compared with the 2 previously published meta-analyses,[37,38] we addressed the most appropriate regimen for the treatment of posttransplant BOS with different course lengths of azithromycin. Whereas previous studies only discussed the therapeutic effects of azithromycin, they did not delve into the appropriate duration of administration regimen. we performed the synthetic analysis of FEV1 in 3 different ways of representation separately and discussed the therapeutic effect of azithromycin on posttransplant BOS from a holistic view. Finally, we also investigated the influence of azithromycin on survival, albeit this benefit is currently only available in LTx.

Inevitably, this study has several limitations. First, the majority of articles included in our meta-analysis were observational studies, lacking a control group, and thus, the relationship between the therapeutic effect of azithromycin and the natural course of FEV1 progression after the onset of BOS is not well appreciated. Second, the administration regimen of azithromycin was not completely uniform in different studies and may introduce some heterogeneity. Third, the heterogeneity of individuals, the disease themselves, and the measurement methods also influenced our results to some extent. Finally, the limited availability of studies restricted our further analysis. Further studies of high quality and large sample are warranted to identify and refine our findings.

5. Conclusion

In summary, our meta-analysis demonstrated that short- to mid-term (≤24 weeks) azithromycin is a reasonably effective and safe therapeutic option for improving pulmonary function in posttransplant BOS patients. Azithromycin may improve survival in patients with BOS after LTx. Notwithstanding the rigor of our methodology, the inherent limitations of the included studies affect the confidence of our conclusions. Future well-designed, extensive follow-up and multicenter RCTs await to validate and update the results of this analysis.

## 1. Introduction

Bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) is a fatal noninfectious pulmonary complication that occurs most frequently in lung transplantation (LTx), hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), and recently reported renal transplantation.[1–3] BOS is characterized by progressive airflow obstruction (persistent decline in forced expiratory volume in 1 second [FEV1]), in the absence of acute rejection, infection, or other coexisting conditions, and obstruction of the physiology of BOS. The clinical symptoms are dyspnea, cough, fatigue, or fever, and the histological hallmark is bronchiolitis obliterans.[4] A study on the incidence and epidemiology of BOS after LTx from the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) showed that only 36.3% of LTx recipients survived and were free from BOS at 5 years posttransplantation, and almost all the survivors developed BOS at 10 years posttransplantation.[5] Also, the incidence of BOS in HSCT was 9.1%, while the mortality rate remained as high as 50%.[6] Overall, BOS has a significant impact on the long-term survival and quality of life of the recipients after transplantation.[7–9]

Potential therapeutic options for posttransplant BOS that have been tried incessantly, including adjustment of immunological strategies,[10,11] montelukast,[12–14] total lymphatic irradiation,[15–18] extracorporeal photopheresis,[16–18] and mesenchymal stem cells.[19,20] However, these strategies have, at best, reduced the rate of graft function decline associated with BOS, and could not arrest its progression. Azithromycin was first reported to halt or even reverse the decline in lung function in patients with BOS in 2003, which has generated enormous benefit for its innovative, well-tolerated, and inexpensive characters.[21] Subsequently, there have been numerous studies on the use of azithromycin in the therapy of posttransplant BOS.[22–36] To our knowledge, 2 previous meta-analyses were conducted on the use of azithromycin for posttransplant BOS, one of which in patients with post-LTx BOS with a mean follow-up of 7 months identified an 8.8% increase in FEV1 with azithromycin therapy (P < .001).[37] And another was in the post-HSCT BOS population, in which a mean increase in FEV1 of only 30 mL was observed (P = .82), and azithromycin for BOS did not obtain conclusive support.[38] And neither article discusses the length of the course of azithromycin for posttransplant BOS. Furthermore, the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation, American Thoracic Society, and European Respiratory Society (ISHLT/ATS/ERS) guidelines shed no clear light on the different courses of azithromycin for posttransplant BOS and its effectiveness.[4]

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of azithromycin treatment for posttransplant BOS patients at different lengths of course and to investigate the benefit of azithromycin on survival.
## 2. Methods

This systematic review was performed in strict compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis guidelines (see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, which shows Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis checklist, http://links.lww.com/MD/G861)[39] and was also registered in the PROSPERO international register of systematic reviews (CRD42021256019).

### 2.1. Search strategy

A comprehensive literature screening was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane library, Web of Science databases, and the ClinicalTrials.gov registry with the key terms “azithromycin” and “bronchiolitis obliterans” from inception to November 2020 without language or date restriction. A manual search was also performed of reference lists of included articles, and literature searches were complemented by perusing the reference lists of previous meta-analyses. Details can be found in the appendix (see Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/G861, which illustrates the search strategy).

### 2.2. Eligibility criteria

#### 2.2.1. Participants.

Adult patients who underwent transplantation developed BOS, and BOS was diagnosed by the definitions of the ISHLT guidelines or the National Institutes of Health Graft Versus Host Disease Consensus guidelines[4,40–43]

#### 2.2.2. Intervention.

Azithromycin or a combination of fluticasone, azithromycin, and montelukast (FAM) supplement of any form or dosage was administered.

#### 2.2.3. Comparison.

Placebo group or no intervention.

#### 2.2.4. Outcomes.

One or more of the following outcomes were assessed. The primary outcomes were changes in pulmonary function parameters before and after azithromycin administration, including the change in the absolute value of FEV1 (L), the percentage change in FEV1 (%) from baseline (at treatment initiation), and the change in predicted FEV1%. The second outcome included survival and adverse drug reactions.

#### 2.2.5. Study design.

Randomized control trials, cohort studies, case series. There is no publication status and language restriction.

#### 2.2.6. Exclusion criteria.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: conference abstract, azithromycin used for prevention, and comparable data could not be extracted.

Altogether, 2 independent reviewers performed literature screening, and each disagreement was discussed and, if required, was arbitrated by a third reviewer.

### 2.3. Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out independently by 2 authors using standardized, prepiloted data-extraction Microsoft Excel sheets and any discrepancies regarding the extraction of data were solved by consultation of the third reviewer. Where >1 publication of 1 study exists, reports were grouped together and the publication with the complete data was used in the analysis. Where relevant information was only published in earlier versions, these data were used. Extracted information included: first author, year of publication, region/country, study design, participant characteristics (age, sex, transplant type, BOS stage), sample size, treatment option, dosage and duration, primary and secondary outcomes, adverse events.

The primary outcomes are extracted at various time points respectively. According to the duration of use of azithromycin, it can be divided into short term (≤12 weeks), medium term (12–24 weeks), and long term (>24 weeks); when there are multiple time points in a certain period of time, the later time is selected. Where only graphs were provided, we selected the graph extraction tool to extract the required data.[44] Mean and standard deviation were used to represent the change in the primary outcome (FEV1) before and after the treatment of azithromycin. Where the data were not presented with mean and standard deviation, we made corresponding assumptions and converted the data.[45] Hazard ratio (HR) was used to assess survival, and data from multivariate analysis were extracted preferentially, if not, we could also extract the data from the univariate analysis or the data from Kaplan–Meier survival curves.[46]

### 2.4. Assessment for study quality

The method to access the methodological quality of the studies for systematic review obeyed the standard established by Cochrane Reviews. Two reviewers independently assessed the selected articles for methodological quality; any discrepancies were solved by the discussion with the third reviewer. Risk of bias in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was assessed for the following criteria using the Cochrane Risk of bias Tool.[47] The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the quality of the Cohort studies.[48] Quality Appraisal of Case Series Studies Checklist was used to assess the quality of case series (Institute of Health Economics).[49]

### 2.5. Statistical analysis

STATA Version 16 software package was used for statistical analysis. For continuous data, the weighted mean difference (WMD) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) are pooled. Changes of the pulmonary function parameter FEV1 with 12% or 200 ml increase from baseline, respectively, were considered clinically significant.[50] For dichotomous data, HR and its corresponding 95% CI are pooled, respectively. Pooled-effect estimates were calculated by a random-effects model, and the heterogeneity between studies was determined by the I2 test.[51] To determine possible clinical heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed that included type of transplantation and administration regimen (azithromycin vs FAM). Sensitivity analysis was performed to investigate the impact of individual studies on the pooled results by sequentially excluding individual studies and to check the robustness of these findings. For any meta-analysis, only a forest plot with ≥10 studies can produce a meaningful funnel plot.[52] Therefore, publication bias was not carried out in this paper. A 2-sided P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.
