Abstract

Background:

During adolescence, suicide risk increases; effective treatments are needed to reduce risk.

Methods:

Databases were searched (1995–2020) for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) concerning psychosocial treatments for suicide prevention in adolescents (10–18 yrs). Data were extracted from the timepoint closest to 6 months. Cohen’s ds were estimated for reducing suicidal ideation (SI), self-harming behaviors (SHB) excluding strictly non-suicidal self-injury, and suicide attempts (SA) and analyzed using generalized least square regression. Meta-analytic innovations included within-person correlations to reflect trait suicidality; annualization to control for exposure; estimated lifetime risk based on ages; and modeling inclusion/exclusion criteria. Alternate approaches included relative risk and comparison of intervention and control treatments to baseline.

Results:

Of 30 RCTs, 6 assessing SHB (4 measuring SA), and 7 assessing SI demonstrated treatment effectiveness. Overall, interventions decreased SI (n = 25) with low effect size (d = 0.08, p = 0.01), non-significant after controlling for publication bias (d = 0.05, p = 0.1); interventions were non-significant for SHB (n = 25, d = 0.001, p = 0.97) or SA (n = 18, d = 0.03, p = 0.52). To prevent one SHB, the number needed to treat (NNT) was 45[26,156]; for SA, NNT=42[24,149]. Non-superiority may relate to effectiveness of control treatments. Thus, experimental and control treatments also were compared to baseline: both reduced SI (p < 0.0001), and effectiveness improved for SHB (NNT=12) and SA (NNT=11).

Limitations:

Study heterogeneity and inconsistent statistical reporting limited meta-analysis.

Conclusions:

Psychosocial interventions for suicide risk in adolescents showed little effectiveness compared with control treatments; suicide outcomes improved in both groups compared to baseline. Different approaches may be needed, including precision medicine methodologies and standardized statistical reporting criteria.

Introduction

1.

Suicide is the second leading cause of death in adolescents in the US, with 2144 known suicide deaths of 10 to 18-year-olds in 2019, also reflecting an increase in rate over previous years (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020). Suicidal ideation (SI) and suicide attempts (SA) in adolescents are prevalent, estimated to be 8–14% and 4–7% (respectively) in the general adolescent population (Cash and Bridge, 2009; Georgiades et al., 2019). With regard to non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), prevalence estimates for adolescents range widely from 7.5% to 46.5% (Cipriano et al., 2017).

In recent decades, there has been increasing interest in developing interventions for suicide prevention specifically for adolescents (Fox et al., 2020). As SI, self-harming behaviors (SHB), and NSSI all have predictive value for future suicide attempts in adolescents and young adults (Hawton et al., 1993), most suicide prevention interventions in adolescents primarily target the reduction of one or more of these outcomes. The study of psychosocial interventions gained additional impetus after 2004, when the FDA and other international regulatory agencies mandated a black box warning that pharmacological treatments may have the potential to increase suicide risk in children and adolescents.

Thus, a push to lower suicide risk in adolescents using psychosocial treatments has resulted in a wide range of interventions, with unclear effectiveness. Several systematic reviews of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of psychosocial interventions in adolescents targeting suicide risk prevention have been published in recent years (Calear et al., 2016; Glenn et al., 2019). To the best of our knowledge, however, only three meta-analyses on this topic have been conducted solely among children and adolescents. Ougrin et al. (2015) examined 19 RCTs and found reduction of SHB, with a trend for superiority of the interventions in reducing NSSI but a non-significant effect on reduction of SA. Hawton et al. (2015), examined 11 RCTs conducted in children and adolescents with a history of SHB and found no or modest effect of these interventions on the reduction of SHB and SI. Kothgassner et al. (2020) analyzed 25 RCTs studying adolescents of whom > 50% had a history of SHB, reporting a small positive effect size overall. In subgroup analyses of treatment approaches, they found moderate effects of Dialectical Behavioral Therapy for Adolescents (DBT-A) in reducing SHB and SI, and of family-focused interventions in reducing SI. Two additional meta-analyses including studies of both adults and adolescents found a significant effect of interventions on SHB in adults but not in adolescents, for cognitive-behavioral psychotherapy (Tarrier et al., 2008) and for Mentalization-Based Treatment (Calati and Courtet, 2016) (n = 7 studies of adolescents in each).

Given this lack of consensus on the effectiveness of psychosocial interventions in adolescents, we undertook to examine a larger sample of 29 suicide prevention studies that considered SHB and/or SI as outcomes, using a rigorous meta-analytic approach. We sought to determine the overall effectiveness of psychosocial interventions, tested in RCTs in the last 26 years, in reducing SI, SHB, and SA among 10- to 18-year-old adolescents. Our objective was to provide evidence-based information regarding the effectiveness of such psychosocial interventions for suicide reduction, and to consider future directions for research in this field.

Methods

2.

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) (Moher et al., 2009) when conducting the current meta-analysis (see Supplemental Table S1).

Outcome measures

2.1.

We included clinical trials with outcome measures corresponding to SHB and SI. SHB here includes (1) suicide attempts, defined as self-harm with suicidal intent irrespective of lethality, as well as (2) deliberate self-harm with unknown intent. We excluded studies restricted to NSSI alone, since, although NSSI is highly linked with, and may predict SA, it is a separate phenomenon. However, as defined here, SHB may include both SA and NSSI; thus some of the events counted in the behavioral outcome in the included studies may have been NSSI. Although SA is one of the most powerful predictors of subsequent suicidal behavior, attempts are more infrequent than the broader category of SHB and low base rates require very large sample sizes that most studies do not have, especially in the short follow-up periods feasible within these types of studies. Moreover, the distinction between SA and NSSI can be challenging, especially when it comes to younger adolescents. Therefore, our primary analyses included all SHB together as an outcome. We also performed additional, separate analyses on the subset of data from studies that reported SA as outcomes.

Selection criteria

2.2.

The following inclusion criteria applied: (1) English language; (2) published between January 1995 and December 2020; (3) peer-reviewed journal; 4) RCT focused on suicide prevention intervention in adolescents; (5) a structured, non-pharmacologic intervention; (6) an appropriate control comparator (such as treatment as usual); (7) at least one primary study outcome was SI and/or SHB; and 8) a pediatric population within the range 10 to 18 years (designated as adolescents according to the World Health Organization definition).

Exclusions were: (1) gatekeeper training studies; (2) studies in which the outcome was restricted to NSSI; (3) insufficiently reported data for meta-analysis; (4) studies where suicide-related outcomes were solely secondary analyses; and (5) cohorts restricted to a specific diagnosis, e. g., bipolar disorder, substance use disorders, or borderline personality disorder, to increase generalizability.

Literature search

2.3.

A systematic search of articles published in English from January 1995 to December 2020 was performed using five different search engines: PubMed, Scopus, Embase, PsychINFO, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). See Supplemental Table S2 for a full description of search terms. Record compilation and management was handled using EndNote (Version X9; Thomson Reuters). Additional articles were located through manual bibliography searching. Articles were reviewed to evaluate their fit to the a priori defined inclusion criteria. Literature searching was performed by members of the study team (KS, SD, LI, SCT, MES).

Assessment of bias risk

2.4.

The risk of bias of each included RCT was evaluated by using the NIH Quality assessment of Controlled Intervention Studies tool (National Heart Lung and Blood Institute (NHLBI, 2014). Risk assessments of all the studies were performed independently by two authors (KH and LI), following which all grading was reviewed, any discrepancies were discussed, and a consensus score was given, with a third author (MES) available in case of disagreement. Interrater reliability was estimated using a kappa statistic with a standard error computed by parametric bootstrap with 15,000 resamples. The 14 consensed scores were grouped into six types of risk of bias (Higgins et al., 2017) for presentation purposes: selection bias (items 1,2,3,6: study described as randomized, adequate randomization method, treatment allocation concealed, groups similar at baseline); detection bias (items 4,5,11: participants and providers blind to assignment, blind outcome assessors, reliable and valid outcome measures); reporting bias (item 13: outcomes reported or subgroups analyzed prespecified); attrition bias (items 7,8,14: high adherence, avoidance of other interventions); performance bias (items 9, 10: adherence to protocols, similar background treatments) and other bias (item 12: sufficient statistical power).

Data extraction

2.5.

Data were extracted by three study team members (LI, SE, MES). The following information was obtained for purposes of the meta-analytic procedures: (1) general population of adolescents vs. an at-risk population defined as patients by attendance at a clinical setting or adolescents at risk identified in a study through a screening procedure, (2) sample sizes for each treatment group at baseline, (3) age range, mean ages and standard deviation within each treatment group, (4) relevant assessment instruments and outcomes used, (5) assessment timepoints, (6) experimental and comparison intervention types, (7) inclusion/exclusion criteria with respect to suicide risk at baseline, (8) quantification of previous SHB at baseline and the length of the time period over which prior SHB was counted, (9) mean number of SHB events and standard deviation or proportion of subjects who exhibited SHB at baseline and at follow-up, (10) nature of the SHB, i.e. SA vs undifferentiated SHB, (11) means and standard deviations of SI levels or proportion of subjects who exhibited high levels of SI at baseline and follow-up, and (12) instruments used to assess SI. Additional information was extracted for purposes of understanding bias, characterizing the samples, and non-quantitative assessment of trial quality, including (13) all remaining inclusion/exclusion criteria, (14) moderators and mediators evaluated in the studies, (15) number of treatment sessions, (16) location of trial, (17) co-occurring medication use, and (18) adherence and attrition rates. Where outcome data reported were of inadequate quality for inclusion, we contacted the authors to request additional details. Values not included explicitly in several published reports (Aseltine et al., 2007; Diamond et al., 2019; Hazell et al., 2009; Huey et al., 2004; King et al., 2009) were provided by the authors as personal communications.

Statistical analysis

2.6.

Statistical analyses were performed in R, version 3.6.1 (Core Team, 2019).

Overview of method

2.6.1.

Our main analyses involved estimating Cohen’s d for every study separately for SI and SHB, as available, and then modeling those Cohen’s d’s using a generalized least squares (GLS) regression model. This was a fixed effect model which assumed that all studies were estimating the same effect size and applied weights to each study via a variance function based on standard error estimates from each study. The variance function model quantified heterogeneity among studies. For SHB, in particular SA, we also computed relative risk and applied the same GLS analysis to it. A supplementary document giving greater detail of the statistical methods is available (Ellis, 2021).

Estimated Cohen’s d.

2.6.1.1.

We applied the framework of the Neyman–Rubin causal model (N-R model) (Splawa-Neyman et al., 1990; Rubin, 2006), which postulates what would happen if each subject could be in both the experimental intervention and control groups, a counterfactual situation that would produce the most perfect comparison of experimental intervention and control conditions. Even though this is actually impossible, by using a statistical model, we were able to estimate Cohen’s d comparing the same subject under both conditions. Under this assumption, baseline SHB or SI would be identical between experimental intervention and control groups, and so we would compare the two subjects by the follow-up difference only. For a given subject, let ΔSI be the difference in SI in the treatment condition minus that in the control condition (in follow-up). SHB is handled similarly. For a given subject, let X = 1 or 0 according to whether they exhibited SHB in the treatment condition or not. Let Y be the same, but for the control condition. Let ΔSHB = X – Y. The estimated Cohen’s d is the average value of ΔSI (or ΔSHB) divided by the SD of ΔSI (or ΔSHB).

Pre- and post-treatment correlations.

2.6.1.2.

Operationally, in the denominator of Cohen’s d we used an estimate of the SD that took into account the fact that if the same subject took part in both arms, the outcome measures would be correlated. Estimating that correlation began by hypothesizing a value for the correlation between a control subject’s baseline and follow-up outcomes. Subjects vary in their proclivity for SHB and SI, but it seems implausible that the correlation would be 0; we expected them to have a trait-like propensity toward SI and/or SHB that may give rise to a positive correlation. To capture this, we chose a plausible correlation strength of ρ = 0.3 (Garrison et al., 1991), which was consistent with the only reviewed study in which the actual correlation was reported, for ideation, at ρ = 0.34 (Diamond et al., 2010). To examine the sensitivity of the analyses to the correlation, we repeated our analysis with alternative assumptions of ρ = 0.1 and ρ = 0.5. We used the combined experimental intervention and control groups to compute baseline statistics.

SI modeling.

2.6.1.3.

We developed a family of probabalistic models that specified the distribution of individual SI at baseline and follow-up for both groups. One can think of each model as describing an idealized population at the level of individual adolescents. We computed what would be expected if such individuals were aggregated in a sample. To incorporate the pre-post correlation in our statistical model, we included for each subject a within-subject latent variable that measures the subject’s tendency toward SI. This latent variable is independent across subjects. Specifically, we modeled SI in an individual subject by independent log normal variables for baseline and follow-up, modulated by a common latent log normal variable that gives rise to the correlation. There are seven unknown parameters in the model: log baseline mean and SD, log follow-up control group mean and SD, log follow-up experimental intervention group mean and SD, and the SD of the log latent variable (we take the mean of the log latent variable to be 0). To estimate these numbers, we used the reported SI sample means and SD’s. Combining the experimental intervention and control groups at baseline, those provided six pieces of information. We needed one more: the posited hypothetical correlation: ρ = 0.1, 0.3, or 0.5. We used the method of moments (Bickel and Doksum, 1977) to do the estimation. Thus, while ΔSI only involves follow-up, we used baseline meta-data in fitting the model.

Although the studies we considered did not all use the same instruments, we assumed that the scales differ primarily in mean and SD. Since mean and SD cancel out in the Cohen’s d formula, the Cohen’s d’s for SI should be approximately comparable.

SI typically was assessed over a short, fixed period prior to an assessment timepoint, e.g., the preceding two weeks. Most studies reported SI as a quantitative variable; however, in four papers (Aseltine et al., 2007; Cottrell et al., 2018; Schilling et al., 2016; Wasserman et al., 2015), only the proportion of subjects with SI was reported. Those data therefore were dichotomized and subject to meta-analysis using the same method we used for SHB, below.

SHB modeling.

2.6.1.4.

In contrast to SI, follow-up SHB was usually evaluated over the entire period from baseline to specified timepoints that varied in length from study to study. For each study, we used data from the time point closest to 6 months (see Section 2.6.2, Study Duration, below). The length of the baseline period over which SHB was determined also differed between studies, and sometimes differed from the follow-up period in the same study. These numbers cannot be compared across studies or between baseline and follow-up. Thus, unlike in the case of SI, the Cohen’s d transformation on its own would not suffice for the SHB meta-data; nor would a simple relative risk. Instead, to make the SHB numbers comparable across studies, we also needed to consider the lengths of the periods to which they referred. Our model used the SHB data, including the period lengths, to compute statistics applying to a hypothetical uniform one-year period, as follows.

Proportion can be regarded as an estimate of probability, so using a statistical model described below, we estimated for each study and condition (baseline, control follow-up, treatment follow-up) the probability that a subject in the population from which the study’s subjects were drawn would exhibit SHB over a hypothetical one-year period. We call these probabilities “annualized probabilities”. As an alternative to Cohen’s d we also considered relative risk, defined as

annualized probability of SHB in treatment conditionannualized probability of SHB in control condition

So relative risk =1 means no effect and low relative risk means treatment is better than control. Defined this way, relative risk does not reflect the Neyman-Rubin standpoint. (We did not use the Neyman-Rubin approach here because we found it numerically unstable for relative risk.)

The statistical model underlying the calculation of these probabilities is as follows. For each subject, we hypothesized that the occurrence of SHB in each of the three conditions (baseline and the two follow-up conditions) followed stationary Poisson processes whose rates were modulated by a shared latent gamma random variable, capturing that subject’s trait-like propensity for SHB. (A Poisson process with a random rate is a “Cox process” (Kallenberg, 1976).) Conditional on the gamma variable, the within-subject processes were independent.

The Cox model involves four numbers: the Poisson rates at baseline and, in the two groups, follow-up, plus one parameter for the gamma latent variable (we may constrain the mean of the gamma variable to be 1, so just one extra parameter is needed). The four pieces of information we use to fit this model are: the proportion of subjects at baseline who exhibited SHB in the relevant interval, the analogous values for the two groups during follow-up, plus the hypothesized correlation value, ρ = 0.1, 0.3, or 0.5.

A complication we observed mainly regarding SHB was that many studies mandated in their inclusion and exclusion criteria some specific pre-existing pattern of SHB at baseline. For those meta-data, the simple Cox model was inadequate. Where the inclusion/exclusion criteria with respect to illness severity included specific SHB criteria, we used the Cox model conditioned on these criteria (Cottrell et al., 2018; Donaldson et al., 2005; Green et al., 2011; Hazell et al., 2009; King et al., 2018; McCauley et al., 2018; Mehlum et al., 2014; Ougrin et al., 2013; Rossouw and Fonagy, 2012; Wasserman et al., 2015; Wood et al., 2001). An analogous approach was needed for SI in Diamond et al., 2010, 2019. In other cases, we took the simple Cox model as being approximately correct. See (Ellis, 2021) for details.

An aspect of the SHB data that was not present for SI was that several studies counted, at baseline, presence of lifetime SHB. We recognize that lifetime risk of suicide does not begin at birth, and so for purposes of this analysis we defined the first age of risk for SHB to be 10 years of age, since suicide risk is much lower for pre-adolescents (Sheftall et al., 2016). Age at enrollment varied across subjects, and all else being equal a priori, it is more likely that a 17-year-old would have engaged in SHB than a 14-year-old. Using the study age limits and baseline age mean and SD, we accounted for this extra layer of variability. The specifics of the manipulations involving both SI and SHB are described in (Ellis, 2021).

The baseline data reported in some studies (Aseltine et al., 2007; Hazell et al., 2009; King et al., 2018; Ougrin et al., 2013) were not adequate for inferring SHB prevalence in the population from which the study sample was drawn. Therefore, in one case (Aseltine et al., 2007), we set the baseline attempt rate for both groups at the follow-up rate for the controls, who were untreated in a waiting list condition. For two papers (Hazell et al., 2009; Ougrin et al., 2013), the baseline rate was derived from the prevalence of SHB in adolescents given in Muehlenkamp et al. (2012). In another study (King et al., 2018) with SA as outcome measure, we imputed the proportion of subjects who made SA at baseline from the prevalence of SA in adolescents reported in Nock et al. (2013).

Study duration

2.6.2.

Each study had follow-up period(s) of pre-specified length. If there were multiple follow-up assessments, we extracted data from the timepoint closest to six months, the modal timepoint, included in 13 (Asarnow et al., 2017; Cottrell et al., 2018; Diamond et al., 2010; Donaldson et al., 2005; Green et al., 2011; Grupp-Phelan et al., 2019; Harrington et al., 1998; Hazell et al., 2009; Kennard et al., 2018; King et al., 2018, 2006; McCauley et al., 2018; Rossouw and Fonagy, 2012) out of 29 articles.

Meta-analytic regression modeling

2.6.3.

For the main analyses, we used the GLS function in the R package “nlme” (Pinheiro and Bates, 2000), with a fixed effects model consisting of only the intercept, to compute overall effect sizes for SI and for SHB.

For both SI and SHB, we estimated the SE of each study’s Cohen’s d individually by parametric bootstraps with 1000 resamples. But considering the Cohen’s d’s as a sample from the Cohen’s d’s in a hypothetical sample of studies, these SE estimates may be incorrect if there is heterogeneity among the studies. We chose the “varConstPower” option in GLS, a constant plus power variance function structure, to model the study-wise variance using the formula σ2(c + SEr), where “SE” is the bootstrap estimate of the Cohen’s d and “σ”, “c” and “r” are estimated from all the Cohen’s d’s. This formula allows considerable flexibility in modeling the variances, including capturing heterogeneity and heteroscedasticity. SHB relative risk was analyzed similarly.

Additional measures of suicide risk reduction

2.6.4.

We explored the relationship between baseline and follow-up SI in non-dichotomized studies, i.e. those that reported SI as a quantitative variable (n = 17) by fitting a regression model in which the outcome measure was follow-up mean scores, and covariates were baseline means, experimental intervention or control group follow-up scores, and the interaction between baseline and group. As before, we fit the model with GLS with “varConstPower” to model variance, but now with sample size, N, in place of SE: σ2(c + Np). Using the GLS output, the null hypothesis that treatment z-score ≥ baseline was tested for four cases: experimental intervention ≥ minimum baseline; experimental intervention ≥ maximum baseline; control intervention ≥ minimum baseline; and control intervention ≥ maximum baseline. Significance for the composite null hypothesis then was calculated by adding all p-values for a composite null. Effects of high SI at baseline were explored by calculating the p-value only for values above the median baseline value.

However, studies did not all use the same instrument to assess SI. We calculated z-scores based on baseline values. To carry out this z-transformation on an instrument’s data, we needed meta-data from at least two studies using the same instrument. The instruments used in more than one study were the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI) (Beck et al., 1979), the Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire (SIQ) (Reynolds, 1988), and the Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire JR (SIQ-JR) (Reynolds and Mazza, 1999).

For behavioral outcomes, we estimated the Number Needed to Treat (NNT) to prevent one subject from performing a SHB (over a hypothetical one-year follow-up period), in two ways. We estimated the probability that someone in the experimental intervention group would make an SHB a) compared to a member of the control group or b) compared to an untreated subject, computed using baseline data from all subjects regardless of the group to which they were assigned.

Assessment of publication bias

2.6.5.

Egger’s graphical test was performed as a quantitative measure of publication bias (Egger et al., 1997). For studies in which Egger’s test results were positive, publication bias additionally was assessed visually based on funnel plot asymmetry, and values were imputed using the Duval and Tweedie Trim-and-Fill Method (Duval and Tweedie, 2000).

Heterogeneity

2.6.6.

Heterogeneity of the studies can be quantified via the assumption that they form a simple random sample from a population of studies on suicide prevention in adolescents. This gives a framework for analyzing the corresponding Cohen’s d’s. The SD of the distribution of Cohen’s d’s in the meta-data contains information about heterogeneity and heteroscedasticity but also reflects the SE’s of the individual Cohen’s d’s. The between-study variance, defined as V = SD2, and the within-study variance v = SE2, are used for describing heterogeneity. If there were no heterogeneity, then the estimate of the variability of the studies would just be the individual v’s. The degree of heterogeneity can be expressed in terms of the relationship between the V’s and v’s. The I2 measure (Higgins and Thompson, 2002), which describes the percentage of total variation attributable to heterogeneity, not chance, is frequently used due to a number of advantages including non-dependence on the number of studies and utility for multiple types of outcomes (Higgins et al., 2003). We calculated I2 along with a bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence interval based on 15,000 resamples (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993). However, a complication is that the degree of heterogeneity might be different for studies with different v, and I2 ignores the variation in the variance v across studies. Therefore, we also employed the “varConstPower” model for study-wise variance (described in Section 2.6.3 above) to capture a more nuanced picture of heterogeneity and heteroscedasticity.

Results

3.

Literature search

3.1.

Our search yielded 29 articles that met all criteria. One publication (Eggert et al., 2002) tested two interventions that met criteria, considered as separate trials for purposes of these analyses. Thus, throughout the remainder of this paper, we report on meta-analysis of 29 articles but 30 RCTs. Another article (Wasserman et al., 2015) tested three different interventions, but only one (Youth Aware of Mental Health [YAM]) met our criteria for inclusion. The PRISMA flow diagram (Moher et al., 2009) is shown in Fig. 1.

Studies were conducted at various locations around the world, encompassing 17 studies in the US (Asarnow et al., 2017; Aseltine et al., 2007; Diamond et al., 2019, 2010; Donaldson et al., 2005; Eggert et al., 2002; Grupp-Phelan et al., 2019; Hill and Pettit, 2019; Huey et al., 2004; Kennard et al., 2018; King et al., 2018, 2015, 2009; King et al., 2006; McCauley et al., 2018; Rengasamy and Sparks, 2019; Schilling et al., 2016), 6 in the UK (Cottrell et al., 2018; Green et al., 2011; Harrington et al., 1998; Ougrin et al., 2013; Rossouw and Fonagy, 2012; Wood et al., 2001), 1 in 10 EU countries (Austria, Estonia, France, Germany, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Romania, Slovenia, and Spain) (Wasserman et al., 2015), 1 in Norway (Mehlum et al., 2014), 1 in Taiwan (Tang et al., 2009), 1 in China (Lu et al., 2019), and 2 in Australia (Hazell et al., 2009) (Hetrick et al., 2017). Details of each study are summarized in Table 1.

Assessment of clinical trial methodology

3.2.

Detailed risk of bias estimates for each of the 29 articles included in the meta-analysis are illustrated in Fig. 2. Most studies had a low risk of selection bias (73%) and detection bias (62%). About half of the studies (52%) had a low risk of reporting bias; however, as this was based on only one item that was evaluated using online documentation of the protocol (i.e., ClinicalTrials.gov or published grant details), it could not be evaluated for the remaining studies (48%). Risk of attrition bias was relatively high, with 45% of the studies being graded as high risk, 41% as low risk and 14% as unclear. Risk of performance bias was unclear for most studies (69%), high for 17% and low for 14%. Risk of bias due to sample size was low for 38% of the studies, high for 38%, and unclear for 24%. Interrater reliability of bias assessment grading was at a moderate level (McHugh, 2012) (κ=0.675, SE=0.032).

Data synthesis

3.3.

We identified 25 RCTs with SI as an outcome, involving 15,016 subjects (Fig. 3A), and 25 RCTs with SHB as an outcome, involving 14,988 subjects (Fig. 3B), in both cases counting two separate experimental interventions tested in one publication (Eggert et al., 2002). In 18 of the studies with an SHB outcome, the actual outcome measure was SA (Fig. 3C). There were 20 RCTs that examined both SI and SHB. The extraction of statistical data posed several study-specific challenges; data inadequacies in some papers resulted in meta-analytic constraints or the need for a workaround, as described in Section 2.6 (Statistical Analysis).

Meta-Analytic results

3.4.

Cohen’s d outcomes

3.4.2.

Suicidal ideation (SI).

Among the subset of studies with SI as a treatment outcome (n = 25), in a model with assumed pre- and post-treatment correlation of ρ = 0.3, the tested interventions significantly decreased SI, but with a very low effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.08, p = 0.01), as illustrated in Fig. 3A. Models with alternative assumptions of ρ = 0.1 and ρ = 0.5 yielded Cohen’s d = 0.09, p = 0.02; and d = 0.09, p = 0.02, respectively. Seven studies with SI as an outcome (Cottrell et al., 2018; Diamond et al., 2010; Eggert et al., 2002; Hetrick et al., 2017; McCauley et al., 2018; Mehlum et al., 2014; Tang et al., 2009) individually demonstrated superiority of the experimental intervention (see Forest plots, Fig. 3).

Egger’s test indicated a significant publication bias (p = 0.03); when this bias was controlled for by adding imputed values (see Fig. 4A for funnel plot) using the trim-and-fill method (Duval and Tweedie, 2000), the overall effect size decreased to d = 0.05 and was not significant p = 0.1.

When, instead of comparing experimental interventions to controls, we explored the effects of the two arms on SI over time, among the subset of studies using ideation scales that could be normalized by z-scoring (n = 20), three findings emerged (Fig. 5). 1) For all studies, experimental intervention and control mean follow-up SI scores were not significantly lower than the respective baseline levels (p = 0.34). However, when only studies with the most severe baseline SI were considered, the follow-up SI scores were highly significantly different from baseline (p = 2.36 × 10−11). 2) Both experimental intervention and control mean follow-up SI scores were strongly positively associated with baseline (t-value= 3.81, β = 0.63, p = 0.001). 3) The two groups were not different at follow-up (t-value = − 0.143, p = 0.120).

Self-harm behavior (SHB).

Considering the RCTs that examined SHB (n = 25), the overall effect size for reduction in SHB in response to experimental intervention compared to control conditions was not significant (Cohen’s d = 0.001, p = 0.97; Fig. 3B). As with the SI outcomes, these calculations were carried out assuming a control group baseline*follow-up correlation of ρ=0.3. With alternative assumptions, if ρ = 0.1, the Cohen’s d was 0.01, p = 0.73; and if ρ = 0.5, then Cohen’s d = 0.05, p = 0.13. Six studies with change in SHB as an outcome (Asarnow et al., 2017; Aseltine et al., 2007; Hetrick et al., 2017; Mehlum et al., 2014; Schilling et al., 2016; Wood et al., 2001) individually demonstrated superiority of the psychosocial intervention (see Forest plots, Fig. 3).

As with SI, for SHB Egger’s test indicated weak evidence for publication bias (p = 0.06). Bias correction through added imputed values (Duval and Tweedie, 2000), resulted in a drop in effect size, from d = 0.001 to − 0.022, with a p-value of 0.54 (see Fig. 4B for funnel plot).

Suicide attempts (SA).

It is possible that treatment could have differential effectiveness regarding SAs vs. other types of SHB. When the model was run (using ρ=0.3) only in the 18 studies in which SAs were the outcome measures, the resulting effect also was not significant (Cohen’s d = 0.03, p = 0.51; Fig. 3C.) Using alternative assumptions of within-subject correlations, we found Cohen’s d = 0.025, p = 0.59 when ρ = 0.1; and d = 0.113, p = 0.004, when ρ = 0.5.

Egger’s test did not indicate publication bias (p = 0.2), and therefore we omitted the funnel plot and trim and fill analyses for SA.

Number needed to treat (NNT)

3.4.2.

SHB.

When we considered the ability of the experimental treatments to prevent one subject from performing an SHB (over one follow-up year) compared to the control treatments, the NNT= 45 (95% CI [26,156]). The attributable risk (AR), or treatment difference in annualized probabilities, was small, at 0.02, but significant, p = 0.01.

However, when the comparison instead was experimental treatment vs. baseline, for all studies with SHB outcomes (excluding one study (Hazell et al., 2009) with non-comparable baseline and follow-up measures), the NNT for SHB decreased to NNT = 12 (95% CI [6,124], AR = 0.09, p = 0.04).

SA.

Similarly, comparing experimental to control treatments with SA outcomes (n = 18), NNT = 42 (95% CI [24,149], AR = 0.02, p = 0.01); this improved to NNT=11 (95% CI [6, 15], AR=0.09, p = 0.04) when experimental treatment was compared to baseline.

Relative risk (RR)

3.4.3.

For SHB, where ρ =0.3, the annualized RR was 0.80 (95% CI [0.63, 0. 96], p = 0.02) Thus, with this method of analysis, the treatment was estimated as 20% more effective than control with confidence ranging from 4% to 37%. If ρ =0.1, then RR=0.77 (95% CI [0.61,0.92], p = 0); if ρ = 0.5, then RR = 0.75 (95% CI [0.61, 0.88], p = 0).

For SA, where ρ = 0.3, the annualized RR was 0.73 (95% CI [0.54 to 0.92], p = 0.01), and thus the treatment was estimated as 27% more effective than control with confidence ranging from 8% to 46%. If ρ = 0.1, then RR = 0.69 (95% CI [0.51, 0.87], p = 0); if ρ = 0.5, then RR = 0.71 (95% CI [0.55, 0.87], p = 0).

Heterogeneity

3.5.

We found that for studies of SI (in models using ρ = 0.3), I2 = 0.62 (95% CI [0.33, 0.79]), indicating moderate heterogeneity. Alternatively, using the “varConstPower” formula for study-wise variance to express heterogeneity as a function of SE, V was approximately 2.3 times v, indicating that the smaller studies (with bigger Cohen’s d SE) were more heterogeneous than the larger studies (with smaller SE).

With respect to SHB, for Cohen’s d, I2 = 0.79 (95% CI [0.57, 0.85]) and for RR, I2 = 0.87 (95% CI [0.77, 0.94]), indicating large heterogeneity. As with SI, the smaller studies of SHB tended to be the most heterogeneous.

Heterogeneity based on study design

3.5.1.

A priori aspects of study design that could contribute to study heterogeneity included (1) recruitment setting; (2) different study entrance criteria based on suicide risk; (3) treatment intensity, i.e., duration and number of sessions, analogous to dose in a medication trial; (4) treatment approaches based on different theoretical premises; and (5) modality, i.e., the nature of the interpersonal interactions (including classroom presentations; in-person or telephone-based therapy with individual adolescents, parents, family, multi-family, or small groups; support from trained adults or adolescents in the community; and online apps). Below we present summary information concerning these potential sources of heterogeneity.

Recruitment settings.

Recruitment settings are described in Supplemental Table S3. Most (n = 23) studies recruited from only one setting; others recruited in two (n = 4) or 4 (n = 3) settings. Over a third of studies recruited in a school environment (n = 8). The remainder recruited from some combination of inpatient units (n = 9), psychiatric ED (n = 8), national child and adolescent mental health agencies (n = 5; UK and Australia), community settings (n = 3), outpatient clinics (n = 3), medical ED (n = 3), or primary care (n = 3).

Pre-existing risk.

Inclusion criteria differed across studies with respect to pre-existing risk (Supplemental Table S4). Specified requirements varied widely, including a recent suicide attempt leading to ED admission (n = 1); at least one episode of SHB, irrespective of intent (n = 8), within varying specifications of time period ranging from immediately before presentation to within 1,3,4, or 12 months; SA or SI with intent and/or plan (n = 4); SA or SI irrespective of intent and/or plan (n = 2); either SA or SH (n = 1); either SA or SH or SI (n = 3); or evidence of all three (n = 1). Two studies by the same team required only SI. In another study, evidence of ‘suicide risk’ was required based on whether the patient responded ‘yes’ to any of the four items on the Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ). Only one study did not require any suicide-specific risk criteria; and another stipulated a lack of suicide attempt history.

Treatment intensity: duration and number of sessions.

The intensity of the planned interventions was also highly variable (see Supplemental Table S5). Durational categories included ultra-brief (n = 5) interventions of a single session; brief (n = 4) treatments lasting 2–4 weeks, of 2–5 sessions; the modal duration, medium-length (n = 12) interventions lasting 5–12 weeks, with a wide-ranging number of sessions from 3 to 64; long treatments (n = 8), encompassing 13–26 weeks and ranging from 6 to 36 sessions; and very long (n = 1), lasting one year but with a mean of only 12 sessions.

Treatment approaches.

Among treatment approaches (see Supplemental Table S6), the most frequently utilized was CBT (n = 8); however, only three featured CBT as monotherapy, while the others combined CBT with Interpersonal Therapy (n = 1), Therapeutic Assessment (n = 1), DBT (n = 2), or DBT together with psychodynamic techniques (n = 1). Also common were supportive (n = 5) and educational (n = 3) approaches. Assessment/screening was considered part of the intervention in several studies (n = 6) including most of the studies performed in schools (5 out of 8). Apart from CBT, studies of a single intervention type utilized DBT (n = 2), Motivational Interviewing (n = 2), Attachment-based (n = 2), Systemic (n = 2), Interpersonal Therapy (n = 1), Mindfulness (n = 1), or Safety Planning (n = 1) approaches. Motivational Interviewing was also used together with an Assessment/Screening approach (n = 1).

Treatment modalities.

Different treatment modalities are detailed in Supplemental Table S7. Most studies used only one modality (n = 18), although some studies employed two (n = 7), three (n = 3) or four (n = 2) modalities. The most frequently employed modalities were individual therapy with adolescents (n = 13), and family therapy (n = 12), with equal numbers of studies employing group therapy (n = 5) and training of other support persons (n = 5). The remaining modalities included therapy with parents (n = 4), classroom-based educational (n = 3), web-based apps (n = 3), multi-family sessions (n = 2), and telephone coaching (n = 1) interventions.

Other sources of heterogeneity

3.5.2.

In addition to a priori study design elements, various aspects of treatment implementation contributed to heterogeneity, including concurrent treatment with psychotropic medications, attendance at treatment sessions, and variable attrition rates.

Concurrent medication use.

Concurrent medication use is detailed in Supplemental Table S8. Most trials (n = 16) did not address medication usage. Among studies that did, some balanced groups with respect to medication usage (n = 6); others reported greater than 10% medication usage that was not balanced across groups (n = 4), 10% or fewer adolescents taking medications (n = 2), or statistical adjustment for medication use (n = 1).

Rates of attrition from treatment or assessment points.

Attrition from treatment was reported in only a subset (n = 23) of RCTs (see Supplemental Table S9). Ten studies reported a completion rate higher than 80% in the experimental intervention group. As to attrition from follow-up assessment, all but three studies reported the rate, and seven studies had overall attrition rates greater than 20%.

Treatment adherence.

Adherence was not a relevant measure for studies of ultra-brief, single-session interventions (n = 4). The remaining studies (n = 25) reported number of sessions attended (n = 17) or percentages of participants who attended certain numbers of sessions (n = 2), or they did not report on adherence (n = 6) (see Supplemental Table S5).

Modulators of effects

3.6.

Mediators and/or moderators were assessed in a minority of articles (n = 12); the remainder (n = 17) did not address this issue (see Supplemental Table S10).

Moderators

3.6.1.

Moderators of study effectiveness were evaluated in nine studies (Aseltine et al., 2007; Cottrell et al., 2018; Green et al., 2011; Harrington et al., 1998; Huey et al., 2004; Kennard et al., 2018; King et al., 2009 2006; Wasserman et al., 2015) and included demographic factors (age, sex, race, ethnicity), SA history, substance abuse history, depression and antisocial symptoms. Only one study evaluated factors related to, traits/other characteristics related to individual differences (Cottrell et al., 2018).

Mediators

3.6.2.

Mediators of the intervention effect on SI/SA were evaluated in four studies (Cottrell et al., 2018; Hetrick et al., 2017; Mehlum et al., 2014; Rossouw and Fonagy, 2012) and included metrics related to intensity of treatment (such as number of sessions attended, duration of treatment), clinical characteristics (depression, hopelessness, anxiety), psychological characteristics (negativity toward problem-solving, mentalization), concomitant medication use, and interpersonal functioning.

Discussion

4.

The search for suicide prevention interventions for adolescents is essential given the increase in suicide deaths in this age group over the last decade (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020). It is, therefore, important to determine if the development and testing of these interventions has been fruitful, and to consider future directions.

Meta-analysis findings

4.1.

The current systematic review and meta-analysis included 30 RCTs of psychosocial interventions published in the last 26 years. The meta-analytic findings indicated that although both the experimental intervention and control arms had some impact in reducing suicide related outcomes, there was no overall statistically significant superior arm with regard to SHB or SI after controlling for publication bias. Our findings diverge from those of the two previous meta-analyses of suicide prevention intervention RCTs in adolescents that reported small but statistically significant differences in effect sizes between the study arms with regard to SHB (Kothgassner et al., 2020; Ougrin et al., 2015) and SI (Kothgassner et al., 2020). Our meta-analysis differed from the above studies in employing a larger sample size (n = 30 vs. n = 19 (Ougrin et al., 2015) and n = 25 (Kothgassner et al., 2020)) and adjusting for several potential confounding effects.

The current meta-analysis included RCTs of interventions based on heterogeneous theoretical approaches, and a lack of replication studies limited our ability to test whether specific types of intervention were more effective than others. Even when considering the studies that implemented the two most frequently cited approaches, CBT (n = 9) and DBT (n = 5), mono-therapeutic treatments were used by only four and two studies, respectively. Thus, we judged it to be of doubtful validity to test for a main effect of CBT or DBT by lumping the studies using pure CBT or DBT approaches together with those that employed a more diluted/eclectic approach.

The reviewed studies differed in many other respects as well. Therefore, rigorous statistical methods were needed to achieve a level of standardization to allow for comparison. Specifically, we employed inclusion of within-person correlations between baseline and follow-up measures to reflect the likelihood of trait tendencies to suicidality; annualization to control for different assessment points across studies; estimating lifetime risk to account for the ages of participants; modeling variability within participants by using the N-R model for more accurate assessment of SD, instead of including the variability across participants; and creating a probability model for each study to account for the studies’ suicide risk-related inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Ten out of the 30 RCTs demonstrated superiority of the experimental interventions: six studies with change in SHB as an outcome (Asarnow et al., 2017; Aseltine et al., 2007; Hetrick et al., 2017; Mehlum et al., 2014; Schilling et al., 2016; Wood et al., 2001) and seven with SI change as an outcome (Cottrell et al., 2018; Diamond et al., 2010; Eggert et al., 2002; Hetrick et al., 2017; McCauley et al., 2018; Mehlum et al., 2014; Tang et al., 2009). These studies evaluated a wide range of interventions using various models, and we cannot point to the superiority of specific interventions or components.

We found that SI significantly decreased from baseline to follow-ups in both arms for studies whose subjects averaged high SI values at baseline, but did not decrease significantly for those whose average baseline scores were low (Fig. 5). This could be because, for studies that recruited participants at the time they were searching for treatment, during crisis, a natural decrease during the study period would be expected.

A possible explanation for the lack of overall superiority of the experimental interventions is that practical and ethical difficulties may preclude designing truly inactive or neutral psychosocial control conditions in this context, as the stakes for treatment failure are high in the study of suicidal adolescents. Thus, treatments as usual (TAU) were likely administered by trained clinicians experienced in treatment of this population. The intrinsic effectiveness of these control treatments is demonstrated in Fig. 5, in which both study arms demonstrated lower average follow-up SI scores than the respective baseline scores. Similarly, for SHB outcomes, NNT estimates comparing ‘active’ with ‘control’ treatments were high. Whereas, compared to the baseline probabilities (i.e., to no treatment rather than a control treatment), the tested interventions were reasonably successful. These findings are consistent with those of a previous meta-analysis (Kothgassner et al., 2020) that reported moderate to large effects of TAU and enhanced usual care (EUC) interventions on SHB and SI. Hence, this raises the question of whether classical clinical trial design methodology is applicable in the context of psychosocial treatments for adolescent suicide risk.

Challenges and future directions

4.2.

Follow-Up assessment timepoints

4.2.1.

Several issues arose in comparing the results across trials with respect to assessments over time. Our statistical modeling accounted for study differences in time between assessments. However, some studies defined an assessment timepoint with regard to baseline, while in other studies the timepoint was measured from completion of the intervention. Thus, depending on the length of the treatment, ostensibly the same timepoint could provide information regarding effectiveness during, immediately after, or months after the completion of the treatment, with obviously different clinical implications. Furthermore, as the outcomes of our analysis were expressed in terms of the estimated probability of SHB over one year, we cannot comment on potential longer-term effects of the experimental interventions.

Medication use as a potential confounder

4.2.2.

It is surprising how few of the included studies reported on medication use concurrent with psychosocial interventions, considering the potential confounding effect of psychopharmacologic treatments on the outcomes (Brent, 2016; Mann et al., 2005). Inclusion of details concerning medication use in future studies could provide a rationale for or against future approaches combining medication with psychosocial treatments.

Mediators and moderators

4.2.3.

Apart from a few studies (Cottrell et al., 2018; Hetrick et al., 2017; Mehlum et al., 2014; Rossouw and Fonagy, 2012), most of the reviewed RCTs did not evaluate possible mediators of treatment effect. Thus, the field is missing potential opportunities to investigate mechanisms of change (Kazdin, 2007), which could have important ramifications for suicide prevention and treatment development.

Similarly, only one-third of the studies evaluated moderators on treatment effects (Aseltine et al., 2007; Cottrell et al., 2018; Green et al., 2011; Harrington et al., 1998; Huey et al., 2004; Kennard et al., 2018; King et al., 2009, 2006; Wasserman et al., 2015). When moderators were evaluated, they usually comprised demographic factors and prior SI/behavior history. As suicidal individuals are not a homogeneous population (Bernanke et al., 2017), evaluating other types of moderators could foster the identification of individuals likely to respond to treatment. For example, one RCT (Cottrell et al., 2018) found that family treatment was effective in reducing SHB only for adolescents who could easily express their emotions. However, for those who had difficulties with emotional expression, the rate of SHB was higher in the experimental treatment group than the control group.

Personalized approaches for suicide prevention

4.2.4.

In line with current health research, personalized treatments are another logical next approach. Rather than asking, “which therapy approach works best?” we could be asking, “what individual characteristics identify responses or resistance to a given therapy?” or “which element of treatment is associated with the greatest response?” Future studies of suicide prevention interventions should test hypotheses concerning the mechanisms that drive suicidality in particular individuals and the treatment approach that best fit their characteristics. In applying precision medicine principles to psychosocial research, the importance of fine-grained statistical reporting cannot be overemphasized. For example, in examining suicide rates along racial lines, a recent report (Bridge et al., 2018) found higher suicide rates in Black compared to White children aged 5–12, but lower rates in all other age groups.

As examples of personalized approaches, RCTs of psychosocial treatments could also quantify and test biologic factors such as nutritional status, genomic or epigenetic markers, and concurrent medication treatment, in addition to psychosocial factors such as psychological characteristics of the child, socioeconomic status, and family composition, as well as gender identification and sexual orientation, given higher suicide rates in LGBTQ adolescents (di Giacomo et al., 2018).

Statistical reporting standards

4.2.4.

In reviewing articles for inclusion in the current meta-analysis, we encountered several types of reporting inadequacies that were severe enough to preclude the consideration of otherwise worthwhile studies, as well as others that were not grounds for exclusion but limited the possibility of more nuanced comparisons and moderator or mediator analyses.

The clinical trials reviewed spanned 26 years, with varying publication standards during this period. Attempts to standardize scientific reporting in general and RCT reporting, in particular, are ongoing (e.g., CONSORT 2010 Statement (Moher et al., 2010); SPIRIT 2013 checklist (Chan et al., 2013)). These guidelines have strongly targeted certain domains of reporting, such as randomization and intent-to-treat designs. However, the same guidelines have been vague concerning reporting standards for statistical tests. For instance, the Clinical Trial Assessment Measure checklists only stipulate assessment of “analysis appropriate to the design and outcome measure type” (Tarrier and Wykes, 2004), and the SPIRIT 2013 criteria merely include instructions to assess “statistical methods for analyzing primary and secondary outcomes,” “methods for any additional analyses (e.g., subgroup and adjusted analyses),” “definition of analysis population relating to protocol nonadherence,” and “any statistical methods to handle missing data” (Chan et al., 2013). We suggest that more specific statistical reporting guidelines are needed for clinical trials. Based on the specific problems we encountered, we propose minimum reporting standards in Supplemental Table S11. If generally adhered to, these, or more comprehensive standards, would increase the value of individual studies with respect to utility in meta-analyses and other data mining approaches.

Limitations

4.3.

The choices made regarding inclusion/exclusion criteria for this meta-analysis conferred some limitations on the results. The exclusion of RCTs in specific diagnostic populations may have resulted in missing some relevant data; however, we believe this exclusion resulted in less heterogeneity and more generalizability.

Another limitation was the inclusion of diverse outcome measures across studies. While both SI and previous SA predict suicide death, past SA are stronger predictors of subsequent SA (Ribeiro et al., 2016). However, many studies examined SHB defined without regard to intent or lethality. As such, the use of this outcome measure included NSSI and thus may have diluted the findings. To capture these diverse outcomes, we reported separately on SHB and SA. There are also disadvantages to SI as a cross-sectional outcome measure when evaluating suicide risk reduction, since SI can exhibit wide temporal fluctuation ranging from chronic to fleeting.

We note that alternative definitions of adolescence could generate samples with different characteristics and result in different outcomes. For instance, youth aged 10–12 may be prepubescent; on the other hand, some consider that adolescence extends into the early 20′s.

In some studies classrooms, not individual children, were randomized. In those cases, we performed the analysis as if it were individual children who were randomized.

In calculating the annualized statistics, we had to contend with different types of reporting in the original papers. Some articles reported mean (Eggert et al., 2002; Hetrick et al., 2017; Wood et al., 2001), median (Mehlum et al., 2014), or at least coarsely discretized (Green et al., 2011) per-person rates of occurrence of the behavior, the most informative way to describe SHB. Most, however, only reported the proportion of participants who exhibited SHB during the relevant period. This cruder form of measurement discarded an essential dimension of SHB occurrence and made it difficult to put the meta-data from different studies on a common footing. Accordingly, for the meta-analysis we had to reduce all studies to the lowest common denominator level, namely the ‘yes-no’ occurrence of SHB. This is but one type of reductionism stemming from variable reporting standards which results in the loss of information across studies, such as contributions of specific covariates.

In assessing relative risk, we found that relative risks computed using the Neyman-Rubin approach were numerically unstable, so we computed them using marginal probabilities, a method not completely congruent with the rest of the meta-analytic results.

While we believe the methods employed in this meta-analysis are valuable in simultaneously addressing effects of within-person tendencies to suicidality, different assessment points across studies, ages of participants, and variability within participants, we recognize that the complexity of this meta-analytic approach does not lend itself to easy replication of our results.

Conclusions

5.

In the face of the ongoing adolescent suicide crisis, many researchers have developed and tested specific psychosocial interventions. In this, the largest meta-analysis to date of psychosocial interventions to reduce SI and SHB among adolescents, we found no clinically significant superiority of experimental interventions over control treatments or vice-versa. Nevertheless, both experimental and control interventions exhibited efficacy in reducing SI and SA compared to baseline. This suggests that traditional ‘placebo’-controlled methodology may not be appropriate for studying psychosocial treatments to ameliorate suicide risk in adolescents. A second major challenge to advancing the knowledge in this field is a lack of standardized statistical reporting standards, thereby creating obstacles to meta-analysis and other big data approaches. A logical next step is to study associations between individual characteristics and treatment responses to identify the adolescents most likely to benefit from specific treatments and to further understand mechanisms of change.
