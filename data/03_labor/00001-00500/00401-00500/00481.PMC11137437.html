Skin cancer risk among testicular germ-cell cancer survivors: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Testicular germ-cell tumours (TGCT) are the most common cancer among young adult men. Previous studies suggested TGCT survivors have an increased risk for skin cancer. The goal of this study was to systematically review the literature and evidence regarding skin cancer risk among TGCT survivors compared with the general population. PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Cochrane Databases and reference lists were included in the search. A systematic review of all comparative studies with more than 10 TGCT survivors reporting on skin cancer incidence was performed. A meta-analysis of the Standardized Incidence Rate (SIR) was calculated by pooling study-specific log-transformed estimates using the random-effects model. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale. Nineteen studies that reported on 147 935 TGCT survivors were included. Pooled SIR for skin cancer and for melanoma incidence among TGCT survivors were 1.93 (95% CI 1.62–2.29, P < 0.0001) and 1.81 (95% CI 1.57–2.08, P < 0.0001), respectively. In conclusion, compared to the general population, TGCT survivors have an increased risk for developing skin cancer and melanoma. Additional long-term studies that include TGCT survivors, additional risk factors and all subtypes of skin cancer are required.

Introduction

With incidence rates steadily increasing, testicular germ-cell tumours (TGCT), including both seminoma and non-seminoma, are the most commonly diagnosed cancer among young adult men. TGCT rates peak between the ages of 30–39 years and reach 13 new cases per 100 000 men.1 However, management and treatment advances in recent decades resulted in a favourable prognosis, and TGCT have a 5-year survival rate of 95%.1 TGCT patients are treated with different combinations of surgery, radiation and chemotherapy drugs that changed over the years, with the most significant changes occurring during the 1970s.2 These young cancer survivors may have an increased risk of second primary cancers, either related to their primary cancer or related to the treatment received.

Skin cancer is the most common cancer in the United States,1 Australia and New Zealand and the third most common cancer in Europe,3 and its incidence is increasing as well.1 Compared to TGCT, skin cancer incidence peaks at an older age. Skin cancer can be divided into two main groups: melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer, including basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and others.1

Some previous studies suggested an increased risk for skin cancer among TGCT survivors. Other studies suggested that TGCT are associated with atypical nevi and dysplastic nevus syndrome, known risk factors for melanoma.4,5 Therefore, many TGCT survivors are referred for a life-long close monitoring by a dermatologist.

Our primary goal was to systematically review the existing literature and evidence regarding skin cancer risk among TGCT survivors compared with the general population. Our secondary goal was to identify factors that may influence skin cancer risk among these patients, including radiation and chemotherapy exposure.

Methods

A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.6 The review was registered with the PROSPERO international prospective register (CRD42020122518).

Eligibility criteria

  • Population: More than 10 seminoma and/or non-seminoma TGCT survivors.

  • Study design: All comparative (analytical) studies with a healthy control group were included.

  • Outcome: Only studies that reported on the incidence of skin cancer and/or its subtypes were included. Permissive criteria for the diagnosis of skin cancer were allowed and studies that did not mention the method for skin cancer diagnosis were also included. Studies reporting on heterogeneous cancers that aggregated their findings, restricting extraction of data on skin cancer, were excluded.

Search strategy

A comprehensive database search was performed using PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and reference lists of included articles. Search criteria included the following: (Testicular cancer [or synonym] AND survivors [or synonym]) AND (follow up [or synonym] OR second primary cancer [or synonym]). Detailed search strategy for each database can be found in Appendix 1. Studies published in or translated into English were included from inception to January 2019. The authors were contacted for missing data and clarifications. Listings were exported into Endnote (Clarivate Analytics).

Data selection and extraction

Using Covidence.org, two reviewers (O.R. and V.V.) independently screened all titles and abstracts followed by full-text articles considered potentially eligible. Any disagreement was resolved by a third author (A.A.M). Subsequently, two reviewers (O.R. and V.V.) independently extracted data from included studies into a predefined electronic form, which included the following: study’s country, year of publication, and design; participants’ number, type of TGCT, stage of TGCT, mean and median age at diagnosis and/or at end of follow-up, and type treatment administered (surgery, chemotherapy and/or radiation); follow-up duration and between which years; total skin cancer, melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma’s incidence and standardized incidence ratio (SIR); and type and size of control group.

Each study was evaluated for risk of bias using the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale7 and was categorized as high (0–2), moderate (3–5) or low (7–9) risk of bias8 (Table 1). Risk of publication bias was assessed using the ‘trim-and-fill method’, which is a way of examining what would have been the average effect if published and unpublished studies were symmetrically distributed around their common average. The method detects the asymmetry of published effects around the average effect and then fills in new ‘unpublished’ effects on the other side to create a more balanced effect between published and suspected unpublished data.9

Data analysis

Primary outcome is the standardized incidence rate (SIR) defined as the risk for developing a second primary skin cancer and/or melanoma among TGCT patients divided by that risk in the general population with similar characteristics. The average SIR was calculated by pooling study-specific log-transformed estimates using the random-effects (of studies) model. Between-study heterogeneity was measured by the I2 statistics and tested by the chi-squared test. All study estimates and their pooled estimate are shown in a Forest plot. The expected SIR was compared across different subgroups or potential influencing factors using meta-regression (with random effects for studies). The subgroups include the following: (i) patients with seminoma vs. non-seminoma TGCT; (ii) patients receiving treatment across different decades (until 1970 vs 1970 onwards); and (iii) patients receiving radiation therapy, chemotherapy or surgery alone. Other factors such as age at beginning of follow-up and follow-up duration were also examined. Possible publication bias was examined by a funnel plot and possibly corrected by the Trim-and-Fill method.10

All analyses were conducted using the ‘metafor’ package (ref) of the R software version 3.6.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Statistical tests were 2-sided using a significance threshold of P < 0.05.11

Results

The initial search yielded 1528 citations. Nineteen studies that reported on 147 935 TGCT survivors from Europe and North America met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). All studies compared skin cancer and/or melanoma incidence among TGCT survivors using local cancer registries. Six studies12–17 reported on seminoma survivors, two studies18,19 reported on non-seminoma survivors, four studies20–23 included two arms, one for each tumour subtype, and seven studies24–30 reported on TGCT survivors, with no indication of testicular tumour subtype (TGCT NOS). In total, 29 786 seminoma survivors, 37 435 non-seminoma survivors and 80 714 TGCT NOS survivors were included in the analysis. Five studies12–16 included only survivors of early-stage TGCT (stage I and/or II), three17,18,30 included all TGCT survivors regardless of stage. Eleven studies19–29 did not mention TGCT stage. Participants were followed up between the years of 1943–2008, median age at beginning of follow-up was 29–40 years, and median follow-up duration ranged between 8 and 22 years (Table 1).

Primary goal – skin cancer risk among TGCT survivors

Four studies23–25,31 reported on the total incidence of skin cancer among TGCT survivors (Fig. 2). The pooled SIR for skin cancer incidence among TGCT survivors compared with the general population was 1.93 (95% CI 1.62–2.29, P < 0.0001). Included studies had substantial heterogenicity32 − 62% (I2, 95% CI 0–95%) of SIR variation was attributed to heterogenicity between studies.

All studies but one24 reported on the incidence of melanoma (Fig. 2). The pooled SIR for melanoma incidence among TGCT survivors compared with the general population was 1.81 (95% CI 1.57–2.08, P < 0.0001). Included studies had moderate heterogenicity32 − 33% (I2, 95% CI 0–80%) of SIR variation was attributed to heterogenicity between studies.

Secondary goal – factors influencing skin cancer risk among TGCT survivors

In the presence of the large heterogeneity of SIR found between studies, we applied meta-regression analysis to evaluate for possible reasons. TGCT subtype (seminoma versus non-seminoma), participants’ age at beginning of follow-up, years of follow-up and follow-up duration, surgical treatment, and chemotherapy treatment did not show a significant difference in melanoma SIR. Of note, since only one33 of 18 studies included patients with no radiation treatment, we could not reliably evaluate the effect of radiation on skin cancer risk. In addition, since only four studies reporting on total skin cancer were included, we were only able to evaluate the effect of TGCT subtype and follow-up duration. Both were found to be non-significant. Of note, nevus count and/or the presence of atypical nevi were not discernable from included studies and were therefore not evaluated as an influencing factor.

Risk of bias assessment

Included studies’ Newcastle–Ottawa7 score ranged from 4 to 7, corresponding to moderate to low risk of bias.8 Major pitfalls were failure to mention skin cancer history prior to follow-up and a relatively short follow-up duration.

In addition, a funnel plot was used to assess risk of publication bias (Fig. 3). It appears that smaller studies were more inclined to report higher effect size, which may point to some publication bias. The correlation between study effect size and their standard error, as assessed by the Pearson and Kendal’s Tao measures, had a P value of 0.01 and 0.06, respectively.

Corrected SIR of TGCT survivors for the development of melanoma using the trim-and-fill method was 1.66 (95% CI: 1.41–1.97). Corrected SIR for total skin cancer could not be obtained due to the limited number of studies.

Discussion

Multiple studies examined the risk of TGCT survivors for the development of skin cancer. This is the first systematic review and meta-analysis that summarized their findings. Although included studies varied in their results and the reason for this variation remains unclear, studies’ quality assessment showed a moderate to low risk of bias.

Compared to the general population, TGCT survivors were found to have a statistically significant increase in their risk for developing skin cancer and melanoma, with a pooled SIR of 1.93 and 1.81, respectively. The increased risk is lower but comparable to other known/major risk factors. For example, the relative risk (RR) for developing melanoma associated with the following factors includes: red hair (RR of 3.64, 95% CI: 2.56–5.37)34; history of sunburn in childhood (RR of 2.24, 95% CI: 1.73–2.89)35; 41–60 nevi (RR of 2.24, 95% CI: 1.90–2.64); and two atypical nevi (RR of 2.56, 95% CI: 1.91–3.43).36

Radiotherapy is a known risk factor for non-melanoma skin cancer37,38 and may also be a risk factor for melanoma.39 Immunosuppressive drugs are also a known risk factor for skin cancer, especially squamous cell carcinoma.40 A sub-analysis of included studies was performed to try and understand whether the observed increased risk for skin cancer and melanoma among TGCT survivors was attributed to the TGCT itself or to the treatment administered. Unfortunately, we could not detect differences in SIR between different TGCT survivors based on cancer subtype or treatment, mainly since most included studies were not designed to perform such comparisons. Furthermore, since history of UV exposure and phenotypic factors such as nevus counts or atypical nevi was not recorded, we could not determine whether TGCT was an independent risk factor for melanoma and skin cancer or it was simply associated with other known risk factors, such as the presence of multiple atypical nevi, as previously suggested.4,5 Further studies that record these factors may help evaluate their impact and could lead to more targeted risk stratification to delineate higher risk subgroups who may need increased clinical monitoring.

This systematic review has several limitations. First, studies significantly varied from each other in terms of their reported SIR, age of participants, follow-up duration and treatment administered. Second, all studies relied on database search and compared skin cancer incidence to cancer registries. Third, most studies only reported on melanoma. A limited number of studies reported on the incidence of skin cancer in general, and no study reported on the incidence of basal cell carcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma, which are the most common subtypes of skin cancer.

In conclusion, compared to the general population, TGCT survivors have an increased risk for developing skin cancer and melanoma. Regarding screening, patient education about the importance of UV protection and self-skin examination should be the cornerstone of management. In addition, periodic skin cancer surveillance should be considered in this population. Further long-term studies that include all subtypes of skin cancer as well as other variables such as nevus counts, skin type, family history and UV exposure are required to determine whether TGCT is an independent risk factor for skin cancer and could help dermatologists develop more targeted clinical screening recommendations.
