The diagnostic accuracy of macrophage colony-stimulating factor for cervical cancer: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Background:

Macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) overexpression in plasma levels serves as a useful predictor of carcinogenesis and poor prognosis. Thus, we aimed to investigate the diagnostic performance of M-CSF for cervical cancer.

Methods:

A comprehensive search of eligible studies was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, and EMBASE published until October 2021. For statistical analysis, Meta-disc software is used. The pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, diagnostic odds ratio, 95% confidence interval (CI), and the area under the curve were estimated. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results:

A total of 5 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The result showed that the pooled sensitivity and specificity of M-CSF to diagnose cervical cancer were 70% (95% CI: 66%–74%) and 84% (95% CI: 80%–88%), respectively. The summary positive likelihood ratio and negative likelihood ratio were 4.41 (95% CI: 2.86–6.82) and 0.36 (95% CI: 0.29–0.45), respectively. The area under the curve of the summary receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.88), indicating the excellent diagnostic performance of M-CSF for cervical cancer.

Conclusions:

The results of this study showed that M-CSF has diagnostic value for the early detection of cervical cancer. As a result, M-CSF can be utilized in conjunction with existing test platforms to diagnose cervical cancer.

1. Introduction

Cervical cancer is the world’s second leading cause of cancer death in women, accounting for over 275,000 deaths each year.[1] Currently, the global incidence and mortality estimates of cervical cancer have risen to 569,847 and 311,365, respectively.[2] The higher death rates are mainly caused by a lack of effective prevention and equal access to early detection and treatment programs.[3] Cervical cancer consists of 2 subtypes, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma, occurring in approximately 80% and 5% to 20% of cases, respectively.[4] Cervical cancer is characterized by increased expression of E6 and E7 genes of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) genes, integration of viral DNA into the host genome with disruption of E2 viral genes, host chromosomal loci, and molecular alterations of key regulators of the cell cycle.[5,6] Clinical and epidemiologic research has declared that HPV is the major risk factor for cervical carcinoma and is present in nearly 100% of the tumors.[7] Besides, early age at first sexual encounter or age at first pregnancy, smoking status, long-term oral contraceptive use, weak immune system, family history, and genetic variation contribute to susceptibility to cervical cancer.[8]

Tumor markers for cervical cancer screening that are available and routinely used in laboratories include SCC antigen (SCC-Ag), cancer antigen 125, cancer antigen 19-9, and serum fragment of cytokeratin.[8] Currently, HPV-DNA testing is approved by the United States food and drug administration as the primary screening test for cervical cancer.[9] The Papanicolaou test (Pap test) is another cytology test of cells collected from the cervix and used to detect abnormal cells before they develop into cancer. The test applies conventional cytological techniques to identify precancerous cells.[10] Major problems in the cytology-based screening of cervical cancer, including low sensitivity and reproducibility, led to variable accuracy.[11] Subsequently, researchers have made efforts to characterize new cancer biomarkers such as macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), microRNA, p16INKa/ki-67, HPV E6/E7/mRNA, and DNA methylation. They have been equally identified in the recent past as potentially useful for early detection of cervical neoplasia.[12]

The M-CSF is a hematopoietic growth factor that promotes macrophage colony formation and regulates the proliferation and differentiation of macrophages.[13] It is secreted by fibroblasts, endothelial cells, activated macrophages, trophoblasts, and some malignant cells such as those of ovarian, endometrial, and breast cancers.[14] The M-CSF activates the colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor, which plays a pivotal role in many aspects related to the tumor microenvironment.[15] Increased expression of M-CSF and its receptor is correlated with poor prognosis in breast, ovarian, and prostate cancer.[16,17] Experimental studies have shown that increased expression of M-CSF and its receptor leads to recruitment of tumor-associated macrophages in different types of cancers that also stimulate cancer cell proliferation and migration.[18] Consequently, M-CSF overexpression in plasma serves as a useful predictor of carcinogenesis and poor prognosis.[11] Recent studies have found that M-CSF expression abnormally increases in many tumors and that circulating M-CSF in the serum has become a molecular marker for various tumors, including cervical cancer.[19]

Overall, research suggests that although the practical use of M-CSF levels as a prognostic factor for cancer risk and/or outcome may be complicated by a high heterogeneity among patient groups and difficulties in determining optimal cutoff levels, at least in some patient groups, M-CSF may be directly involved in tumor progression and malignant behavior and thus constitute interesting therapeutic targets.[20,21] Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the diagnostic performance of M-CSF for cervical cancer.

2. Methods

2.1. Study protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted per the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines. Because this is a systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published studies, no ethical approval and patient consent are required.

2.2. Eligibility criteria

2.2.1. Inclusion criteria.

Articles were eligible for this study if they were

  1. Cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort studies;

  2. Published in peer-reviewed journals in the English language;

  3. Published online up to October 2021;

  4. Patients diagnosed with cervical cancer;

  5. Noncervical cancer patients or healthy volunteers as the control group; and

  6. Sufficient data on the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of M-CSF.

2.2.2. Exclusion criteria.

Studies were excluded if

  1. They did not report or insufficient data on the sensitivity and specificity of M-CSF;

  2. They were case reports, reviews, poster presentations, and editorial letters; and

  3. They were published in non-English languages and nonjournal articles.

2.3. Search strategy

A comprehensive search of eligible studies was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, and EMBASE published until October 2021. The reference lists of published articles were investigated to identify additional relevant studies. The following combinations of keywords and medical subject heading (MeSH) terms were used: “Macrophage colony-stimulating factor” or “M-CSF” AND “cervical cancer” or “cervical neoplasms” or “cervical intraepithelial neoplasia”.

2.4. Selection process

To avoid duplicates, we have imported the retrieved articles to EndNote X9 (Thomson Reuters, USA). After duplicate removal, the authors (T.A. and S.G.) independently screened the titles and/or abstracts of articles. Possible arguments between the 2 review authors were solved through discussions and mutual consensus. Then, articles that comply with the eligibility criteria undergo full-text appraisal.

2.5. Data extraction

The extracted data from the included articles was summed up into an excel spreadsheet. Author and year of publication, number of cases and controls, age range of cases and controls, diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, specimen type, and detection method were all extracted by the authors.

2.6. Outcomes of interest

The main outcome of interest is investigating the power of M-CSF value in diagnosing cervical cancer.

2.7. Risk of bias measurement

The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 tool was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies. The tool has 4 categories to evaluate eligible studies: patient selection, index test, reference standard, flow and timing. The first 3 categories were assessed in terms of risk of bias and applicability. However, the last category; flow and timing, were evaluated in terms of risk of bias only. The bias risk may be determined as “low,” “high,” or “unclear” depending on the answers to the signaling questions in each part (“yes,” “no,” or “unclear”).[22]

2.8. Statistical analysis

Meta-disc software was used to conduct the statistical analysis. We have estimated the pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), diagnostic odds ratio, 95% confidence interval (CI), summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve, and the area under the curve (AUC). The I2 statistics were used to examine statistical heterogeneity between the included studies. A meta-regression was performed to explore the potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger regression test. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Study selection

A total of 122 articles were screened for inclusion. Of the articles, 108 were excluded as not being relevant and/or duplicates, leaving 14 studies for screening. Finally, 5 studies were included in the qualitative and quantitative analysis (Fig. 1).

In all included articles, M-CSF was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. The diagnostic sensitivity ranged from 54% to 87.72%, while the diagnostic specificity was from 64.71% to 93%. In their study, Ławicki et al[23] used control groups of 30 cervical intraepithelial neoplasia patients and 45 healthy volunteers. Besides, Zajkowska et al[11] included 50 healthy, untreated women and 30 women with ectropion lesions as a control group (Table 1).

3 5. Diagnostic performance of M-CSF for cervical cancer diagnosis

A meta-analysis of all the 5 studies showed that the summary sensitivity and specificity of M-CSF to diagnose cervical cancer were 70% (95% CI: 66%–74%) and 84% (95% CI: 80%–88%), respectively (Fig. 3). The summary PLR and NLR were 4.41 (95% CI: 2.86–6.82) and 0.36 (95% CI: 0.29–0.45), respectively (Fig. 4). The AUC of the SROC curve was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.88), indicating the excellent diagnostic performance of M-CSF for cervical cancer (Fig. 5).

3.7. Meta-regression

In this study, meta-regression was performed on the continuous covariates, year of publication. Accordingly, the result of the meta-regression showed that year of publication is associated with the heterogeneity of the included studies (coefficient = 2.63, P = .0016).

4. Discussion

Hematopoietic growth factors are synthesized by cells of various malignant tumors. Higher expression of these growth factors has also been found in the serum of patients with malignancies of the reproductive organs.[24] For example, increased plasma concentration of M-CSF is seen in ovarian, endometrial, breast, and cervical cancer or cervical intraepithelial neoplasia patients. These markers are capable of discriminating cervical cancer patients from those without malignant transformation.[23] Significantly elevated levels of M-CSF in advanced stages of cancer have also been found in patients with malignancies of the reproductive organs.[25]

Cervical cancer screening methods include Pap smear, ThinPrep cytology test, second-generation gene hybridization capture technology, colposcopy, cervical tissue biopsy, and others.[18] The accuracy of Pap smear in diagnosing cervical precancerous lesions remains low. The sensitivity and specificity of Pap smear in diagnosing cervical cancer vary from 34.3% to 93.8% and from 34.7% to 96.5%, respectively.[26,27] Besides, the cytology test and pap smear are also expensive, not suitable for low-resource settings, require trained personnel, and long assay time.[28] Studies showed that the sensitivity of carcinoembryonic antigen and CA125 were 25% to 60% and that of SCC-Ag was <30% when used to diagnose early cervical SCC.[29,30]

Although sophisticated diagnostic instruments can provide highly sensitive, selective, and reproducible results, many of these settings in developing nations lack the necessary infrastructure. Thus, there is a significant unmet need for an ideal cancer screening platform having characteristics that make it amenable for developed and developing countries. These include low cost, portability, simplicity of assay protocol, sensitivity and selectivity, rapid and robust, and multiplexing.[28] The purpose of the new markers is primarily to reduce the percentage of undiagnosed patients, which will also improve their survival rate.[31] An ideal tumor marker should have a high sensitivity and specificity to discriminate between patients with cancer and those with benign conditions or healthy controls and should also provide information related to tumor burden and activity.[32]

When compared with the existing test platforms, the finding of the present study showed that M-CSF has a good diagnostic value for diagnosing cervical cancer. In this study, the pooled sensitivity and specificity of M-CSF to diagnose cervical cancer were 70% (95% CI: 66%–74%) and 84% (95% CI: 80%–88%), respectively. The AUC of the SROC curve was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.88), indicating the excellent diagnostic performance of M-CSF for cervical cancer. In general, an AUC of 0.5 suggests no discrimination, 0.7 to 0.8 is considered acceptable, 0.8 to 0.9 is considered excellent, and >0.9 is considered outstanding.[33] Sensitivity measures the proportion of positive results that are correctly identified. Diagnostic specificity, which is explained as the ability to exclude healthy individuals without the disease, was high and reached the value of 84% for M-CSF. Additionally, the summary PLR and NLR of M-CSF were 4.41 (95% CI: 2.86–6.82) and 0.36 (95% CI: 0.29–0.45), respectively. A likelihood ratio of 1.0 indicates that there is no difference in the probability of the particular test result (PLR and NLR) between those with and without the disease. A likelihood ratio >1.0 indicates that the particular test result is more likely to occur in those with disease than in those without disease, whereas a likelihood ratio of <1.0 indicates that the particular test result is less likely to occur in those with disease than those without the disease. As likelihoods move farther away from the value of 1.0, the strength of their association with the presence or absence of disease increases. Thus, tests with very high PLR and very low NLR have a greater discriminating ability, and tests with likelihood ratios >10 or <0.1 are very useful in establishing or excluding a diagnosis.[34] Besides, the collection of serum samples for M-CSF is more convenient and quicker, which is comfortable for initial screening and will not cause great trauma to patients. The M-CSF can be combined with other existing diagnostic platforms. For example, screening for M-CSF in women with abnormal cytology results or other patients at high risk of malignant cervical disease, or for whom routine cervical cancer screening procedures are not available, can aid in early diagnosis of the diseases more quickly and accurately.[18] Some authors recommended the interpretation of the M-CSF value in conjunction with other complementary markers such as VEGF or SCC-Ag to improve specificity for optimal treatment benefit, given that their levels are equally raised in other types of cancer.[11,35]

4.1. Limitations

Although the results are in corroborate with the individual studies included in the meta-analysis, clinical translation studies with larger cohorts of cervical cancer patients would be appropriate for adequate validation of M-CSF. There is also heterogeneity in the included studies limiting the interpretation of the study.

5. Conclusion

The result of this study showed that M-CSF has a diagnostic value for early detection of cervical cancer. Thus, M-CSF can be used in conjunction with the existing test platforms for cervical cancer diagnosis.

## 1. Introduction

Cervical cancer is the world’s second leading cause of cancer death in women, accounting for over 275,000 deaths each year.[1] Currently, the global incidence and mortality estimates of cervical cancer have risen to 569,847 and 311,365, respectively.[2] The higher death rates are mainly caused by a lack of effective prevention and equal access to early detection and treatment programs.[3] Cervical cancer consists of 2 subtypes, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma, occurring in approximately 80% and 5% to 20% of cases, respectively.[4] Cervical cancer is characterized by increased expression of E6 and E7 genes of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) genes, integration of viral DNA into the host genome with disruption of E2 viral genes, host chromosomal loci, and molecular alterations of key regulators of the cell cycle.[5,6] Clinical and epidemiologic research has declared that HPV is the major risk factor for cervical carcinoma and is present in nearly 100% of the tumors.[7] Besides, early age at first sexual encounter or age at first pregnancy, smoking status, long-term oral contraceptive use, weak immune system, family history, and genetic variation contribute to susceptibility to cervical cancer.[8]

Tumor markers for cervical cancer screening that are available and routinely used in laboratories include SCC antigen (SCC-Ag), cancer antigen 125, cancer antigen 19-9, and serum fragment of cytokeratin.[8] Currently, HPV-DNA testing is approved by the United States food and drug administration as the primary screening test for cervical cancer.[9] The Papanicolaou test (Pap test) is another cytology test of cells collected from the cervix and used to detect abnormal cells before they develop into cancer. The test applies conventional cytological techniques to identify precancerous cells.[10] Major problems in the cytology-based screening of cervical cancer, including low sensitivity and reproducibility, led to variable accuracy.[11] Subsequently, researchers have made efforts to characterize new cancer biomarkers such as macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), microRNA, p16INKa/ki-67, HPV E6/E7/mRNA, and DNA methylation. They have been equally identified in the recent past as potentially useful for early detection of cervical neoplasia.[12]

The M-CSF is a hematopoietic growth factor that promotes macrophage colony formation and regulates the proliferation and differentiation of macrophages.[13] It is secreted by fibroblasts, endothelial cells, activated macrophages, trophoblasts, and some malignant cells such as those of ovarian, endometrial, and breast cancers.[14] The M-CSF activates the colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor, which plays a pivotal role in many aspects related to the tumor microenvironment.[15] Increased expression of M-CSF and its receptor is correlated with poor prognosis in breast, ovarian, and prostate cancer.[16,17] Experimental studies have shown that increased expression of M-CSF and its receptor leads to recruitment of tumor-associated macrophages in different types of cancers that also stimulate cancer cell proliferation and migration.[18] Consequently, M-CSF overexpression in plasma serves as a useful predictor of carcinogenesis and poor prognosis.[11] Recent studies have found that M-CSF expression abnormally increases in many tumors and that circulating M-CSF in the serum has become a molecular marker for various tumors, including cervical cancer.[19]

Overall, research suggests that although the practical use of M-CSF levels as a prognostic factor for cancer risk and/or outcome may be complicated by a high heterogeneity among patient groups and difficulties in determining optimal cutoff levels, at least in some patient groups, M-CSF may be directly involved in tumor progression and malignant behavior and thus constitute interesting therapeutic targets.[20,21] Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the diagnostic performance of M-CSF for cervical cancer.
## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Study protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted per the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines. Because this is a systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published studies, no ethical approval and patient consent are required.

### 2.2. Eligibility criteria

#### 2.2.1. Inclusion criteria.

Articles were eligible for this study if they were

Cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort studies;

Published in peer-reviewed journals in the English language;

Published online up to October 2021;

Patients diagnosed with cervical cancer;

Noncervical cancer patients or healthy volunteers as the control group; and

Sufficient data on the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of M-CSF.

#### 2.2.2. Exclusion criteria.

Studies were excluded if

They did not report or insufficient data on the sensitivity and specificity of M-CSF;

They were case reports, reviews, poster presentations, and editorial letters; and

They were published in non-English languages and nonjournal articles.

### 2.3. Search strategy

A comprehensive search of eligible studies was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, and EMBASE published until October 2021. The reference lists of published articles were investigated to identify additional relevant studies. The following combinations of keywords and medical subject heading (MeSH) terms were used: “Macrophage colony-stimulating factor” or “M-CSF” AND “cervical cancer” or “cervical neoplasms” or “cervical intraepithelial neoplasia”.

### 2.4. Selection process

To avoid duplicates, we have imported the retrieved articles to EndNote X9 (Thomson Reuters, USA). After duplicate removal, the authors (T.A. and S.G.) independently screened the titles and/or abstracts of articles. Possible arguments between the 2 review authors were solved through discussions and mutual consensus. Then, articles that comply with the eligibility criteria undergo full-text appraisal.

### 2.5. Data extraction

The extracted data from the included articles was summed up into an excel spreadsheet. Author and year of publication, number of cases and controls, age range of cases and controls, diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, specimen type, and detection method were all extracted by the authors.

### 2.6. Outcomes of interest

The main outcome of interest is investigating the power of M-CSF value in diagnosing cervical cancer.

### 2.7. Risk of bias measurement

The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 tool was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies. The tool has 4 categories to evaluate eligible studies: patient selection, index test, reference standard, flow and timing. The first 3 categories were assessed in terms of risk of bias and applicability. However, the last category; flow and timing, were evaluated in terms of risk of bias only. The bias risk may be determined as “low,” “high,” or “unclear” depending on the answers to the signaling questions in each part (“yes,” “no,” or “unclear”).[22]

### 2.8. Statistical analysis

Meta-disc software was used to conduct the statistical analysis. We have estimated the pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), diagnostic odds ratio, 95% confidence interval (CI), summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve, and the area under the curve (AUC). The I2 statistics were used to examine statistical heterogeneity between the included studies. A meta-regression was performed to explore the potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger regression test. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.
