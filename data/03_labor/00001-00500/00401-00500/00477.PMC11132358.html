Is vitamin D deficiency a risk factor for all-cause mortality and rehospitalization in heart failure patients?: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Objective:

The association of low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level with mortality and rehospitalization remains inconsistent in patients with heart failure. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the value of blood 25-hydroxyvitamin D level in predicting all-cause mortality and hospitalization in heart failure patients.

Methods:

Two reviewers independently search the articles indexed in PubMed and Embase databases until November 30, 2021. Only the prospective or retrospective cohort studies evaluating the association of blood 25-hydroxyvitamin D level with all-cause mortality and rehospitalization in heart failure patients were selected. The predictive value of 25-hydroxyvitamin D level was summarized by pooling multivariable adjusted risk estimates for the bottom versus reference top 25-hydroxyvitamin D level.

Results:

Seven studies with a total of 5941 patients with heart failure were identified. The pooled adjusted risk ratio (RR) of all-cause mortality was 1.37 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.13–1.66), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 70.5%; P = 0.002). However, there was no clear association between low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level and all-cause rehospitalization risk (RR 1.38; 95% CI 0.87–2.19).

Conclusions:

Low blood level of 25-hydroxyvitamin D may be an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality in patients with heart failure. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level may provide prognostic information in heart failure patients. Additional randomized controlled trials are required to explore whether treatment of 25-hydroxyvitamin D deficiency by supplementation of vitamin D can improve survival in heart failure patients.

1. Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a worldwide public health concern. The incidence of HF patients continues to rise due to a growing and aging population.[1] Despite advancement in evidence-based therapies, HF patients remain facing substantially high risk of mortality, morbidity, and recurrent hospitalization.[2] Therefore, there is in great demand for risk stratification of HF patients.

Vitamin D is a hormone precursor that is essential for human health. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level is clinically accepted index for estimating vitamin D status.[3] Although there is no consensus on the optimal levels of vitamin D deficient, a level of 25-hydroxyvitamin D <50 nmol/l is generally considered as insufficient. Increasing attention has been addressed on the role of vitamin D in disease management, particularly in cardiovascular disease.[4] Vitamin D deficiency is prevalent among HF patients.[5,6] However, these available studies have reported the controversial results regarding the low serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level as a prognostic indicator in HF patients.[7–11] On the other hand, clinical trials investigating the impact of vitamin D supplementation on clinical outcomes in HF patients remain inconclusive.[12,13]

No previous meta-analysis has comprehensively explored the prognostic association of vitamin D deficiency with adverse outcomes in HF patients. Therefore, we performed this meta-analysis to examine the prognostic role of low serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level in patients with HF in terms of all-cause mortality and rehospitalization.

2. Methods

2.1. Search strategy

The current meta-analysis was followed by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis checklist.[14] This study was not registered in the PROSPERO database owing to the time-consuming process after COVID-19 pandemic. Two reviewers independently search the articles indexed in PubMed and Embase databases up to November 30, 2021. The following keywords was used in combination: “25-hydroxyvitamin D” OR “vitamin D” AND “heart failure” OR “cardiac failure” AND “death” OR “mortality” OR “hospitalization” OR “hospitalization” OR “readmission”. In addition, we also manually reviewed references of pertinent articles. Language of articles was restricted in Chinese and English. This study only adopted the study-level data and ethical approval was not required.

2.2. Study selection

The criteria for inclusion included: (1) population: patients were diagnosis with HF; (2) predictor: baseline serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level; (3) comparison: participants with the bottom versus those with top 25-hydroxyvitamin D level; (4) outcomes: all-cause mortality or hospitalization; (5) provided multivariable adjusted relative risk of all-cause mortality or all-cause rehospitalization associated with serum25-hydroxyvitamin D level; and (6) study design: prospective or retrospective cohort studies. The exclusion criteria were as follow: (1) studies without providing risk estimate data of death or hospitalization; (2) studies reported the risk estimate by continuous 25-hydroxyvitamin D data; (3) outcome measures were not of interest; and (4) abstracts or unpublished studies.

2.3. Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers collected the relevant data using a standard form. Surname of the first author, year of publication, study design, country of origin, sample sizes, gender distribution, patients’ age, follow-up duration, low 25-hydroxyvitamin D cutoff, outcome measures, fully adjusted risk estimate, and adjusted variables were abstracted from the included studies. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was applied to assess the methodological quality of the included studies.[15] Studies with 7 or over NOS points were deemed as high-quality. A consensus was reached between 2 reviewers when any discrepancies existed in these processes.

2.4. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 12.0. To summarize the prognostic value of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level, we pooled the fully adjusted risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the bottom versus top group of 25-hydroxyvitamin D level. Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated using the Cochran Q test and the I2 statistic. A P value > 0.1 for the Cochran Q test and/or I2 statistic < 50% suggested a lack of heterogeneity, and we selected a fixed-effect model. When there was significant heterogeneity, a random effect model was used. Leave-out one study sensitivity analysis was conducted to observe the impact of each individual study on overall risk summary. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the impact of the type of HF, sample sizes, study design, and follow-up duration. Publication bias was checked by the Begg test and Egger test.

3. Results

3.1. Search results and study characteristics

In total, 1861 articles were identified in our initial electronic database search. After removing duplicates, 1432 articles were left. We excluded 1401 articles based on reviewing the titles and abstracts. Thirty-one full-text articles were retrieved for detailed evaluation, and 7 studies[7–11,16,17] were finally included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Five studies[7,9,10,16,17] were prospective cohorts and 2[8,11] were retrospective studies. Two studies[9,17] included chronic HF patients and other studies did not report the specific type of HF. A total of 5941 HF patients were identified (sample sizes ranging between 136 and 3009). Follow-up periods ranged from 12 months to 5.1 years. Based on the NOS criteria, all studies were deemed as high-quality.

3.2. All-cause mortality

All studies investigated the impact of low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level on all-cause mortality. As shown in Figure 2A a random effect model meta-analysis indicated that the bottom group of 25-hydroxyvitamin D level was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (pooled RR 1.37; 95% CI 1.13–1.66) compared with the top group of 25-hydroxyvitamin D level, with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 70.5%; P = 0.002). The pooled risk estimate remained statistically significant in the leave-out 1 study sensitivity analysis (data not shown). Moreover, Table 3 summarized the results of subgroup analysis. No evidence of publication bias was detected based on the Begg test (P = 0.133) and the Egger test (P = 0.170).

3.3. Rehospitalization

Two studies[9,11] investigated the impact of low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level on all-cause rehospitalization. As shown in Figure 2B a random effect model meta-analysis showed that the bottom 25-hydroxyvitamin D level was not significantly associated with an increased risk of rehospitalization than the top group of 25-hydroxyvitamin D level (pooled RR 1.38; 95% CI 0.87–2.19). Significant heterogeneity was observed between studies (I2 = 87.2%; P = 0.005).

4. Discussion

This meta-analysis summarized the prognostic role of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level in HF patients. The main findings of our meta-analysis suggested that low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level may be a significant predictor of all-cause mortality in patients with HF, even after adjustment for confounding factors. When compared with high 25-hydroxyvitamin D group, patients with the low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level had an approximately 35% higher risk of all-cause mortality. However, there was no clear association between low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level and risk of rehospitalization for HF patients.

Apart from categorical analysis, analysis of vitamin D level as a continuous variable also supported its prognostic role. Each 10 nmol/L decrease in 25-hydroxyvitamin D level was associated with a 10% higher risk of all-cause mortality in patients with HF.[18] A 2.72-fold increase in 25-hydroxyvitamin D level conferred a 14% lower risk of all-cause mortality in chronic HF patients.[17] In addition, low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level also independently predicted cardiovascular death and rehospitalizations for worsening HF in Chinese patients.[10] Another cohort of patients with mild-to-moderate HF, 25-hydroxyvitamin D level was a significant and independent predictor of HF-related mortality.[19]

Different stages of HF may affect the prognostic role of 25-hydroxyvitamin D level. Our subgroup analysis suggested that the association of low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level with all-cause mortality appeared to be weaker in chronic HF patients (pooled RR 1.21) compared with all HF patients (pooled RR 1.65). This result indicated the value of low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level in predicting all-cause mortality may be stronger in acute HF patients. However, the value of low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level in predicting all-cause mortality was not statistically significant in retrospective studies subgroup. This finding may be explained by selection bias of retrospective design. Moreover, lack of significantly prognostic value of low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level among studies within 1 year of follow-up may be correlated with a limited number of the death event.

Several potential mechanisms have been proposed to explain the reduced survival in HF patients with vitamin D deficiency. Firstly, vitamin D deficiency can activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.[20,21] Secondly, vitamin D deficiency may damage HF patients by increasing inflammatory response.[22] Finally, low vitamin D level leads to increase parathyroid hormone level, subsequently causing left ventricular hypertrophy and affecting systolic function.[23]

The current meta-analysis suggested that HF patients with low serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level should be received closely monitored and intensive treatment. HF patients with low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level may be potentially benefited by vitamin D supplementation. Previous meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials indicated that vitamin D supplementation could reduce serum level inflammatory mediators but had no benefits on improvement of left ventricular function in chronic HF patients.[12,13] Studies investigating the impact of vitamin D supplementation on mortality have yielded contradictory results in HF patients. A retrospective observational study showed that treatment with vitamin D 800 and 1000 units per day was associated with a 32% reduced risk of mortality in HF patients with vitamin D deficiency.[8] In contrast, a 3-year randomized clinical trial showed that a daily vitamin D dose of 4000 IU did not reduce mortality in patients with advanced HF.[24] More well-designed randomized controlled trials are warranted to elucidate whether supplementation of vitamin D can improve the prognosis of HF patients.

There are several potential limitations in our meta-analysis. Firstly, single detection of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level at baseline may have led to classification bias. Secondly, different thresholds of vitamin D deficiency were used in the prediction of all-cause mortality and rehospitalization across studies which prevent us to determine the optimal cutoff value of low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level. Thirdly, our meta-analysis did not assess the prognostic value of 25-hydroxyvitamin D level by continuous variable analysis due to lack of insufficient studies. Fourthly, our pooling results were limited by significant heterogeneity, which may be partly interpreted by the different study designs, stages of HF, cutoff values of vitamin D deficiency, or follow-up duration. Fifthly, we did not perform subgroup analysis based on the subtypes (HF with a reduced ejection fraction and HF with preserved ejection fraction) due to lack of such data. Finally, some important confounding factors such as NYHA class, renal function, season of the year, and medications were not consistently adjusted in the statistical model. Lack of adjustment for residual confounding may have resulted in overestimation of the risk estimate.

5. Conclusions

Low 25-hydroxyvitamin D level is possibly an independent prognostic factor for all-cause mortality in patients with HF. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level can provide prognostic information in HF patients. Future randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are necessary to evaluate whether management of 25-hydroxyvitamin D deficiency by supplementation of vitamin D can improve survival in HF patients.
