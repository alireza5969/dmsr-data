Systematic Review of Discourse and Social Communication Interventions in Traumatic Brain Injury

Abstract

Purpose:

This study reviewed the current state of discourse and social communication interventions in traumatic brain injury (TBI) to provide clinically focused guidance about treatment efficacy, treatment approaches by TBI severity, treatment components, and treatment outcome measures.

Method:

Searches were conducted in five electronic databases and reference lists of topical articles for discourse or social communication interventions in TBI published between 2012 and 2021. Search terms reflected three concepts: TBI, treatment, and cognitive-communication. Studies were evaluated for methodological quality using rating scales specific to study design.

Results:

Seven hundred sixty-seven records were identified, culminating in 21 studies for qualitative synthesis. All approaches resulted in improvement post-treatment, but durability and strength of evidence varied. Five treatment components were identified as “essential” for fostering change. Discourse approaches were generally more effective in mild-to-moderate TBI, whereas social communication approaches were more effective in moderate-to-severe TBI. Communication outcome measures were generally more sensitive to change than measures of other domains of functioning.

Conclusions:

The evidence suggests that discourse and social communication treatments are promising for improving communication in TBI. Selection of treatment components and tailoring treatment to the individual are important clinical considerations. Use of at least two proximal outcome measures that evaluate the target behavior and extent of functional generalization may be advantageous. The field would benefit from additional, more rigorous treatment studies to provide a greater understanding of how best to treat cognitive-communicative impairments in people with TBI.

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a substantial contributor to the global and national injury burden with a prevalence of more than 55 million cases worldwide (James et al., 2019). The majority of TBI cases, approximately 75%, are of mild severity (National Center for Injury Prevention and Control, 2003). Cognitive-communication disorders (CCDs) are difficulties in communication that arise as a result of underlying cognitive impairments (MacDonald, 2017) and are very common following TBI. Occurring in an estimated 80%–100% of TBI cases, CCDs potentially affect tens of millions globally and several million individuals in the United States alone (MacDonald & Wiseman-Hakes, 2010).

Impairments in discourse and social communication are a hallmark of CCDs in TBI and are well documented and pervasive across all levels of severity, including mild TBI (Gallagher & Azuma, 2018; Stockbridge & Newman, 2019). As may be expected, there is much overlap between discourse and social communication features. Common characteristics of discourse impairments following TBI include deficient global coherence, reduced efficiency of conveying information, impoverished or inaccurate content, disorganization of ideas, and decreased initiation and maintenance of topic in conversation (Coelho, 2007; Coelho et al., 2005; Hough & Barrow, 2003; Stout et al., 2000). Impaired social communication following TBI is often reflected in poor turn-taking, verbosity or paucity of speech, problems with topic initiation and management, reduced tactfulness and management of nonverbal cues, difficulty understanding and conveying emotional tone, and difficulty with theory of mind (ToM; Coelho, 1995; Douglas, 2017; McDonald, 2013; Snow et al., 1997).

Discourse and social communication impairments often do not resolve over time and have wide-ranging functional impact. Various studies have shown that impaired discourse persists several years and even decades post-injury (Coelho et al., 2013; Ponsford et al., 2008; Snow et al., 1998). Discourse functioning has been tied to return to work and job stability, with fewer than half of individuals with TBI securing stable work after their injury (Isaki & Turkstra, 2000; Meulenbroek & Turkstra, 2016; Rietdijk et al., 2013; van Velzen et al., 2009). Individuals with discourse deficits after TBI also routinely report struggles with maintaining social relationships, loss of social contact, and social isolation (Dahlberg et al., 2006; Galski et al., 1998). More severe impairments in discourse ability have been shown to predict poorer psychosocial outcomes (Elbourn et al., 2019). Discourse and social communication impairments impact functioning across a number of life domains, presenting significant challenges to occupational functioning, social reintegration, and quality of life (QoL) following TBI.

The prevalence, chronicity, and functional impact of discourse and social communication impairments following TBI have spurred researchers both in the United States and abroad to develop and trial assessments and interventions targeting various aspects of cognitive-communication ability. In particular, development of cognitive-communication interventions targeting discourse and social communication impairments has expanded considerably over the past 10–15 years, with treatment trials in multiple countries, reflecting the global importance and potential impact of this field of work. In 2012, an international panel of experts in TBI and cognitive rehabilitation, referred to as the International Cognitive Working Group (INCOG), was convened to develop evidence-based and best practice guidelines for the assessment and management of CCDs. The INCOG working group put forth seven recommendations to guide the development of cognitive-communication intervention programs (Togher et al., 2014). Overall, the recommendations advocated for recognition of the influence of personal context on communication, implementation of patient-centered approaches, involvement of communication partners (CPs), and addressing goals and measuring outcomes functionally at the level of participation in activities that have meaning and ecological validity for the individual with TBI.

As the field of discourse and social communication treatments in TBI grows, there is value to having evidence-based frameworks and models that can guide clinicians in assessment and intervention decisions. A recent scoping review sponsored by the Academy of Neurologic Communication Disorders and Sciences (ANCDS) identified three categories of social communication models (i.e., cognitive, social competence, and pragmatic) and underscored the utility of models to inform patient-centered clinical practice (Byom et al., 2020). MacDonald’s (2017) model of cognitive-communicative competence integrates social communication in the broader context of multiple domains of functioning and, therefore, may be a particularly valuable resource for clinicians (Sohlberg et al., 2019). The model provides guidance for evidence-based cognitive-communication assessments and interventions after TBI by integrating the INCOG recommendations and current practice guidelines into a comprehensive framework. There are seven interconnected domains identified in the model that should be considered in cognitive-communication rehabilitation: the individual, context/environment, cognition, communication, physical/sensory functioning, and emotional/psychosocial functioning, all of which contribute to the last domain of communication competence.

Although the focus of this systematic review is on discourse and social communication treatments in TBI, a brief discussion of assessment is germane and provides context for this review’s examination of outcome measures that may be useful for evaluating change. In 2005, the ANCDS writing committee reviewed the assessment literature and presented guidelines for cognitive-communication assessment in people with TBI (Coelho et al., 2005; Turkstra et al., 2005). The authors recommended exercising caution in the use of standardized language assessments due to their limitations in evaluating this population (e.g., weak construct validity, little consideration of the individual’s functional context) and implementing standardized tests in a broader and contextualized patient-centered assessment framework (Turkstra et al., 2005). As such, nonstandardized, functional assessments (e.g., discourse analyses, pragmatic rating scales, and communication checklists) were viewed as necessary components of the evaluative process.

Since publication of the 2005 ANCDS papers, research in cognitive-communication assessment has grown considerably, offering new perspectives and resources. Steel and Togher (2019) reviewed standardized and nonstandardized assessment measures for TBI, including assessment batteries, pragmatic rating scales, and discourse analyses, to identify diagnostic resources and their potential clinical application. A key point of discussion was the need to improve feasibility of discourse analyses, as they are often laborious and time consuming. In a more recent ANCDS-sponsored review of social communication assessment tools, Sohlberg et al. (2019) found that only about a third of the measures reviewed evaluated communication while the remainder assessed social cognition. The communication measures comprised a combination of clinician-reported, self/other-reported, and performance-based tools. Despite growth in social communication assessment research, both Steel and Togher and Sohlberg et al. (2019) noted the dissociation between assessment approaches in clinical practice and research with clinicians continuing to rely on decontextualized, impairment-focused tools.

There have been several prior literature reviews of cognitive interventions for TBI. However, these reviews have covered the broader field of cognitive interventions, such as those targeting attention, memory, and executive functions, and did not focus on cognitive-communication impairments following TBI specifically (Cicerone et al., 2000, 2005, 2011, 2019; Hallock et al., 2016; Kennedy et al., 2008; Sohlberg et al., 2007). While CCDs are associated with cognitive impairments, improving cognitive function alone does not appear to improve cognitive-communicative function (Henderson et al., 2020; Youse & Coelho, 2009).

Cognitive-communication treatment in TBI is a nascent field, and controlled trials for discourse and social communication interventions are only beginning to emerge. Consequently, literature reviews of interventions that target communication at the level of discourse and social communication are sparse. To date, there have only been three literature reviews specifically examining discourse or social communication interventions in adults with TBI (Finch et al., 2016; Meulenbroek et al., 2019; Steel et al., 2021). Finch et al.’s (2016) narrative systematic review evaluated 15 social communication treatment studies published through 2013, with the focus of the review on the efficacy of context-sensitive versus impairment-specific communication treatments in TBI. Overall, the authors concluded that individuals with cognitive-communication impairments following TBI benefit from treatments targeting social communication regardless of approach. In an ANCDS-sponsored mapping review, Meulenbroek et al. (2019) assessed 21 treatment studies (those in the systematic reviews of Finch et al., 2016, and Togher et al., 2014, and an additional four articles) with the intent to complement existing related systematic reviews by examining and connecting social communication and treatment theories to the identification of treatment targets and treatment ingredients based on an intervention framework. The authors recommended three key treatment guidelines: (a) consideration of ability and need in intervention selection, (b) measurement of multiple aspects of performance, and (c) promoting generalization. Steel et al.’s (2021) systematic review of narrative discourse interventions examined six studies for efficacy, theoretical basis, and treatment ingredients. While posttreatment gains were noted on some aspects of treated narratives, generalization and maintenance of learned skills was variable. Based on the findings, the authors reiterated the importance of incorporating the INCOG guidelines into intervention development.

The current systematic review is an update and expansion of the previous work by Finch et al. (2016), Meulenbroek et al. (2019), and Steel et al. (2021). We aimed to not only evaluate the research evidence for social communication interventions in TBI but also extend the scope of our review to discourse interventions in TBI. None of the prior reviews examined discourse and social communication interventions together. This review has no overlap in articles with Finch et al. (2016), two overlapping articles with Meulenbroek et al., and six overlapping articles with Steel et al. While the current systematic review shares similarities in approach with the prior reviews, it also offers novel information in the combined evaluation of discourse and social communication interventions, inclusion of more recent treatment studies, and an exploration of treatment approaches based on TBI severity.

The overarching goal for the current review is to provide guidance to clinicians who are seeking to treat discourse and social communication impairments following TBI. In support of this goal, we identified four questions of interest:

  1. What is the efficacy of discourse and social communication treatments?

  2. Which treatment approaches show promise for different levels of TBI severity?

  3. What are the treatment components that show promise for individuals with TBI?

  4. What measures are useful for measuring change in discourse and social communication for individuals with TBI?

Given the limited number of controlled trials and heterogeneity of outcome measures across treatment studies, a meta-analysis could not be performed for the current review. Because a statistical approach was not possible, a narrative approach was implemented in answering the study questions.

Method

Study Identification and Selection

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed in conducting and reporting this review study. Searches were performed to identify in-press or published articles in peer-reviewed journals in English in five electronic databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and Scopus. Dates of coverage for searched articles were January 1, 2012, through January 24, 2021. Although the prior review by Finch et al. (2016) included articles through October 2013, the current study searched for articles from the prior year to ensure no relevant articles were overlooked or omitted by their review. An exception was made to include two studies (Cannizzaro & Coelho, 2002; Youse & Coelho, 2009) that were published prior to 2012 as they were both early investigations of discourse treatment in TBI, were not included in the prior review, and were referenced in a number of subsequent discourse and social communication treatment studies in TBI.

Search terms entered into each electronic database were related to three main concepts: TBI, treatment, and cognitive-communication. TBI search terms were entered as “TBI OR traumatic brain injury OR head injury OR concussion OR brain trauma OR blast injury.” Treatment search terms were entered as “treatment OR intervention OR therapy OR management OR rehabilitation OR remediation OR program.” Cognitive-communication search terms were entered as “cognitive-communicat* OR pragmatic OR social communicat* OR cognitive communicat* OR vocational communicat* OR work communicat* OR discourse.” Where allowed by the database, searches were limited to English language articles, and “review” and “child OR children OR pediatric” were entered as exclusion terms to exclude review articles as well as studies focusing on cognitive-communicative intervention in children. In addition to articles identified through the electronic database search, reference lists of identified articles were also manually searched for articles that met the aforementioned inclusion criteria.

As per PRISMA guidelines, eligibility criteria were specified for study characteristics, including participants, intervention, comparison, outcomes, and study design, discussed as follows. (a) Participants must have been adults (18 years or older) with TBI. This review did not include discourse or social communication treatment studies in acquired brain injury, which included other etiologies of brain injury (e.g., stroke, brain tumor) where the data for TBI were not presented separately, given that the clinical presentation associated with CCDs following TBI is significantly different from those associated with aphasia following stroke and other more focal brain injuries (Glosser & Deser, 1991; Heilman et al., 1971). (b) The intervention described must have directly targeted aspects of discourse, social communication, or both in individuals with TBI. Treatments that did not directly treat the individual with TBI were excluded. For example, treatments that focused primarily on caregiver or CP training were excluded. However, treatments that included the individual with TBI in treatment along with CP training were included. (c) Eligible studies did not have to include a comparison or control group or alternative to treatment. (d) Eligible studies must have reported measurable outcomes for discourse and/or social communication. (e) Records that were clinical trial registrations, errata, review articles, or didactic papers were excluded.

Article titles and abstracts were examined for the delineated eligibility criteria. In cases where the title and abstract did not provide enough information to determine eligibility, the full article was reviewed to determine inclusion or exclusion into the current review. If an article had questionable eligibility, it was discussed among the authors, and eligibility was determined through consensus.

Please refer to the PRISMA flow diagram (see Figure 1) for details of the selection process. The total number of identified articles was 769, including 767 from electronic database searches and two manual additions. Duplicates were removed. Of the 524 remaining records that were screened, 45 full-text articles met the first thresh-old of focusing on the interventions of interest. These articles were reviewed for inclusion according to the eligibility criteria, and 24 articles that did not meet the criteria were excluded. The 21 remaining articles were included for qualitative synthesis.

Assessment of Methodological Quality

First and third authors independently rated the treatment studies for methodological quality using well-established and widely used rating scales specific to the treatment design. Supplemental Material S1 summarizes the assessment categories and items for each scale. Dis-agreements in ratings were resolved through discussion and consensus. Corresponding authors for each study were contacted and informed of their study’s inclusion in this review and of the point-by-point quality rating as a courtesy and were provided an opportunity to comment on initial quality ratings. Feedback from study authors was taken into consideration in reaching consensus among all authors of the current manuscript regarding the final study ratings score.

Nonrandomized and randomized controlled trials (nRCTs and RCTs, respectively) were assessed using the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale (Maher et al., 2003). The PEDro scale comprises 11 items and assesses the external and internal validity of a clinical trial and interpretability of findings. The PEDro quality rating score is based out of a total possible score of 10, as the first item is related to external validity and not tallied in the total score.

Treatment studies that were single-case designs (SCDs) were evaluated for methodological quality using the Single-Case Experimental Design (SCED) scale (Tate et al., 2008). The SCED scale is also an 11-item rating, for which 10 items, evaluating methodological quality and use of statistical analysis, contribute to the total quality rating score.

For studies that implemented a cohort design without a comparison group and did not fit study design criteria for either the PEDro or the SCED scale, the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Before–After (Pre–Post) Studies with No Control Group (NIH Pre–Post; National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, 2021) was used. The NIH Pre–Post was developed as a guide for evaluation of a study’s internal validity and not intended as a rating scale to determine methodological quality. Because no rating scale exists to assess cohort studies, the NIH Pre–Post was implemented to provide a sense of study quality in these studies, which comprise one third of the articles in this review, as the alternative was to bypass rating them altogether. Rather than using qualitative ratings of “good,” “fair,” and “poor” as provided in the original scoring guidance, the scoring system was modified to be similar to that of the PEDro and the SCED. Thus, a tally system was used to provide a numeric score. If a study met the criteria for an item, 1 point was awarded. The NIH Pre–Post consists of 12 items. Item 12 was omitted from the calculation of the score as it pertained to study characteristics inapplicable to the cohort studies in the current review. Thus, the maximum score was 11.

There was great variability in study type that was not accurately reflected by quality ratings on the three different scales. Hence, the quality of each research design was determined using the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) Levels of Evidence hierarchy (Howick et al., 2011). Based on OCEBM criteria, RCTs are considered the highest quality research design and nRCTs the next highest. After controlled trials, cohort studies without a comparison are next in the hierarchy, followed by SCDs. Thus, although an SCD may receive an SCED score of 10, an RCT with a PEDro score of 5 would still meet criteria for a higher level of evidence based on the rigor of the research design.

Results

Twenty-one studies met the criteria for inclusion for this review, comprising one RCT, five nRCTs (including partially randomized RCTs), seven cohort studies, and eight SCDs. Thirteen unique treatment approaches were represented in the 21 studies as several treatment approaches were evaluated in multiple studies.

Methodological Quality

Each study was assessed for methodological quality using the assessment tool associated with the research design. RCTs and nRCTs were evaluated using the PEDro, cohort studies with the NIH Pre–Post, and SCDs with the SCED (see Tables 1 and S1).

The quality rating scales provided a general sense of the strengths and weaknesses by study type. There was one RCT (Harrison-Felix et al., 2018) and two nRCTs (several studies described different aspects of the same clinical trial [first nRCT: Rietdijk et al., 2020a, 2020b; second nRCT: Sim et al., 2013; Togher et al., 2012, 2016]). Studies from the same trial were scored based on their combined information. Overall, methodological strengths of the controlled trials included baseline similarities between groups, use of “intention to treat” analysis, and reporting of treatment effects and variability. Weaknesses concerned concealment and randomization of participant allocation and double-blinding as only the RCT met these criteria. Methodological strengths across the cohort studies were specification of objectives, participant criteria and intervention, use of statistical methods to examine treatment effects, and multiple measurements of outcomes before and after the intervention, whereas weaknesses involved blinding of assessors, accounting for participants lost to follow-up, and use of adequate sample size. Methodological strengths across the SCDs were identified as use of A-B-A or multiple-baseline design, reporting of raw data points, and testing of generalization, whereas weaknesses pertained to sufficient treatment phase sampling, independence of assessors, use of statistical analysis, and replication (across participants, therapists, or settings).

Participants

Participant characteristics are shown in Table 2. The overall age range across studies was 18–68 years. Of the studies that tabulated average age, the vast majority had mean ages (in years) in the 30s and 40s across groups. Education ranged from 9 to 18 years across studies, with the majority of participants having high school level or higher.

There were a total of 370 participants with TBI (98 female; 272 male). For manuscripts stemming from one singular clinical trial, the number of participants was counted once per trial using the largest numbers reported across studies. In Whitworth et al.’s (2020) study, only one individual had TBI of the four participants in the study. As such, this study is listed as having an n of 1 for the purposes of this review. The overall proportion of males to females with TBI was 2-to-1, comparable to findings in epidemiological studies of TBI (Frost et al., 2013; Langlois et al., 2006).

Time post-onset of injury varied from 4 months to almost 48 years across studies. Participants were well beyond the acute stage of TBI with the vast majority at least 1 year post-onset of TBI. Posttraumatic amnesia was reported in only nine studies, ranging from 1 to 183 days. Thirteen studies characterized severity in terms of the actual brain injury, whereas the remainder described severity in terms of cognitive-communicative impairment. In Whitworth et al.’s (2020) study, the sole individual with TBI performed within the normal range on a standardized cognitive-communicative assessment but demonstrated pragmatic deficits at the conversational level, suggesting that TBI severity was likely mild.

Overview of Treatment Approaches, Treatment Components, and Outcome Measures

Across the 21 studies reviewed, 13 unique and direct discourse and social communication intervention approaches were identified as shown in Table 3. Formal cognitive training (i.e., APT) was incorporated into two treatment approaches and is listed separately. APT is not considered a stand-alone discourse or social communication treatment program. Intervention approaches fell into two broad categories: discourse oriented or social communication oriented. Although several studies used discourse analysis as an outcome measure, five studies implemented discourse-oriented approaches, in which specific aspects of discourse performance were targeted and the primary outcomes were discourse measures. Four of the discourse-oriented approaches addressed impairments in narrative discourse, whereas the fifth addressed those in conversational discourse. Social communication approaches focused on communicative impairments at the level of social interaction. Treatment was delivered in one of four formats: individual session with participant with TBI, group session consisting of participants with TBI, dyadic session with participant with TBI and the CP, and group session with pairs of participants with TBI and their respective CPs. Some studies incorporated a combination of formats.

Each study was reviewed for primary treatment components, which are the main ingredients and principles of the intervention. Overall, 10 treatment components were identified across studies (see Table 3): (a) feedback, (b) simulated/actual social context, (c) functional practice of learned skills (i.e., routine application of learned skills and strategies to interactions with CPs), (d) training in metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies, (e) hierarchical training (i.e., variety of increasingly complex stimuli), (f) structured training prompts or scaffolding, (g) patient-centered goal setting, (h) errorless learning, (i) training in nonverbal behavior (i.e., understanding facial expressions, gesture, and body language), and (j) cognitive training (i.e., training to improve aspects of attention, memory, and/or executive functions). While not an exhaustive list of treatment components, the 10 treatment components reflect the major components implemented across the 21 studies as a whole and merit consideration by clinicians developing cognitive-communication treatment programs for people with TBI.

Of the 10 components, feedback was the most frequently implemented (n = 13) across the 14 treatment approaches followed by training in metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies (n = 12). The use of simulated or actual social contexts and functional practice were also common treatment components, occurring in 11 and 10 approaches, respectively. Hierarchical training and structured training prompts/scaffolding were incorporated in more than half of the treatment approaches (n = 9 and n = 8, respectively). Less widely implemented treatment components included patient-centered goal setting (n = 6), nonverbal behavior training (n = 5), cognitive training (n = 2), and errorless learning (n = 1). Treatment components associated with each treatment approach are presented in Table 3 (APT was listed as a separate approach for ease of treatment component identification in addition to the 13 unique discourse and social communication treatment approaches).

Outcome measures varied widely across studies in type and number and could be broadly grouped into seven categories: discourse, pragmatic/social communication, functional communication, QoL/functioning, psychological distress, participant goal achievement, and cognition (see Table 4; for exemplars of outcome categories, see Table S2). For the purposes of presenting the outcome measures in a clinically meaningful way, operational definitions were developed for the categories. For this review, pragmatic/social communication measures and functional communication measures were considered separate outcome categories. Pragmatic/social communication measures were operationalized as assessments of specific and discrete social communication behaviors, whereas functional communication measures were operationalized as those that evaluate communication competence in the context of the real-life environments of the individual with TBI and that are often self-reported. Measures assessing QoL, social integration, and functioning in daily life were grouped into the QoL/functioning category. The measurement of changes in outcomes is key to understanding treatment effects.

Research Question 1: Efficacy of Discourse and Social Communication Interventions

Treatment efficacy is evidenced by the extent to which an intervention effects a beneficial change in target outcomes, with the gold standard being RCTs involving comparison between a treatment and a control/placebo condition. Given that there was only one full RCT in studies reviewed, rigorous efficacy data regarding discourse and social communication interventions are currently limited, but preliminary data are promising.

Overall, improvement in at least one aspect of communication was observed at the end of treatment in all 21 studies, although treatment effects varied across studies. Detailed treatment outcomes for each study are presented in Table 1. Maintenance data were not reported for four studies (Keegan et al., 2019; Schneider & Van Auken, 2018; Sim et al., 2013; Togher et al., 2012). Of the 17 studies that did report on durability of effects after treatment, two discourse treatment studies found no carryover of skills at follow-up (Cannizzaro & Coelho, 2002; Youse & Coelho, 2009). In the remaining 15 studies, at least one of the therapeutic effects observed at posttreatment was maintained at follow-up. In a few studies, maintenance of some treatment gains was observed along with declines on some outcome measures (Douglas et al., 2014; Rietdijk et al., 2020b; Whitworth et al., 2020).

The controlled trials provided the strongest level of evidence and were all social communication oriented. The sole RCT in this review compared two group social communication treatment approaches: group interactive structured treatment (GIST) and alternative intervention (AT; Harrison-Felix et al., 2018). GIST implemented a psychoeducation curriculum in an interactive group format. In contrast, AT delivered the GIST curriculum in a class lecture format with a weekly individual session. There were no significant differences between groups on the various outcome measures (i.e., social communication, functional communication, psychological distress, QoL). Participants with TBI improved their communication skills and maintained gains regardless of treatment condition. Both groups also made significant gains in functional communication at follow-up. In AT, psychological distress decreased after treatment with changes maintained.

The nRCTs focused on social communication interventions involving training of individuals with TBI and their CPs (TBIconneCT: Rietdijk et al., 2020a, 2020b; JOINT: Sim et al., 2013; Togher et al., 2012, 2016). The TBIconneCT studies compared in-person versus telehealth (videoconference) treatment delivery against a historical control group. The JOINT treatment approach implemented similar treatment elements to TBIconneCT but engaged participants in group sessions with other pairs. The studies examining JOINT compared dyadic treatment to individual treatment of the participant with TBI (SOLO condition) and/or to a no-treatment control condition. Because the Togher et al. (2013) paper on outcomes for JOINT was already examined in the prior Finch et al. (2016) review, the current review focused on the JOINT studies that were not included in that review (Sim et al., 2013; Togher et al., 2012, 2016).

Findings for the nRCTs were variable based on the outcome measures and participant categories. In both TBIconneCT and JOINT approaches, trained CPs demonstrated significant gains on conversational discourse measures, reflecting increased support of conversations with their partners with TBI. Both modes of treatment delivery of TBIconneCT were equally effective. In the JOINT approach, there was also a treatment effect on the functional communication measure when reported by the CPs. However, no significant differences were found between trained participants with TBI and their respective control groups on functional communication measures in either approach. A possible reason for the lack of change in functional communication measure may have been due to limited change in the discourse content of participants of TBI as evidenced in the JOINT approach. Posttreatment analysis revealed that trained participants endorsed significantly more items with perceived positive change in communication ability. However, an expectancy bias is possible given that trained participants knew they were receiving an experimental treatment, whereas the historical control group received no treatment.

A number of social communication treatment approaches were evaluated in cohort studies without control groups. These types of studies can provide preliminary information regarding potential treatment effects but can-not ascertain that a beneficial change in communication was due primarily to the treatment, as opposed to factors such as practice effects, loss to follow-up, cohort effects, and other potential confounds. Cognitive pragmatic treatment (CPT; Bosco et al., 2018; Gabbatore et al., 2015; Parola et al., 2019; Sacco et al., 2016) is one such approach that addressed multiple aspects of social communication using group treatment, whereas Communication-specific Coping Intervention (CommCope-I; Douglas et al., 2014, 2019) addressed social communication using principles of cognitive behavioral therapy and self-coaching in individual and dyadic treatment. In both CPT and CommCope-I, participants demonstrated improvements on social and functional communication measures and maintained gains at follow-up. Improvements in discourse production occurred at posttraining, but maintenance data were not provided (Parola et al., 2019). Changes in cognitive performance after CPT were variable (Bosco et al., 2018; Gabbatore et al., 2015; Parola et al., 2019). Notably, in CommCope-I, changes in psychological distress only emerged during follow-up, suggesting that improvements in psychosocial functioning may take some time to emerge following treatment (Douglas et al., 2019).

The two remaining cohort studies (metacognitive strategy intervention [MSI]: Finch et al., 2017; Improving Natural Social Interaction: Group reHabilitation after TBI [INSIGHT]: Keegan et al., 2019) implemented goal achievement in their social communication approaches. Both MSI and INSIGHT featured group treatment, whereas MSI provided additional individual treatment. Improvements on communication outcomes, including goal achievement, were observed in both approaches. In MSI, participants made gains on social communication measures, but no changes in functional communication were observed posttreatment. Participants with TBI receiving INSIGHT improved discourse skills at the end of treatment.

The SCDs comprised both social communication and discourse treatment studies. SCDs that were then followed up with a larger cohort or controlled study (e.g., TBIconneCT, CommCope-I) will not be discussed here as the treatment programs were discussed in previous sections. The Schneider and Van Auken (2018) SCD study implemented a pragmatic language group and used a single outcome measure of communication QoL. Improvements in some aspects of communication QoL were observed at the end of treatment. No maintenance data were provided. Although the study suggests that the intervention increased communicative competence, it is unclear what aspects of the intervention contributed to the change. The lack of effect size reporting and small sample size limit interpretation of treatment benefits.

The remaining SCDs were discourse-oriented treatment approaches and, as a group, had variable treatment effects and durability. The SCDs that targeted narrative discourse all resulted in some improvement in aspects of narrative discourse posttreatment. Gains were maintained in two treatment approaches, an adaptation of the Novel Approach to Real-life communication: Narrative Intervention in Aphasia (NARNIA; Whitworth et al., 2020) and discourse processing treatment (DPT; Kintz et al., 2018), but not maintained in Cannizzaro and Coelho’s (2002) approach, which trained story grammar and metalinguistic. The NARNIA adaptation also improved performance on functional communication measures but not on QoL/functioning or cognitive measures. At follow-up, functional communication declined, suggesting that while learned skills were maintained, generalization of those skills to everyday communication was less successful.

The two other discourse-oriented SCDs (Henderson et al., 2020; Youse & Coelho, 2009) treated discourse impairments in combination with APT (Sohlberg et al., 1994), a cognitive training program. Youse and Coelho (2009) paired APT with interpersonal process recall (IPR; Helffenstein & Wechsler, 1982) using videotaped feedback to improve conversational discourse. Negligible to small effects were observed on discourse measures following treatment but not at follow-up. Cognitive outcomes did not change. Henderson et al. (2020) examined DPT in combination with APT in a crossover study to address narrative discourse deficits. DPT alone resulted in improved discourse performance compared to APT alone, but the combination of DPT and APT produced greater generalization. Cognitive gains were variable. Of note, two participants demonstrated generalization of skills to a different discourse genre, expository discourse. The extent to which skills generalized to communication in daily activities is unclear as a functional communication measure was not administered.

Research Question 2: Promising Treatment Approaches for Different Levels of TBI Severity

A range of TBI severity levels were represented across the studies. Only the RCT (Harrison-Felix et al., 2018) included individuals with all severities of TBI. Although both GIST and AT treatment approaches resulted in significant improvements in communication, data were not stratified by severity. GIST and AT may be beneficial across severity levels given that each treatment arm had a range of severity, but it is also plausible therapeutic effects were driven by one or two groups of severity.

Four studies (Henderson et al., 2020; Kintz et al., 2018; Schneider & Van Auken, 2018; Whitworth et al., 2020) included individuals with mild-to-moderate severity. Three of the studies targeted discourse production deficits in individual sessions (Henderson et al., 2020; Kintz et al., 2018; Whitworth et al., 2020). Overall, these approaches (DPT, DPT/APT, NARNIA) resulted in improved discourse performance for those with mild-to-moderate severity with durability of some effects observed at follow-up. Participants in Schneider and Van Auken’s (2018) post-concussion syndrome pragmatic language group demonstrated gains in functional communication. Because no follow-up was conducted, durability of gains is unknown.

Four studies included participants with moderate-to-severe TBI and implemented social communication approaches (MSI: Finch et al., 2017; INSIGHT, Keegan et al., 2019; TBIconneCT: Rietdijk et al., 2020a, 2020b). Treatment delivery varied between group sessions with others with TBI (MSI, INSIGHT) and dyadic sessions with the individual with TBI and their CP (TBIconneCT). In MSI, participants with TBI also received concurrent individual sessions. Across the three treatment approaches, participants with moderate-to-severe TBI demonstrated gains in discourse and social communication outcomes that were generally maintained for the studies that conducted follow-up (MSI and TBIconneCT). However, functional communication outcomes did not change in these studies, and no functional communication measure was used in the INSIGHT study. There were no major differences on outcomes between the in-person and telehealth groups who both received TBIconneCT, suggesting that individuals with moderate-to-severe TBI benefited equivalently from either delivery format.

The majority of the studies (n = 12) focused treatment solely on individuals with severe TBI, 10 oriented toward social communication and two toward discourse ability. Since the subsequent TBIconneCT nRCTs were discussed in the previous section and provided more robust findings, the related SCD study (Rietdijk et al., 2019) will not be discussed here. As discussed in the previous section, both CPT (Bosco et al., 2018; Gabbatore et al., 2015; Parola et al., 2019; Sacco et al., 2016) and CommCope-I (Douglas et al., 2019) improved social communication with durability of gains in participants with severe TBI. Those in CommCope-I also improved in functional communication and psychological distress. While participants in CPT engaged in group treatment with other participants with TBI, those in CommCope-I received a combination of individual and dyadic treatment with their CPs. Based on these results, social communication approaches provided in some variation of group treatment show promise for people with severe TBI. Findings suggest that the combined individual/group delivery may further support functional communication in severe TBI and that improvements on distal outcomes (i.e., outcomes of functioning beyond communication) may not be immediately observed posttreatment.

The only treatment approaches specifically addressing individuals with severe TBI that were examined in controlled trials were the JOINT and SOLO social communication approaches (Sim et al., 2013; Togher et al., 2012, 2016). As described in the previous sections, JOINT was delivered in concurrent dyadic sessions to the participant with TBI and their CP and group sessions comprising other dyads, whereas SOLO was delivered in concurrent individual and group sessions with others with TBI. Trained CPs demonstrated more facilitating behaviors to increase communicative productivity and reported improved functional communication in their partners with TBI (Sim et al., 2013) compared to untrained CPs. However, as noted previously, JOINT and SOLO were not effective in improving communication outcomes for individuals with severe TBI based on self-reported functional communication and discourse measures, although those who received treatment perceived more positive change than those who did not.

The JOINT and SOLO approaches were very similar to TBIconneCT, but the latter improved communication outcomes for people with moderate-to-severe TBI. It is possible that the extent of severity in the JOINT and SOLO approaches may have limited learning and that individuals with severe TBI may not have had the insight to recognize changes in functional communication. Treatment delivery may have also been a factor as TBIconneCT participants only received dyadic treatment, and dyadic sessions were of longer duration than in JOINT and SOLO. The results suggest that group treatment with multiple dyads of people with TBI and CPs may not be beneficial for those with severe TBI and, in some cases, training of the CP may be the more effective way to support communication with people with severe TBI.

Discourse-oriented treatment approaches were used in two SCD studies with participants with severe TBI (narrative discourse production treatment: Cannizzaro & Coelho, 2002; IPR/APT: Youse & Coelho, 2009). In both studies, treatment was delivered individually. Although some gains in discourse ability were observed, there was no maintenance of learned skills at follow-up. The lack of durable effects in the narrative discourse approach may be attributed to the lack of treatment components to support maintenance and carryover of skills, such as use of a simulated/actual social context and functional practice. Closer examination of IPR/APT revealed that treatment included simulations of social situations and functional practice with an unfamiliar CP, all in the therapy setting. As such, it is plausible that these activities may have been less ecologically valid and meaningful for the participants with TBI and less likely to support generalization to communication in daily tasks. As noted for the JOINT and SOLO approaches, severity of TBI may have also been a barrier to learning.

Research Question 3: Promising Treatment Components

A closer examination of all treatment components associated with the different discourse and social communication approaches may provide further insights into the range of outcomes and which aspects of treatment hold promise for people with TBI. In the sole RCT in this review, both GIST and AT approaches improved communication skills in people with TBI (Harrison-Felix et al., 2018). Social communication performance and psychological distress improved significantly for the AT group only, but functional communication improved in both groups. As noted previously, the lack of significant differences between groups on outcomes suggests the approaches may be of comparable benefit to people with TBI. Of the 10 treatment components identified, GIST incorporated seven: feedback, simulated/actual social context, functional practice of learned skills, training in metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies, hierarchical training, patient-centered goal setting, and training in nonverbal behavior. AT included all GIST treatment components with the exception of simulated/actual social context. The AT classroom format may have provided more structure that may have been more conducive to learning for people with TBI than the interactive GIST format, thereby producing more robust outcomes.

Across the approaches implemented in the nRCTs, TBIconneCT (Rietdijk et al., 2019, 2020a, 2020b) and JOINT/SOLO (Sim et al., 2013; Togher et al., 2012, 2016), discourse ability improved in the former but not the latter approach, and neither had an impact on functional communication for the participant with TBI. However, treatment effects were observed for the CPs. Both approaches incorporated didactic instruction, role plays conversational practice, and review of recorded conversations. TBIconneCT included five of the 10 identified treatment components: feedback, simulated/actual social context, functional practice of learned skills, training in metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies, and structured training prompts/scaffolding. Both JOINT and SOLO integrated the five TBIconneCT components with patient-centered goal setting and training in nonverbal behavior for a total of seven treatment components each. TBIconneCT may have been more successful in improving discourse outcomes by focusing on and allowing more time in dyadic treatment (i.e., participant with TBI and CP) than JOINT or SOLO, which delivered shorter duration dyadic treatment in combination with group sessions with other dyads. The treatment components in TBIconneCT and JOINT/SOLO are similar to those for GIST and AT (Harrison-Felix et al., 2018) but were less successful in effecting change in communication for individuals with TBI, which suggests that TBI severity and delivery format may be important considerations in treatment outcomes in addition to selection of treatment components.

The treatment approaches in the cohort studies (CPT: Bosco et al., 2018; Gabbatore et al., 2015; Parola et al., 2019; Sacco et al., 2016; CommCope-I: Douglas et al., 2014, 2019; MSI: Finch et al., 2017; INSIGHT: Keegan et al., 2019) resulted in improvements on at least one communication measure. CPT and CommCope-I both improved social and functional communication in participants with TBI. CPT administered training in language, gestures, paralinguistic cues, social appropriateness, ToM, planning, and self-awareness. CommCope-I targeted improving awareness, skill development, and performance evaluation. MSI, which employed metacognitive training, was successful in improving social communication but not functional communication. INSIGHT focused on environmental modifications, compensatory strategy training, and education, and gains were observed in discourse ability, but the impact on functional communication is unclear as the latter was not assessed. Four treatment components were common to CPT, CommCope-I, MSI, and INSIGHT: feedback, simulated/actual social context, functional practice of learned skills, and training in metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies, indicating that these components are likely important considerations in the foundation of a treatment program. CPT also incorporated two other treatment components: training in nonverbal behavior and cognitive training, for a total of six components. Patient-centered goal setting was an additional treatment component common to CommCope-I, MSI, and INSIGHT. CommCope-I and INSIGHT also shared hierarchical training and structured training prompts/scaffolding and implemented the same seven treatment components, whereas MSI had five components. The inclusion of these two components in CommCope-I may have supported generalization of communication skills to daily tasks, which may explain why functional communication improved in CommCope-I but not in MSI.

Examination of treatment components may provide insights into the variability of treatment effects in the SCDs. Although some small effects were observed post-treatment in Cannizzaro and Coelho’s (2002) narrative discourse treatment and in the conversation discourse treatment IPR with APT (Youse & Coelho, 2009), there was no durability of effects. The treatment approach by Cannizzaro and Coelho integrated four treatment components: feedback, metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies, hierarchical training, and structured training prompts/scaffolding. Modeling, coaching, and rehearsal were primary features of IPR. The combined IPR/APT approach subsumed those in Cannizzaro and Coelho’s approach with the exception of training in metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies and included simulated/actual social context, functional practice of learned skills, and cognitive training for a total of six components. However, upon closer examination, the social context and functional practice involved conversations with the examiner or an unfamiliar conversational partner (e.g., another clinician) in the treatment room. Thus, similar to Cannizzaro and Coelho’s narrative discourse treatment, the decontextualized nature and reduced ecological validity of the interactions in IPR/APT may have accounted for the smaller effects and lack of carryover.

Findings for the remaining SCDs were inconsistent across outcomes or limited in outcome reporting, but examination of differences in their treatment components may be helpful in understanding the outcomes. Schneider and Van Auken’s (2018) pragmatic language group treatment focused on relationship building among group members, goal setting, counseling, accountability, and functional practice and resulted in improvements on a functional communication questionnaire created for the group, which was the sole outcome measure. Because of the limited outcome measures and lack of maintenance data, it is unclear what aspects of communication or functioning in other domains may have influenced outcomes and if treatment effects were durable. In contrast, the adapted NARNIA approach (Whitworth et al., 2020), an individual narrative discourse treatment, administered multiple outcome measures. The participant with TBI improved discourse performance and functional communication but only maintained gains in discourse. Like NARNIA, DPT (Henderson et al., 2020; Kintz et al., 2018) addressed narrative discourse impairments and resulted in durable improvements in aspects of story content but did not assess functional communication. However, greater generalization to a different discourse genre was observed when DPT was combined with APT.

All three treatment approaches had six of the 10 identified treatment components and shared four: feedback, simulated/actual social context, metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies, and hierarchical training. Functional practice of learned skills and training in nonverbal behavior were only included in the pragmatic language group treatment, whereas structured training prompts/scaffolding were only implemented in NARNIA and DPT. Errorless learning was unique to NARNIA, and cognitive training was unique to DPT/APT. These findings suggest that the addition of functional practice and understanding of nonverbal cues may have promoted better carryover of learned skills into everyday activities, but it is also possible that the socialization associated with group treatment may have influenced functional communication outcomes. The results also implicate the use of more structured treatment approaches with fixed treatment criteria to effect change in discourse and the potential utility of including cognitive training to support generalization of discourse skills.

Summary of Promising Treatment Components

With a few exceptions, the treatment studies that had six or more treatment components were generally more effective than those that had less. In addition to quantity, the quality of treatment components emerged as an important consideration. In general, five particular treatment components were found across approaches that produced durable improvements in communication: feedback, simulated/actual social context, functional practice of learned skills, metalinguistic/metacognitive strategy training, and hierarchical training. This suggests that these five treatment components may be good “building blocks” in developing a discourse or social communication intervention program.

Research Question 4: Outcome Measures Useful for Evaluating Change

Outcome measures can be broadly categorized as proximal or distal measures (see Tables 4 and S2). Proximal measures are those that assessed the communication behaviors of interest and include pragmatic/social communication, functional communication, and discourse measures. Goal achievement was considered a proximal measure as studies that evaluated goal achievement assessed progress toward goals pertaining to a communication behavior. In most studies, changes following treatment were observed on proximal measures regardless of treatment orientation and delivery format.

Information regarding communication ability can be collected in different ways. Ordered by approximate length of administration, the measurement approaches include self-report and informant report, ratings of language samples, and fine-grained analyses of language samples. Self-reported measures may be the most feasible as they are generally the quickest and easiest to administer and score. Collateral input from CPs through informant-reported measures can provide useful information. For example, a discrepancy in ratings between patients and CPs may reflect reduced insight of the person with TBI or may help to identify areas of communication breakdown. A caveat in using self-reported measures is that expectancy bias can influence outcomes, such that patients who receive treatment may endorse improvements based on merely having received treatment rather than an actual change in communication ability.

Pragmatic rating scales, which often depend on recorded language samples, can be helpful in assessing aspects of communication not readily gleaned from self-reports, such as nonverbal behavior, appropriateness, participation, and elements of propositional and interactional communication. Ratings are inherently subjective, and pragmatic rules governing interactions can be specific to the individual’s social and cultural context. Thus, an understanding of the patient’s background is critical to the accuracy and validity of the ratings.

Fine-grained analyses of language samples are the most time consuming of the measurement approaches, but they also provide more objective information than self-reported measures or rating scales. Typically, these analyses require transcription of language samples and involve utterance by utterance scrutiny. These analyses can identify discrete deficits in specific aspects of communication that can help to focus treatment. Given the labor-intensive nature of fine-grained analyses, they are not widely used in the clinical setting.

Studies that included goal achievement measures consistently reported gains in goal completion (Finch et al., 2017; Harrison-Felix et al., 2018; Keegan et al., 2019). Although patient-centered goal setting and progress assessment can be done informally, use of a standardized process would produce more accurate and valid results (e.g., goal attainment scaling: Turner-Stokes & Williams, 2010). An advantage of using patient-centered goal setting is that it creates a sense of the patient’s accountability in treatment and incentivizes the patient toward goal achievement.

Distal measures (i.e., QoL/overall functioning, psychological distress, cognitive measures) are those that assess aspects of functioning and behavior beyond the target treatment behaviors and domain of communication, providing a way to evaluate the impact of cognitive-communication interventions on other domains and life activities. Studies that used QoL/functional measures generally found positive changes after communication treatment (Harrison-Felix et al., 2018; Rietdijk et al., 2019, 2020a, 2020b; Schneider & Van Auken, 2018; Whitworth et al., 2020). Similarly, psychological distress was generally observed to decrease following treatment, although changes were sometimes not evident until the maintenance phase (Douglas et al., 2014, 2019; Harrison-Felix et al., 2018), suggesting that clinicians wishing to use psychological distress measures may find it helpful to administer them not only immediately after treatment but also at follow-up. QoL/functional and psychological distress measures are typically self-reported and, as such, are prone to the same problems as self-reported measures, such as expectancy bias and questionable validity if insight is limited. Some caution is also warranted in interpreting changes in QoL and psychosocial functioning as the sole or direct result of communication treatment as these domains can be influenced by a number of factors (e.g., engagement with the clinician, medications, sleep).

Changes in cognitive performance were more variable across studies, even when using the same treatment approach. For example, participants with TBI demonstrated improvements in memory and executive functions in some CPT studies but not in others (Bosco et al., 2018; Gabbatore et al., 2015; Parola et al., 2019). Although cognitive assessments afford a more objective, performance-based approach to measuring change, the variability in treatment effects on these measures suggests that they may not be the most sensitive measures for evaluating change in discourse and social communication treatment.

Discussion

Deficiencies in discourse and social communication after TBI are chronic, disruptive to functioning in a number of life domains, and pervasive across severity levels, making evident the need for effective treatments to address these cognitive-communicative breakdowns. The aim of this systematic review was to evaluate the state of the evidence to provide guidance to clinicians who are seeking to treat discourse and social communication impairments in individuals with TBI. The aggregation and analysis of the treatment literature allowed for an examination of treatment efficacy across the spectrum of study design, treatment considerations for different levels of TBI severity, identification of key treatment components that may be important to include in cognitive-communicative intervention programs, and outcome measures that may be useful in examining change following treatment.

Efficacy of Discourse and Social Communication Interventions

Overall, the corpus of evidence suggests that discourse and social communication treatments can be effective in improving communication in people with TBI, which is consistent with findings from prior related systematic reviews (Finch et al., 2016; Steel et al., 2021). The evidence from social communication approaches was stronger as there were more studies of higher quality compared to those evaluating discourse approaches, which were all SCDs. Nevertheless, all treatment approaches demonstrated some benefit to individuals with TBI on at least one communication-related measure at the end of treatment. Therapeutic effects were highly variable across treatment approaches. Of the studies that provided maintenance phase data, the majority demonstrated maintenance of gains on at least one outcome measure.

Findings from the current systematic review are analogous to those from Finch et al. (2016) and Steel et al. (2021). In their review comparing context-sensitive and impairment-specific social communication treatment approaches, Finch et al. (2016) found both treatment types to be beneficial for people with TBI, although the quality of evidence was stronger for context-sensitive approaches. Similarly, both types of approaches examined in the current review (discourse and social communication) improved communication in people with TBI. Some caution is warranted in aligning discourse approaches as impairment specific and social communication approaches as context sensitive as individual treatment programs using either approach can be focused on impairments or contextualized. The current review also came to similar conclusions as Steel et al.’s review of narrative discourse interventions in identifying gains posttreatment but variability in generalization and maintenance of learned skills. An ongoing concern based on the current findings and noted in the prior systematic reviews and Meulenbroek et al.’s (2019) mapping review is the need for generalization of learned skills and incorporating treatment components that promote functional generalization. Consideration of TBI severity in evaluating candidacy for treatment and of treatment components that foster carryover of communication skills to functional contexts may provide insights into treatment selection and planning.

Promising Treatment Approaches for Different Levels of TBI Severity

Understanding which treatment approaches may be more efficacious for different severities of TBI may help clinicians to address the INCOG recommendation to tailor treatment to the individual’s needs and aligns with Meulenbroek et al.’s (2019) proposed treatment guideline to select intervention based on the individual’s ability. As the prior reviews did not specifically examine treatment approaches by severity, the current review offers novel, emerging clinical guidance on the topic.

In cases of mild-to-moderate TBI, the research evidence supported the use of discourse-oriented approaches, suggesting that people with mild-to-moderate TBI can potentially improve from treatment that addresses communication at multiple levels (e.g., word/sentence and global aspects of discourse), uses hierarchical training and structured training prompts, and engages the individual in understanding discrete aspects of their own communication. The findings also reflect that individual sessions were a viable treatment delivery format in mild-to-moderate TBI.

Social communication approaches in cases of mild-to-moderate TBI are less well studied, and thus, information regarding treatment effects for this clinical population is limited. As such, the efficacy of social communication approaches in mild-to-moderate TBI is an area of further study.

For moderate-to-severe TBI, social communication approaches offered promising preliminary results. In social communication treatment programs, such as TBIconneCT, MSI, and INSIGHT, the lack of change in functional communication despite improved discourse and social communication suggests that generalization of learned skills was not fully achieved or that perhaps individuals with moderate-to-severe TBI were unable to recognize the changes due to reduced awareness. Clinicians opting to implement these approaches may consider incorporating treatment components from studies that demonstrated durable gains in functional communication. Additionally, the use of informant-reported functional outcomes can provide further insights into the individual’s communicative functioning outside of the therapy setting. The aforementioned social communication approaches that had positive effects delivered treatment in dyadic or group sessions at least in part, suggesting that the use of groups may be a delivery format to consider in treating people with moderate-to-severe TBI. Clinicians who wish to provide telehealth may find TBIconneCT (Rietdijk et al., 2020a, 2020b) particularly noteworthy as treatment outcomes for telehealth were comparable to in-person.

Findings from social communication approaches (CPT, CommCope-I, and JOINT/SOLO) for severe TBI were variable, which may underscore a difference in the format of treatment delivery. The results from these social communication approaches as a whole suggest that the inclusion of the CP at all treatment sessions may have inhibited or reduced the participation of the individual with TBI, and people with severe TBI may benefit more from treatments in which they receive some sessions without their trained CPs. Based on the JOINT/SOLO outcomes, a potential limiting factor may be the alternating delivery formats involving groups, which may pose challenges for people with severe TBI in shifting and adapting from one setting to another.

Discourse-oriented approaches were less successful for people with severe TBI. Possible explanatory factors include the structured, hierarchical, and fine-grained nature of discourse treatment as too difficult for those with severe TBI and the lack of activities to support functional carryover in the discourse approaches. Discourse intervention may not be the most appropriate treatment avenue in cases of severe TBI. Clinicians should consider discourse-oriented treatment for this population cautiously and, if pursued, should integrate tasks to enhance generalization of skills to the individual’s personal context. As discourse treatment in severe TBI was only examined in SCDs, further exploration in this area would be advantageous to inform treatment development.

Promising Treatment Components in Addressing Discourse and Social Communication Impairments in TBI

Several treatment components identified in the current review overlap with those in prior reviews, suggesting a convergence in the “building blocks” for discourse and social communication intervention. The integration of essential components from both discourse and social communication treatment approaches made the case for five essential treatment components: feedback, simulated/actual social contexts, functional practice, training in metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies, and hierarchical training.

Of the 10 treatment components examined in this review, six were in common with or similar to those in Meulenbroek et al.’s (2019) review, although terminology may have differed, including the aforementioned five “building blocks” as well as cognitive training. Because six treatment studies were shared between the current review and Steel et al.’s (2021) review, similar treatment components were also identified, including training in metalinguistic/metacognitive strategies, hierarchical training, structured training prompts, and practice.

In the current review, a general trend was noted between treatment components of an approach and extent of therapeutic effects. Although there were exceptions, treatment approaches that incorporated more of the 10 identified treatment components tended to demonstrate more of an effect, whereas those with fewer components demonstrated minimal or small effects.

Beyond the essential treatment components, components that may further enhance generalization of learned skills should be considered. The three approaches (GIST/AT, CPT, and CommCope-I) that produced durable improvements in functional communication implemented either patient-centered goal setting or training in nonverbal behavior or both. Patient-centered goal setting may promote greater generalization by establishing the patient’s investment in the therapeutic process from the beginning and motivating the patient to implement skills and strategies to progress toward personal goals. Training in nonverbal behavior is often overlooked in communication treatment but critical to the success of social interactions. The ability to understand facial expressions, gestural cues, and body language in tandem with verbal communication treatment may further support the patient’s ability to manage interactions in their daily environments. There is also some preliminary evidence that cognitive training may support generalization of skills (Henderson et al., 2020), but further research in this area is needed as cognitive training was not consistently effective in improving communication or cognitive outcomes across studies.

Many of the treatment components identified across studies addressed several of the seven INCOG best practice guidelines for the management of CCDs. The five “building block” components identified pertained to INCOG recommendations (indicated by number) related to recognizing the individual’s personal context, tailoring treatment to the individual’s needs, and integrating contextually appropriate practice of learned skills and strategies (INCOG 1, 3, and 4). Implicit across all studies was delivery of treatment by a speech-language pathologist (INCOG 2).

Areas that merit addressing in the future pertain to the remaining INCOG recommendations. While a few studies focused on CP education and training (INCOG 5), most studies did not. While formal CP training may not be clinically indicated in every case, educating CPs may further support interactions with the individual with TBI functional carryover. It may also be helpful to teach individuals with TBI about how to educate their CPs regarding their communicative difficulties and advocate for themselves in their daily interactions. No studies in this review examined augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) as a potential treatment avenue (INCOG 6), but all participants, including those with severe TBI, were capable of verbal communication. In some cases of severe TBI, AAC may be particularly advantageous and necessary. Only six treatment approaches included patient-centered goal setting, and outcome measures did not consistently include functional communication across studies, suggesting the seventh INCOG recommendation is an area for future work.

Useful Measures for Measuring Change in Discourse and Social Communication for Individuals With TBI

Overall, proximal measures (discourse, pragmatic/social communication, functional communication, participant goal achievement) frequently captured change in following treatment as did some distal measures (QoL/functioning, psychological distress). However, the inconsistent change on cognitive measures suggests that these measures may have less clinical utility in measuring treatment effects than others. Individuals with TBI across a range of severity demonstrated the ability to improve aspects of their communication in the absence of improvements in cognitive ability. Thus, the extent of cognitive impairment may not be entirely predictive of therapeutic outcome. A possible alternative explanation could be that the discrepancy occurred because communication measures were ratings based and cognitive measures are performance based. However, in studies where communication measures were performance based, the dissonance between communication and cognitive outcomes was still apparent. Thus, for clinicians with limited time, other outcome measures are likely to be more fruitful in measuring change following treatment, although clinicians may wish to consider incorporating cognitive tasks to potentially enhance treatment effects.

Key factors in selection of outcome measures for an intervention are the ability of a measure to detect change in the target behavior and extent of generalization to functional situations. Based on these factors, clinicians should consider using at least two proximal measures, one to assess potential gains in the domain of the target behavior (e.g., presence of story grammar elements in a narrative) and one to evaluate functional carryover (e.g., relating a story that is understandable to a family member). If possible, distal measures may be included as ancillary assessments to gauge generalization to other life domains, such as psychosocial functioning and QoL. As an important clinical consideration, Meulenbroek et al. (2019) recommended measuring multiple aspects of performance, including accuracy, efficiency, and stability. Thus, a multi-faceted approach to assessment using multiple types of measures is likely to provide greater insights in the abilities and functioning of the individual over duration of treatment. In summary, the advantage of using a combination of measures is the capacity it affords in evaluating how successful treatment was within and beyond the therapy setting.

Although standardized discourse and social communication assessments are still very limited, there has been growth in structured, nonstandardized measures, such as questionnaires and pragmatic rating scales (see the work of Sohlberg et al., 2019, for a review of assessment tools). Nevertheless, the dissociation between clinical practice and research findings persists with clinicians often continuing to use discrete, impairment-focused measures (Sohlberg et al., 2019). These findings highlight the need to address the persistent gap in knowledge translation from the research literature to the clinical setting.

Limitations

There were several limitations to the current systematic review. Foremost among these is the early state of the cognitive-communication intervention research, which precluded the use of “gold standard” meta-analytic review that requires data from numerous large-scale randomized studies. Although this review took into account the INCOG recommendations and MacDonald (2017), an intervention framework was not formally implemented in the analysis of the treatment studies.

There were also a number of weaknesses in methodological design in the 21 reviewed studies as a group. There was only one RCT, and only six studies included control conditions. The majority of trials were cohort and SCD studies with small sample sizes that did not have control groups. Only the RCT was double-blinded, and blinding of any type was frequently missing from many of the studies, increasing the likelihood of bias. Weaknesses in outcome measurement were also noted, including omission of assessments to measure the target behavior or generalization, reliance on self-reported outcomes, and lack of maintenance data.

As noted in Finch et al.’s (2016) prior review, there is a possibility that some studies may have been missed, and there was no single scale that could be universally applied to evaluate the methodology of all studies, which would allow for more straightforward comparison. A notable difference from the prior review is the implementation of a rating scale to assess methodological quality in the current systematic review.

Conclusions and Future Directions

The current research in discourse and social communication interventions is promising for people with CCDs following TBI. This systematic review examined treatment efficacy of these interventions and identified treatment components that were likely foundational to an intervention in order to maximize gains in communicative ability and promote generalization. TBI severity and delivery format were noted as important clinical considerations in tailoring treatment development and shaping outcomes. This review also underscored how the selection of outcomes is critical to demonstrating change in the target behavior and to understanding how that change may translate to other life domains.

As evident in this and prior reviews, the field is continuing to advance in number, methodology, and scientific rigor of therapeutic approaches. More studies with greater methodological rigor, such as RCTs, are necessary to evaluate treatment efficacy of various approaches. Specific treatment approaches should be clearly delineated, and ideally manualized, to allow evaluation and replication by independent research groups. Greater specificity is needed in understanding which treatment approaches may be more suitable for specific patient populations (e.g., TBI severity, age, education, availability/willingness of CP to participate in treatment, underlying mental health factors). For studies that include varied samples of TBI, inclusion of subgroup analyses and/or examination of severity as a covariate would be helpful. Treatment approaches could also be further strengthened by more consistency in connecting the individual’s personal context throughout treatment and linking learned skills and strategies with how the individual with TBI manages real-world communicative situations and consequences. Such modifications to treatment approaches are likely to help clinicians in translating findings from treatment research and improve treatment implementation.

Theoretical frameworks and models that can be tested and guide intervention, providing the rationale for why a treatment works and assisting clinicians in adapting treatment to their patients, are important and valuable clinical resources. MacDonald’s (2017) model of cognitive-communicative competence is one such model, integrating social communication with personal context and multiple functional domains. The model stipulates that the more comprehensive treatment approaches can be in addressing these domains, the more likely they are to engender lasting improvements for people with CCDs following TBI. Such assertions merit empirical testing. As such, potentially promising areas of future research in cognitive-communicative interventions following TBI would be to test discourse and social communication models to inform treatment development and selection and to identify factors that would bridge the persistent gap in knowledge translation from the research literature to adopted clinical practice.
