Epithelial–myoepithelial carcinoma of the maxillofacial and sinonasal region: a systematic review of presenting characteristics, treatment modalities, and associated outcomes

Abstract

Epithelial–myoepithelial carcinoma (EMC) is a rare salivary gland malignancy. Controversy exists in the literature regarding the effectiveness of treatment modalities employed in the management of EMC. This systematic review was undertaken to understand the presenting characteristics of EMC and identify the most common treatment modalities and their associated outcomes, in order to help guide an evidenced-based approach to the algorithm of care. The MEDLINE (PubMed) and Embase databases were searched (up to February 23, 2022), and the review was performed in accordance with the PRISMA statement. Fifty-seven studies (51 case reports and six case series) describing 91 cases of EMC were included in this review. In the included studies, a slow-growing painless mass was the most common presenting clinical feature. EMC was most frequently treated with surgery alone (65%). Local disease recurrence occurred in 24% of the cases and metastatic disease in 11%. A positive surgical margin was found to be associated with a higher risk of recurrence (P < 0.001), while adjuvant radiotherapy was associated with a decreased risk of local disease recurrence (P = 0.034). Metastatic disease and multimodal therapy were found to be associated with decreased disease-free and overall survival (all P < 0.05). The current literature supports surgery with clear margins as the mainstay of treatment for EMC of the salivary and seromucous glands of the head and neck. In certain situations, radiotherapy may improve disease-free survival.

Epithelial–myoepithelial carcinoma (EMC) is a rare low-grade malignant tumour most frequently encountered in the parotid and to a lesser extent the submandibular gland and minor salivary glands of the maxillofacial and sinonasal region1–4. Rarely, cases of a similar histopathological entity have been reported in the lung,5 breast,6 vulvovaginal,7 and penile8 areas. These are considered to be separate entities. Salivary gland EMC is most common in individuals in their sixth decade of life1,4,5,9 and more commonly presents in females.1,2,4

Although the name for this neoplasm was first conceived in 1972 by Donath and colleagues,10 it was recognized in the literature as early as 1956, initially as a glycogen-rich clear cell adenoma.11 EMC continued to be identified under various other names until 1991, when it was accepted as a distinct pathological entity in the second World Health Organization histological classification of salivary gland tumours.12

Histologically, classic EMC is described as a biphasic salivary tumour with clear staining myoepithelial cells surrounding small lumina lined by ductal epithelial cells.1 Rarely, EMC with high-grade transformation,1,13 as well as other morphological variants such as oncocytic,14 sebaceous,15 and apocrine14,16 have been identified.

Owing to the rarity of this malignancy, rates of local recurrence and metastatic disease reported in the literature vary considerably from 26.7% to 50%1,9,17,18 and 4.5–35%,1,4,9,17,18 respectively. Survival rates of EMC, however, have been reported as relatively favourable among the salivary carcinoma subtypes, with 5-year and 10-year disease-specific survival of 93.5% and 81.8%,1 and 5-year and 10-year overall survival estimated at 91.3% and 90.2%.4

The optimal management strategy for EMC is unclear. There appears to be a general acceptance of surgical excision with clear margins forming the mainstay of treatment.1–3 However, the use of adjuvant therapies such as radiotherapy is controversial. A recent Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database analysis suggested that postoperative radiotherapy shows no additional benefit.4 In contrast, individual case reports have speculated that the use of radiotherapy is associated with good overall outcomes and provides a lower propensity for disease recurrence based on observation alone, with no comparative studies in the literature.19,20 There is no evidence regarding the use of chemotherapy in the management of EMC.

Due to the paucity of evidence, the current management of EMC of the head and neck is often based upon clinical experience, case reports and case series, as well as extrapolation from other low-grade salivary gland malignancies. This is not an uncommon situation when treating rare tumours, however it should not preclude efforts to at the very least collate and analyse the available evidence, which in many cases forms the highest level of evidence available for the management of rare pathologies. As such, this systematic review was designed to collate and analyse the current published literature in order to better understand the presenting characteristics of EMC and its prognosis, and to form an evidence-based approach to the algorithm of care.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement.21 The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) of the National Institute for Health and Care Research, UK (CRD42021232434).

Search strategy

A search of the MEDLINE (PubMed) and Embase databases was undertaken, with a final search completed on February 23, 2022. No restriction on publication year was applied. The search strategy was designed to capture all articles describing EMC of the head and neck under its current and previous names. The keywords and MeSH terms used to develop the search strategy were as follows: “Epithelial Myoepithelial carcinoma” OR “Clear cell carcinoma” OR “Glycogen rich Adenocarcinoma” OR “Glycogen rich Adenoma” OR “Glycogen rich Tumour” OR “Monomorphic clear cell tumour” OR “Glycogen-rich clear cell adenoma” AND “Paranasal Sinuses” OR “Salivary Glands” OR “Nasal Cavity” OR “Major salivary gland” OR “minor salivary gland” OR “Parotid gland” OR “sublingual gland” OR “submandibular gland” OR “Palate” OR “Paranasal Sinuses”[Mesh] OR “Salivary Glands, Minor”[Mesh] OR “Salivary Glands” [Mesh] OR “Nasal Cavity”[Mesh]. A manual bibliography search of articles identified via the primary search was completed and additional papers were added.

Eligibility criteria

Results were limited to human subjects and articles in English describing cases of EMC diagnosed in the maxillofacial and/or sinonasal region. To be included in the review, identified articles required a clear description of the treatment provided as well as associated outcomes. Articles that did not include explicit information pertaining to exact treatment modalities, surgical margin status, and outcomes were excluded. Furthermore, cases of EMC involving the lacrimal gland or tissues of the hypopharynx were also excluded from the study.

Study selection and data extraction

Titles and abstracts from the initial search were independently assessed by two reviewers (RSW and SSS). The full texts of all potentially eligible articles were then evaluated in detail by the two authors to identify those that conformed to the inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved in a consensus meeting between the two authors.

If an article was missing only one variable to meet the inclusion criteria, the corresponding author was contacted; if the necessary data were provided, the study was included in the review. Data on the following parameters were then collected, tabulated, and analysed: study type, author, publication year, sample size (where applicable), patient demographics and pre-intervention clinical findings (symptoms and clinical presentation), anatomical location of EMC, diagnostic biopsy information, treatment provided (single or multimodality, which included surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy), surgical margin status (positive or negative), metastasis, local recurrence, follow-up time, and status at recent follow-up.

Quality assessment

To assess the quality of included articles, the authors utilized the Joanna Briggs Institute 2017 critical appraisal checklists for case reports and case series (https://jbi.global/sites/default/files/2019-05/JBI_Critical_Appraisal-Checklist_for_Case_Reports2017_0.pdf; https://jbi.-global/sites/default/files/2019-05/JBI_Critical_Appraisal-Checklist_for_Case_Series2017_0.pdf). Case reports were assessed independently against an eight-point checklist, while case series were assessed against a 10-point checklist. The individual specific parameters are outlined in detail in Supplementary Material Figs. S1 and S2. Each parameter was scored as yes (Y), no (N), not applicable (NA), or data not stipulated in original article but retrieved from contacting the author (AC).

Data analysis and statistics

Descriptive summaries including treatment modalities, disease-free survival (DFS), and overall survival (OS) are presented as counts and percentages, or as the median and range. Oncology interventions listed in the included studies were surgery only, radiation only, surgery + radiation, radiation + chemotherapy, and surgery + radiation + chemotherapy. These were grouped into surgery only and combination treatment for the regression analysis. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for the pooled treatment outcomes DFS and OS.22–24 The Cox model had a reduced sample size (87 rather than 91), because of missing data for time-to-event (follow-up). Predictors analysed included treatment, metastasis, sex, age categories, and site of infection. The data analysis was conducted using Stata version 17.0, 2021 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Inference was based on a 5% level of significance.

Results

Search results and study selection

The search of the MEDLINE (PubMed) and Embase electronic databases identified 906 references (Fig. 1). Following the removal of 276 duplicates and the addition of 24 studies identified through a manual bibliography search, the titles and abstracts of the remaining 654 papers were screened. From these, a total of 191 full-text articles were evaluated, of which 134 were excluded, leaving a total of 57 studies meeting the eligibility criteria. These studies were included in the systematic review.

Description of individual studies

Of the 57 articles included in the review, 51 were case reports and six were case series, describing a total of 91 cases of EMC. Initially, seven of the studies did not meet the inclusion criteria due to a single piece of missing information.1,25–30 The authors of these studies were contacted and they were able to provide additional information thus making their studies eligible for inclusion. Included studies were published between 1986 and 2021 and originated from 20 different countries. Supplementary Material Table S1 presents the individual characteristics of each included study.

Patient demographics

Individual patient and clinical characteristics are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Patients were aged between 6 and 103 years, with a median age of 63.5 years; 47% of the patients were aged ≥ 65 years, while only 20% were aged < 44 years. Of the 91 patient cases identified and included in this review, 53 were male (58%) and 38 were female (42%), giving a male to female ratio for these reported cases of 1.4:1.

Anatomical location, clinical features, and biopsy findings

In the included studies, EMC was reported in six different anatomical locations. The parotid gland (n = 49; 54%) was the most common site, followed by the minor salivary glands of the oral cavity (n = 19; 21%), seromucous glands of the sinonasal region (n = 10; 11%), minor salivary glands of the oropharynx (n = 5; 5%), the submandibular gland (n = 5; 5%), and minor salivary glands of the nasopharynx (n = 3; 3%). Multiple involvement was presented in three cases: two cases had synchronous bilateral EMC of the parotid glands26,31 and one presented bilateral EMC of the sinonasal region.32

Ninety presenting symptoms were reported in 65 patients, the most common being a slow-growing painless mass, identified in 72% (n = 47) of cases, with a median time between first recognition and diagnosis of 21 months. Local pain, rapid lesion growth, and facial nerve weakness were reported in 15%, 12%, and 3% of cases, respectively. All presenting symptoms are outlined in Table 3.

Pre-intervention biopsy information was available for 56 cases, 38 of which received a biopsy and 18 did not. Of the patients who received a biopsy before their definitive treatment, 16 had fine needle aspiration biopsies (FNAB) and 22 had open biopsies (17 incision, five excision). Of the FNABs, 38% (n = 6) returned a false-negative diagnosis of benign pathology, including pleomorphic adenoma (n = 3), or a report showing no suspicion of malignancy (n = 3). In contrast, only 18% (n = 4) of the open biopsies returned a false-negative report.

Histology

Information pertaining to the morphological subtypes of EMC was outlined in 86 cases, with the classic EMC histology reported in 77% (n = 66). This was followed by EMC ex-pleomorphic adenoma (n = 6; 7%), sebaceous (n = 3; 3%), oncocytic (n = 2; 2%), oncocytic/sebaceous (n = 2; 2%), and apocrine (n = 1; 1%). Histopathological grading of the tumours identified that four cases (5%) were high-grade tumours. The presence or absence of perineural invasion in the histological specimen was specifically reported in 35 cases, with 16 (46%) specimens showing evidence of perineural invasion. Additionally, 32 cases commented specifically on the presence or absence of lymphovascular invasion, with the specimens in 13 (41%) cases showing evidence of lymphovascular invasion.

Treatment modalities, follow-up, and patient outcomes

The treatment provided was reported in all cases and involved various combinations of surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy; refusal of intervention was also reported. Surgery formed the most common treatment modality, with 65% (n = 59) of patients receiving surgery only. The next most common modality of treatment was surgery and postoperative radiotherapy (n = 22; 24%), followed by surgery and postoperative chemoradiotherapy (n = 7; 8%), concurrent chemoradiotherapy without surgery (n = 1; 1%), and definitive radiotherapy without surgery (n = 1; 1%). One case did not receive any treatment at the patient’s request. Of the patients who received radiotherapy, the dose received ranged from 50 Gy to 70.70 Gy.

Of the 88 patients who had surgery as part of their treatment, 67 had the specific type of surgical procedure stated. In total there were 70 primary surgical procedures completed in 67 patients. A total of 40 specified surgical procedures involving the parotid or submandibular glands were performed on 38 patients (two patients had synchronous bilateral EMC of the parotid glands with both glands in both patients being surgically removed), with the most common being a superficial parotidectomy, carried out in 35% (n = 14) of these cases. A total of 12 specified surgical procedures were performed on 11 patients with an EMC of the sinonasal region (one patient had a bilateral EMC of the sinonasal region), with the most common procedure being endoscopic resection, carried out in 33% (n = 4) of these cases. A total of 18 specified surgical procedures were performed on 18 patients with an EMC of a minor salivary gland of the oropharynx or oral cavity, with the most common being a wide local excision, carried out in 83% (n = 15) of these cases. All surgical procedures are outlined in Table 4.

Of the 88 patients who received surgery as part of their treatment, 30% (n = 26) were reported to have a positive surgical margin, with the remaining cases having negative margins. Surprisingly, a positive margin was not automatically an indication for radiation, with only 11 of the 26 cases with positive margins receiving radiotherapy in the postoperative setting.

Information pertaining to neck dissection was specified in 66 cases. Of these 66 patients, 14 (21%) underwent neck dissection: nine of 14 (64%) were elective neck dissections and five of 14 (36%) were therapeutic neck dissections.

Twenty-five percent (n = 22) of patients suffered local recurrence, with a median time of 22 months between the treatment of the primary disease and diagnosis of recurrence.

Information about metastasis was available for all of the patients: 10 (11%) suffered from metastatic disease, of whom two presented with metastasis at first diagnosis and the remaining eight developed metastasis following primary treatment, with a median time of 22 months. The lung (n = 7; 41%) was the most common site, followed by the cervical lymph nodes (n = 4; 24%), brain (n = 2; 12%), bone (n = 2; 12%), kidney (n = 1; 6%), and then liver (n = 1; 6%).

Status at the most recent follow-up was described in all cases. However, two articles published their findings immediately following the primary treatment and therefore did not have any information on the outcomes past this point,33,34 and two articles outlined the status at most recent follow-up but did not specify the time frame of the follow-up.35,36 The median follow-up time was 24 months (range 2–362 months). The majority of patients in this review (n = 72; 79%) were alive without evidence of disease at their most recent follow-up. Thirteen patients had died, with six (7%) of these dying from the disease and seven (8%) dying of other causes. The remaining six (7%) patients were alive with the disease at their most recent follow-up.

Treatment modalities and associated risk of local recurrence

Table 5 presents the association of treatment modalities with risk of recurrence. Patients with a positive margin were more likely to have a recurrence compared to those with negative margins (60% vs 11%; χ2(1) = 21.047, P < 0.001).

Likewise, on stratifying for positive margin, patients given radiotherapy were less likely to have a recurrence compared to those without radiotherapy (30% vs 80%; P = 0.034).

Survival

Survival was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier survival function, with the 2-year DFS being 89% (95% CI 77–95%) and the 5-year DFS being 76% (95% CI 54–85%). The 2-year OS was 97% (95% CI 86–99%) and the 5-year OS was 85% (95% CI 66–94%).

The associations between the risk factors and treatment received and DFS and OS is presented in Table 6. Relative to metastasis, non-metastatic disease was associated with a lower hazard ratio and hence longer DFS (HR 0.07, 95% CI 0.02–0.22; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A) and OS (HR 0.02, 95% CI 0.00–0.21; P = 0.001) (Fig. 2B). The use of combined treatment (surgery, radiation, and/or chemotherapy) was associated with a higher hazard ratio and hence reduced DFS (HR 3.59, 95% CI 1.05–12.26; P = 0.041) (Fig. 3A) and OS (HR 11.07, 95% CI 1.19–102.67; P = 0.034) (Fig. 3B).

Quality of the included studies

The Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools for case reports and case series measures for each study are summarized in Supplementary Material Figs. S1 and S2. No single study appraised failed to fulfil > 50% of the criteria. All studies provided a clear description of the demographic characteristics, and most of the studies outlined diagnostics tests, interventions, and post interventional conditions clearly. Therefore, the authors were satisfied that the quality of the included studies was generally high.

Discussion

In this systematic review, the presenting characteristics, treatment modalities and the associated outcomes for patients with EMC of the major and minor salivary and seromucous glands in the maxillofacial and sinonasal region were investigated. Owing to the rare nature of this pathology, the low numbers in this review made it difficult to generate sufficient evidence to determine optimal management. Additionally, the lack of specific information in many studies meant that a reasonable number of published cases of EMC could not be included, further limiting the already small sample size and therefore the review findings. Nonetheless, this review represents a comprehensive systematic assessment of the best available evidence published on this entity in the worldwide literature.

With regard to the anatomical distribution and median age at diagnosis, the present results are in accordance with those of previous studies, which have reported EMC as being most frequently diagnosed in individuals in their sixth decade of life and to affect the parotid gland more than any other gland or anatomical location.1–4,9,17 Although the previous literature has reported that EMC presents more commonly in females than males,1,2,4,9,17 the male to female ratio in the present review was 1.4:1, with more male cases than female cases reported in the included articles.

The most common clinical finding at the time of diagnosis was a painless mass that had been present for just under 2 years before the patient sought treatment. Other symptoms were reported, but these were uncommon, indicating that EMC when present in the maxillofacial and sinonasal region is frequently asymptomatic and indolent in nature. This can be assumed to be a reflection of the low-grade characteristics of EMC, and is consistent with the behaviour of other low-grade salivary gland malignancies such as acinic cell carcinoma.37 Another consideration in the context of a prolonged painless swelling, is that EMC may arise from pleomorphic adenoma (carcinoma ex) more frequently than has been identified in this review. It was found that 7% of cases were histologically confirmed to have arisen from a pre-existing pleomorphic adenoma. In contrast, a recent study suggested that up to 80% of EMC of the salivary glands will arise from a pre-existing pleomorphic adenoma.38 The inherent pleomorphism of many salivary gland tumours contributes to the concept of carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma being a somewhat controversial issue. Progressive transition to genetic marker identification may allow this situation to evolve. EMC with high-grade transformation, which was previously known as dedifferentiated EMC with regards to either the myoepithelial component (myoepithelial anaplasia) or the ductal component (high-grade carcinoma transformation), has been observed and reported in the literature.1,39,40 This is significant, as a histological diagnosis of EMC with high-grade transformation in addition to other high-grade features, such as necrosis, has been suggested to lead to worse outcomes in terms of recurrence, metastasis, and survival.39,41 The present review was not able to identify any trends with regards to histological grade and outcome, largely due to the restricted sample size.

Of the patients who received a biopsy as part of their diagnostic workup, almost three quarters (71%) returned a true-positive diagnosis for either EMC or a suspicion of malignancy. However, when divided into FNAB versus open biopsies, FNAB results delivered a false-negative diagnosis for benign pathology in 38% of cases. This is in keeping with the findings of Daneshbod et al.,42 who performed a retrospective audit and identified that FNAB on EMC had one of the highest rates of false-negative diagnosis (50%) among all salivary gland neoplasms. Fortunately, even in cases where false-negatives occurred, all patients in the included studies went on to have definitive surgical excision.

Due to the rare nature of this pathology, the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) classification of salivary gland malignancy is utilized, and forms a valuable descriptive and universally understood staging system. The literature suggests that ultrasound is useful in the initial diagnostic workup of patients with superficial parotid and submandibular swellings,43,44 as well as in conjunction with fine needle aspiration cytology (ultrasound-guided FNAC), despite its aforementioned limitations.44,45 Additionally, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been identified as the most appropriate imaging technique for mapping of the primary malignant lesion43 and assessing local invasion,44 and is sensitive for perineural spread.44 In the present review, the most common region for metastasis to occur was the lungs and to a lesser extent the liver, which is in keeping with other salivary gland malignancies.46,47 Therefore computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen should be employed in initial staging. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG PET)–CT appears to be the most sensitive imaging technique for the assessment of distant metastases and detection of disease recurrence.43 However, its use has restrictions, with evidence suggesting that it may show limited discrimination between benign and malignant salivary gland tumours,48 as well as the fact that low-grade salivary gland malignancies have a low avidity for FDG.49 Therefore, some authors have recommended against its use in the staging of low-grade salivary gland neoplasms,43 and in the case of early-stage non-recurrent disease, the present authors would not advocate the routine use of FDG PET–CT scanning for staging.

This systematic review supports the conclusion that the excision of EMC with clear margins should form the mainstay of treatment.1–3,50,51 Whilst positive margins are the greatest predictor of local recurrence, they were not an automatic indication for adjuvant therapies and they do not have a clear association with overall survival. However, the converse is observed across other salivary gland malignancies, with a positive margin being accepted as an indication for postoperative radiotherapy52,53 and generally associated with a decrease in overall survival.54–56 Consequently it may be the case that due to the overall small sample size, meaningful conclusions about the benefit of postoperative radiotherapy on overall survival cannot be made with certainty. Decisions about the impact of the overall survival benefit of postoperative radiotherapy should be made on a case by case basis with this in mind. As such, the authors would recommend aggressive local surgery to obtain clear margins with careful consideration given to preservation of structures of functional significance. The balance between disease control and survival benefit and sacrifice resulting in unacceptable morbidity is a surgical decision that should be confirmed by careful surgical tumour board discussion.

Most cases of EMC were in the parotid gland site. Particular consideration therefore needs to be given to the ability to obtain clear cancer margins whilst offsetting the functional morbidity associated with facial nerve sacrifice. In this review, 35 surgical procedures involving an EMC of the parotid gland were specified, with 31 being superficial/nerve-sparing and four being radical. These numbers are too low to suggest superiority of one approach over the other, and management decisions were made on the basis of clinical and imaging findings in tumour workup. It is accepted that in the case of malignant salivary gland tumours of the parotid in which the facial nerve is functional preoperatively and the tumour is restricted to the superficial lobe, there is no radiological infiltration into the facial nerve by the tumour, and there is an absence of pain, then a facial nerve-sparing procedure should be performed.44,57,58 Thus for EMC in the parotid, this would represent a nerve-sparing parotidectomy with sacrifice only in the context of a non-functioning nerve preoperatively or radiological or intraoperative assessment of direct tumour invasion into the facial nerve. This analysis of all of the available literature on EMC supports this approach.

For tumours in the submandibular gland, there is insufficient evidence to suggest compartmental resection or localized removal of the gland. However logically, if the tumour appears to have infiltrated beyond the capsule of the gland based on MRI findings, then a compartmental resection may be indicated. For the oral cavity and oropharynx, wide local excision with a macroscopic 1-cm margin (aiming for > 5 mm microscopic clearance) should be the goal of surgery, which would include a composite resection if required. Sinonasal disease is most often treated endoscopically where en bloc resection and 1-cm margins are not feasible. In this setting every attempt to clear macroscopic and microscopic disease, with formal histopathological assessment, should be made.

Radiotherapy forms the mainstay of adjuvant treatment for the majority of salivary gland malignancies and has been associated with improved survival.59,60 The role of radiotherapy as a treatment strategy for EMC is controversial, with the largest population-based survival analyses of salivary gland EMC suggesting that radiotherapy confers no survival advantage.4 These findings were not statistically significant, however, and the authors outlined that conclusions on the matter could not be definitively drawn. In contrast, various case reports have shown good individual responses to radiotherapy when used in the adjuvant setting,61,62 or as part of definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy.20 This systematic review has now offered further insight into this ongoing debate, with lower rates of local disease recurrence observed when adjuvant radiotherapy was delivered in the context of positive surgical margins. Outside of the setting of positive surgical margins, other authors have suggested that radiotherapy should be considered for advanced T-stage tumours, extra-parenchymal extension, aggressive histological features (such as perineural invasion and/or lymphovascular invasion), and local lymph node metastasis for all low-grade salivary gland malignancies.63,64 These recommendations are in accordance with the American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines for the management of salivary gland malignancies,53 as well as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Head and Neck Cancer, version 1.2022 guidelines.65 Therefore, the authors of this review suggest that the basis of treatment for EMC should still rely on adequate surgical clearance of the primary tumour, and radiotherapy should be offered to those with positive surgical margins. Additionally, radiotherapy should be considered in cases of advanced primary disease, aggressive histological features, and local metastatic spread to the cervical lymph nodes on the basis of extrapolation from other low-grade salivary gland malignancies.

The number of patients in the present review who received chemotherapy was too low to comment on this treatment strategy independently with any great confidence. As such there is still no proven role for the value of chemotherapy in the management of EMC. As molecular analysis becomes more sophisticated, the role of novel systemic therapies, such as the use of trastuzumab (Herceptin) in the case of salivary ductal carcinoma,66,67 may become evident. For now, systemic therapy should be reserved for advanced stage disease with incomplete surgical resection or for cases where the patient is not a surgical candidate. However the evidence for this is non-existent and once again based on extrapolation rather than disease-specific evidence.

Combined treatment that included surgery plus radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy was associated with a poorer DFS and OS. The authors consider that this is due to these therapies being reserved for patients with advanced primary and/or metastatic disease, which have a poor prognosis. No comparative data were available.

In cases where the tumour is clearly determined to involve the regional lymph nodes, then a therapeutic neck dissection is recommended. The radiosensitivity of EMC remains unclear for any individual case orientation. There has been much debate in the literature regarding the role of elective neck dissection for salivary gland disease with a clinically negative neck. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that elective neck dissection is recommended for patients with a high-grade histology or advanced T-classification. For a cN0 salivary gland malignancy with low grade histology and T1/T2 classification that has been resected with free surgical margins, observation might be acceptable.68 The authors suggest that a selective neck dissection should be considered in cases of EMC with an advanced T-classification, high-grade transformation, or cases where reconstruction and thus neck access is required. In cases where the primary is small, an informed discussion with the patient should take place with the full knowledge that the decision to treat the neck electively is based on evidence extrapolated from other pathological entities and the risk and benefits must be considered on a case by case basis.

The median time to local recurrence and/or metastatic disease diagnosed in the patients treated for EMC in this review was 22 months. As such, the first 2 years post-treatment represent the most critical period during which the patient should be reviewed and physically examined on a regular basis. Following this, 6-monthly review until 5 years post-treatment would be acceptable. Following this initial 5 years, annual follow-up can be offered to the patient. This follow-up protocol is in accordance with current NCCN recommendations for the surveillance of upper aerodigestive tract squamous cell carcinomas,65 which, broadly speaking, salivary gland malignancies follow.53 Additionally, post-treatment baseline imaging with contrast CT or MRI of the primary site should be obtained 3 months postoperatively, as well as follow-up surveillance imaging every 6 months for the first 2 years. Imaging thereafter should be guided by clinical findings and symptoms.53

This systematic review had several limitations, most of which are a result of the rarity of EMC. Due to the confusion over the nomenclature used for EMC up until 1991, the authors cannot be certain that all cases under previous names were found and included. The strict inclusion criteria limited the number of articles entered into the study. Publication bias of the included reports could have occurred, which may have led to overrepresentation of positive and/or negative outcomes.

In conclusion, historically, the relatively low numbers of many salivary gland malignancies and repeated reclassification has meant that treatment has been based on anecdotal evidence, clinical experience, and extrapolation. This has resulted in a heterogeneous approach to the management of rare malignancies such as EMC and thus variations in outcomes. This systematic review of EMC of the head and neck is novel. Whilst the intrinsic limitations of this study are acknowledged, such as the small sample size limiting the ability to perform a robust statistical analysis over multiple variables, it was possible to demonstrate the presentation of this disease, the benefit of surgery achieving clear surgical margins as the first-line of treatment, the utility of adjuvant radiotherapy in the setting of positive margins to obtain loco-regional control, and the disease-free and overall survival rates.
