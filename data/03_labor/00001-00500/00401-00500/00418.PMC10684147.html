Comparing the efficacy and safety of short-term spinal cord stimulation and pulsed radiofrequency for zoster-related pain:

A systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Background:

Pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) is a commonly used method for the treatment of zoster-related pain in the clinic. However, PRF therapy has a high recurrence rate and many adverse reactions. Recent studies have shown that short-term spinal cord stimulation (stSCS) can effectively alleviate zoster-related pain. Due to the lack of evidence, it is unclear whether stSCS is superior to PRF in the efficacy of treating zoster-related pain.

Objective:

This study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of stSCS and PRF for zoster-related pain.

Methods:

We searched seven electronic databases from the establishment of the database to January 2021. Related randomized controlled trials were included in this meta-analysis. After extracting the data and evaluating the methodological quality of the included trials, the outcome indicators were statistically analyzed by using RevManV.5.3.

Results:

This meta-analysis included 6 trials with a total of 509 patients. Compared with PRF group, stSCS group showed lower pain intensity (standardized mean difference=-0.83, 95%CI [-1.37, -0.30], P=.002), better sleep quality (mean difference=-1.43, 95%CI [-2.29, -0.57], P=.001), lower pain rating index scores, and less incidence of adverse events (RR=0.32, 95%CI [0.12, 0.83], P<.05). However, the efficacies of PRF and stSCS for treating postherpetic neuralgia were consistent in the response rate (RR= 1.10, 95% CI [0.82, 1.48], P=.51) and the complete remission rate (RR=1.05, 95% CI [0.66, 1.68], P=.84).

Conclusions:

In this study, stSCS showed a better analgesic effect and higher safety than PRF. Our meta-analysis results suggested that stSCS may be a feasible and safe invasive treatment for zoster-related pain. However, high-quality, randomized controlled trials with large sample sizes are needed to further verify our conclusions.

1. Introduction

Herpes zoster is a common acute skin disease caused by the activation of varicella-zoster virus that leaves latent infections in the dorsal root ganglion or cranial nerve ganglion.[1] Herpes zoster usually presents as a self-limited and banded skin rash distributed along the nerve. Although herpes zoster is a self-limiting disease, zoster-related pain may last for several months, even after the rash has healed. According to different courses of the disease, pain persisting within 1month after rash onset is defined as acute herpetic neuralgia, pain persisting from 1 to 3 months after rash onset is defined as subacute herpetic neuralgia, and pain persisting more than 3months is defined as postherpetic neuralgia. Postherpetic neuralgia is the most common complication of herpes zoster, which is defined as neuropathic pain that lasts for more than three months after rash onset.[2] The incidence of postherpetic neuralgia is high, especially in elderly individuals or patients with severe acute pain or serious skin rash.[3] Postherpetic neuralgia usually presents the following major types of pain: (1) allodynia and hyperalgesia in response to mechanical and thermal stimulation and (2) paroxysmal or persistent spontaneous pain (often described as burning, stabbing, or like electric shock).[4] Due to the obstinacy of postherpetic neuralgia, postherpetic neuralgia often has a great negative impact on the patient’s quality of life, physiological state, and mental state.[5,6]

Management of postherpetic neuralgia is challenging, and there is no effective treatment. At present, drug therapy is still the most basic and primary treatment for postherpetic neuralgia. However, for patients with refractory postherpetic neuralgia, drug therapy does not alleviate the patients’ pain effectively and even causes serious adverse reactions in patients. Some interventional treatments are proposed to address zoster-related pain to prevent postherpetic neuralgia, such as epidural block or sympathetic block. However, some studies reported that a single epidural injection and sympathetic nerve block could not effectively prevent postherpetic neuralgia.[7] Neuromodulations, including pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) and spinal cord electrical stimulation (SCS), are increasingly used to prevent or treat postherpetic neuralgia.[8]

PRF is a type of neuromodulation that delivers short bursts of high voltage to the target nerve, followed by a silent phase that allows heat to disseminate and keeps the target tissue temperature below 42°C.[9] Since Sluijter first successfully applied PRF in the clinic, PRF has been rapidly promoted worldwide.[10] To date, pulsed radiofrequency has been widely used in the treatment of intractable neuropathic pain, such as trigeminal neuralgia,[11] occipital neuralgia[12] and postherpetic neuralgia.[13] In addition, some studies have reported that PRF can also be used to prevent postherpetic neuralgia. Kim et al[14] conducted a retrospective comparative study and found that PRF was more effective in treating acute herpetic neuralgia and preventing postherpetic neuralgia than continuous epidural block. Although there is substantial evidence showing that PRF is effective in treating zoster-related pain, there are still some problems. First, the mechanism of PRF is not completely clear. At the moment, most studies point to changes in synaptic transmission.[15] However, there is no strong evidence to support these views. Second, the overall effective rate of pulsed radiofrequency in the treatment of neuropathic pain is relatively low, and the recurrence rate is high. Finally, PRF may cause some adverse reactions or complications, including drowsiness, dizziness, local reactions, headache, nausea and vomiting, and transient bradycardia.[16,17]

Conventional SCS is also a neuromodulation, which evokes paranesthesia in the area of pain to relieve pain by transmitting electrical pulses to the dorsal columns.[18] In 1967, Shealy et al[19] successfully relieved the cancer pain of patients by using SCS. Later, SCS was gradually used to treat chronic pain with the development of SCS technology. At present, the main indications of SCS include failed back surgery syndrome, complex regional pain syndrome and angina pectoris.[20] In addition, several studies have reported that SCS effectively treats neuropathic pain, including postherpetic neuralgia.[21] SCS therapy includes shortterm spinal cord stimulation (stSCS) and permanent spinal cord stimulation. It is necessary to test the effect of the temporary electrodes in the preliminary experiment before the permanent electrodes are implanted. If temporary SCS is considered effective (the patient’s pain is relieved by more than 50% under a normal activity level and normal use of painkillers), permanent SCS can be implanted a few weeks later.[22] Although permanent SCS can maintain a long-term analgesic effect for postherpetic neuralgia, its application is limited by difficult patient selection and high cost. On the contrary, stSCS is increasingly valued because it has some advantages, such as simplicity, economy and efficiency. To date, some clinical studies have shown that stSCS can be used to treat zoster-related pain in different courses of disease effectively.[23-25] In addition, a literature review showed that 77.8% of patients with postherpetic neuralgia treated with stSCS received pain relief, with an average pain relief of 57.0%.[26]

Recently, some studies have compared the efficacy of stSCS and PRF for zoster-related pain. However, there is still no clear conclusion. The advantages and safety of stSCS in the treatment of postherpetic neuralgia are still unclear. Therefore, we carried out this systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the efficacy and safety of stSCS and PRF in treating zoster-related pain and explored the potential advantages of stSCS for zoster-related pain.

2. Methods

The protocol for the meta-analysis is registered with PROSPERO (CRD42021269598).

2.1. Search strategy

This meta-analysis included Chinese and English literature. The following electronic databases were searched from their establishment to January 2021: the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Chinese Biomedical Database (CBM), Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang database and Chinese Science and Technique Journals Database. To expand the search scope, our search formula mainly included three search terms. The first search term was ‘postherpetic neuralgia’, ‘neuralgia, postherpetic’, ‘PHN’, ‘postherpetic neuralgia’, ‘herpes zoster’, ‘herpetic neuralgia’, ‘zoster-related pain’ or ‘zoster-related neuralgia’. The second search term was ‘spinal cord stimulation’, ‘cord stimulation, spinal’, ‘stimulation, spinal cord’, ‘spinal cord electrical stimulation’ or ‘SCS’. The third search term was ‘pulsed radiofrequency’ or ‘pulsed neuromodulatory’ or ‘PRF’ or ‘pulsed’ or ‘radiofrequency’. We searched the databases with combinations of the three terms. The corresponding Chinese search terms were also searched. In addition, we also checked the references of all relevant literature to ensure that the search was comprehensive.

2.2. Study selection

Search results from the electronic databases were imported into Endnote X9 for study selection. Two researchers independently performed literature screening according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Differences were resolved through discussion or consultation with third parties. We applied the following inclusion criteria: (1) randomized controlled trials (RCTs)/quasirandomized controlled trials (quasi-RCTs); (2) a diagnosis of zoster-related pain; (3) patients agreed to be treated with stSCS or PRF; (4) the included studies had outcomes assessing efficacy or safety. We excluded studies for the following reasons: (1) case series with a sample size smaller than 10 patients; (2) conference papers, case reports, clinical guidelines, reviews and animal experiments; (3) unavailable data; and (4) repetitive studies.

2.3. Outcome measures

We used the following outcome measures to compare the efficacy and safety of stSCS and PRF for postherpetic neuralgia. The primary outcome includes pain intensity measured by numeric rating scale or visual analog scale (VAS) and adverse events. The secondary outcomes include sleep quality, response rate, complete remission rate, pain rating index (PRI) scores, inflammation levels, and the use of analgesics and calcium channel antagonists. If the definitions of the outcomes are different, we classify the author’s original results according to an explicit criterion.

2.4. Data extraction and quality assessment

Two researchers independently extracted the data and assessed the methodological quality of the included trials. The differences were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third party to reach consensus. We extracted data from all included trials according to the defined criteria. If the required data were lost, we contacted the corresponding author of the trial. The data we extracted were as follows: first author, year of publication, intervention, inclusion criteria, herpes-involved area, sample size, patient sex and age, patient mean course of disease, duration of neuromodulation, neuromodulation parameters, baseline scores and follow-up time points. The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool was used to assess the methodological quality of the included trials, such as the following aspects: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting and other sources of bias.

2.5. Statistical analysis

We used the Review Manager statistical software (RevManV.5.3) to conduct a meta-analysis of relevant outcomes. When outcome measures were dichotomous data, we used relative risk (RR) to perform quantitative analysis. When outcome measures were continuous data, we used mean difference (MD). If the measurement scales were different, the standardized mean difference (SMD) values were used instead of MD. Continuous data included pain intensity, sleep quality, pain rating index scores, inflammation levels and the use of analgesics and calcium channel antagonists, while dichotomous data included response rate, complete remission rate and adverse events. The chi-square test and the Higgins I2 test were used to assess heterogeneity. If I2 ≤50% and the P value ≥.10, the fixedeffect model or the random-effect model was used for the meta-analysis. Otherwise, only a random-effect model was used. Moreover, if the heterogeneity was high, the subgroup analysis was carried out to find the causes of heterogeneity, and we performed a sensitivity analysis to ensure that the meta-analysis results were relatively stable. For the data that could not be merged, the descriptive analysis was performed. P<.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Literature screening

A total of 286 studies were identified after we searched seven databases and other sources. After discarding duplicate studies and screening the titles and abstracts, 276 articles were excluded. The full-text articles for the remaining titles were then screened based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. After complete evaluation, 6 studies were included in the final analysis. Only one article[31] was published in English, and the other five articles[27-30,32] were published in Chinese (Fig. 1).

3.3. Risk of bias

Because the six trials were all RCTs, we used the Cochrane risk of bias tool to assess the methodological quality of the included trials. Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 show the results of the bias assessment of the included trials. In terms of random sequence generation, four trials[27,30-32] reported that patients were grouped by a random digital table method, one study[28] did not report a specific grouping method, and one study[29] reported an inappropriate method of random sequence generation so that allocation concealment could not be achieved. Because patients in the experimental group and the control group received interventional therapy, it was impossible to blind personnel. However, all patients received interventions based on standard parameters. We thought the performance bias was unclear in affecting the results in all trials. Five trials[27,28,30-32] did not mention allocation concealment. None of the included studies reported blinding the outcome assessment. One trial[31] reported three patients lost to follow-up, and no dropouts were reported in the other five trials. We found no selective reporting or other bias in our included trials.

3.4. Efficacy

3.4.1. Pain intensity. A total of six trials[27-32] reported pain intensity, which was measured by VAS or numeric rating scale at the end of follow-up. The meta-analysis of pain intensity showed that stSCS was more effective in relieving pain intensity than PRF (Fig. 4: SMD=-0.83, 95% CI [-1.37, -0.30], P=.002). Because our heterogeneity test showed that the included trials had high heterogeneity (P<.00001, I2=87%), a subgroup analysis was conducted according to follow-up time points. The follow-up time points of each trial are shown in Table 1. The subgroup analysis of pain intensity within 3 months after treatment showed stSCS was more effective in relieving pain intensity than PRF (Fig. 5: the first day: MD=-1.03, 95%CI [-1.30, -0.76], P<.00001; the first week: SMD=-0.66, 95%CI [-1.26, -0.06], P<.05; the first month: SMD=-1.37, 95%CI [-2.17, -0.57], P=.0008; the second month: SMD=-1.77, 95% CI [-2.26, -1.27], P<.00001; the third month: SMD=-0.77, 95%CI [-1.32, -0.22], P=.006). However, the subgroup analysis of pain intensity at the sixth month after treatment showed that both stSCS and PRF were consistent in relieving pain intensity (Fig. 5: SMD=-0.63, 95% Cl [-1.27, 0.02], P=.06).

Figure 4. Comparison of stSCS and PRF in relieving pain intensity at the end of follow-up.

Figure 5. Comparison of stSCS and PRF in relieving pain intensity at different follow-up time points.

We compared the efficacy of stSCS and PRF in patients with different courses of disease at different follow-up time points. Two trials[28,29] reported patients with a mean disease course less than 3months in the SCS group and the PRF group. For the patient’s mean disease course less than 3months, our subgroup results showed that stSCS significantly relieved pain intensity compared to PRF (Fig. 6: MD=-1.11, 95%CI [-1.91, -0.31], P=.006 at 1month; MD=-0.93, 95%CI [-1.79, -0.08], P=.03 at 3months; MD=-1.16, 95%CI [-1.58, -0.75], P<.00001 at 6 months). Two trials[30,32] reported patients with a mean disease course greater than 3months in the SCS group and the PRF group. For patients with a mean disease course of more than 3 months, our subgroup results also showed that stSCS significantly relieved pain intensity compared to PRF (Fig. 7: MD=-2.33, 95% Cl [-4.22, -0.44], P=.02 at 1month; MD=-1.67, 95% Cl [-2.55, -0.79], P=.0002 at 2 months).

Figure 6. Comparison of stSCS and PRF in relieving pain intensity for patients with a mean course of disease less than 3months.

Figure 7. Comparison of stSCS and PRF in relieving pain intensity for patients with a mean course of disease of more than 3 months.

In addition, we also compared the efficacy of stSCS and the higher-voltage and longer-duration (compared to standard 45 V and 120s) PRF at different follow-up time points. Three trials[27,29,31] clearly reported higher-voltage and longer-duration PRF in the PRF group. Our subgroup results[29,31] showed that there was no significant difference between the stSCS group and the higher-voltage and longer-duration PRF group in relieving pain intensity (Fig. 8: SMD=-0.39, 95% Cl [-0.83, 0.04], P=.07 at 1week; SMD=-0.47, 95% Cl [-0.94, 0.01], P=.05 at 1month; SMD=-0.35, 95% Cl [-0.71, 0.02], P=.07 at 3months; SMD=-0.35, 95% Cl [-1.08, 0.39], P=.35 at 6 months). Likewise, Han et al[27] also reported the same results.

Figure 8. Comparison of stSCS and the higher-voltage and longer-duration PRF in relieving pain intensity.

3.4.2. Response rate. Two RCTs[27,31] reported the response rate (pain relief of more than 50%) after treatment. Because there was no heterogeneity (P=.72, I2 =0%), we used a fixed-effect model for the meta-analysis. The meta-analysis results showed that there was no significant difference between the stSCS group and the PRF group in the response rate (Fig. 9: RR=1.10, 95% CI [0.82, 1.48], P=.51).

Figure 9. Comparison of stSCS and PRF in the response rate.

3.4.3. Complete remission rate. Two RCTs[27,31] reported a complete remission rate (pain score≤3 or pain relief of more than 75%) after treatment. Because there was no heterogeneity (P=.56, I2=0%), we used a fixed-effect model for the meta-analysis. The meta-analysis results showed that there was no significant difference between the stSCS group and the PRF group in the complete remission rate (Fig. 10: RR=1.05, 95% CI [0.66, 1.68], P=.84).

Figure 10. Comparison of stSCS and PRF in the complete remission rate.

3.4.4. Sleep quality. A total of four trials[27-30] reported PSQI scores at the end of follow-up. Because there was no heterogeneity (P=.006, I2 =76%), we used a random-effect model for the meta-analysis. The meta-analysis results showed that stSCS was more effective in improving sleep quality than PRF (Fig. 11: MD=-1.43, 95% CI [-2.29, -0.57], P=.001).

Figure 11. Comparison of stSCS and PRF in the PSQI at the end of follow-up.

3.4.5. PRI-S/A scores. Two RCTs[30,32] reported PRI-S/A scores after treatment. Because there was high heterogeneity (PRI-S: I2 =93%; PRI-A I2 =95%), we did not perform quantitative analysis. Han et al[30] assessed PRI-S/A scores at 10 days, 1 month and 2 months and reported that the stSCS group exhibited lower PRI-S/A scores than the PRF group at all follow-up time points. Likewise, Yang et al[32] recorded PRI-S/A scores at 1day, 1week, 1month, 2months and 3months and reported that the stSCS group exhibited lower PRI-S/A scores than the PRF group at all follow-up time points.

3.4.6. Other outcomes. The following outcomes, such as inflammation levels and the use of analgesics and calcium channel antagonists, only had a single set of data, which could not be used for quantitative analysis. Therefore, we conducted a descriptive analysis. In terms of inflammation levels, Han et al[27] reported that levels of IL-8 and TNF-a in the stSCS group and PRF group decreased significantly after treatment compared with preoperative values (P<.05), but there was no significant difference between the two groups (P>.05). Regarding the use of analgesics and calcium channel antagonists, as Liu et al[31] reported, the number of patients who used analgesics and calcium channel antagonists in the stSCS group and the PRF group significantly decreased from 4 to 24weeks after treatment (P<.001), but there was no significant difference between the two groups (P>.05).

3.5. Safety

3.5.1. Adverse events. A total of 5 trials[27,29-32] reported adverse events. Wang et al[29] and Yang et al[32] did not report serious adverse events in either the stSCS group or the PRF group. However, Han et al[27] reported 2 cases of dizziness and drowsiness, 1 case of nerve injury, and 1 case of pneumothorax in the stSCS group, and there were 5 cases of dizziness and drowsiness, 3 cases of nerve injury, 3 cases of pneumothorax, and 4 cases of local hematoma in the PRF group. Liu et al[31] reported 2 cases of pneumothorax in the PRF group. In addition, Han et al[30] reported one case of a patient with infection in the PRF group. In conclusion, stSCS was safer than PRF (Fig. 12: RR= 0.32, 95% CI [0.12, 0.83], P<.05).

Figure 12. Forest plot of adverse events.

3.6. Sensitivity analysis

We performed sensitivity analyses for outcomes with high heterogeneity. When we excluded one study at a time, the overall effect size in each group did not change significantly, implying that the meta-analysis results were relatively stable.

3.7. Publication bias

We created a funnel diagram of pain intensity at the end of follow-up. As shown in Fig. 13, the funnel plot of the included trials seemed to be symmetrical, indicating that there was little publication bias in the included studies. Because we pooled only 6 studies, we did not perform Begg and Egger tests.

Figure 13. Funnel diagram of pain intensity at the end of follow-up.

4. Discussion

Zoster-related pain refers to neuralgia occurring during herpes and after herpes healing, which includes acute herpetic neuralgia, subacute herpetic neuralgia and postherpetic neuralgia. Compared with acute herpetic neuralgia/subacute herpetic neuralgia, postherpetic neuralgia is more difficult to treat because of its complex generation mechanism. To date, the pathogenesis of postherpetic neuralgia is still not completely clear. At present, the pathogenesis of postherpetic neuralgia mainly includes the following aspects: (1) pathogenesis of the central nervous system: changes in brain function and structure,[33] central sensitization,[34] attenuation of GABA inhibitory function,[35] neuro-inflammatory reaction[36]; (2) pathogenesis of peripheral nerve: peripheral sensitization,[37] ion channel alteration,[38] nerve conduction dysfunction.[39] To prevent and treat postherpetic neuralgia, neuromodulations, including stSCS and PRF, are increasingly used to treat patients with early zoster-related pain. Although some clinical studies have shown that both stSCS and PRF can be used to treat zoster-related pain, including postherpetic neuralgia, we do not know the difference between stSCS and PRF in the efficacy and safety for the treatment of zoster-related pain. Thus, we performed this meta-analysis.

In this meta-analysis, we obtained four main findings. First, stSCS significantly relieved the pain intensity of zoster-related pain patients and improved sleep quality and the feeling and emotion of zoster-related pain patients compared with PRF. Second, the efficacy of stSCS is similar to that of PRF in the response rate, the complete remission rate, the inflammation level and the use of analgesics and calcium-channel antagonists. Third, stSCS is effective in the treatment of zoster-related pain and has a longlasting effect. Fourth, stSCS caused fewer adverse events than PRF.

It has been reported that conventional SCS can be used to treat different kinds of neuropathic pain. A prospective two-center randomized controlled trial showed that SCS treatment significantly relieved pain and improved global impression of change scores for patients with painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy compared with traditional medicine therapy.[40] Caridi et al[41] also reported that SCS treatment effectively relieved phantom limb pain for patients who did not respond to conventional therapy. However, conventional SCS still has some shortcomings, such as limited coverage and unbearable paranesthesia. To improve the efficacy of SCS, some new stimulation methods have been gradually applied in clinical treatment. New stimulation methods mainly include high-frequency spinal cord stimulation, dorsal root ganglion stimulation and burst SCS. Different from the conventional SCS, new stimulation methods can achieve analgesia without generating paranesthesia. In addition, some clinical studies have shown that new stimulation methods are obviously superior to conventional SCS in terms of relieving pain.[42,43] In our meta-analysis, all trials reported using conventional stSCS to treat zoster-related pain. Thus, our research objects were limited to conventional SCS and PRF. Some clinical trials are needed to confirm whether the new stimulation method is more effective in treating zoster-related pain than PRF.

Although stSCS and PRF are both neuromodulation methods, the duration of neuromodulation between the two treatments is obviously different. The duration of neuromodulation of stSCS is usually 1 to 2weeks, which is longer than that of PRF. In addition, in terms of mechanisms of action, conventional SCS can directly stimulate the posterior column to block pain signal transduction and decrease the excitability of sensory neurons in the dorsal horn, which is beneficial to the early effect of SCS. Thus, stSCS may be superior to PRF in terms of early efficacy in theory. To test this idea, we compared the effect of stSCS and PRF in relieving pain intensity at different follow-up time points. Our subgroup results showed that stSCS significantly decreased pain scores in patients with zoster-related pain within 3months after treatment compared to PRF. In addition, our subgroup results showed that stSCS and PRF were equally effective in reducing pain intensity at 6months. At present, there is sufficient evidence to prove that PRF is effective in the treatment of zoster-related pain. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials showed that PRF significantly reduced pain in patients with herpetic neuralgia, and the effects of PRF lasted for 6months.[44] In addition, another meta-analysis also showed that PRF was effective in the treatment of postherpetic neuralgia, and the efficacy of PRF could last for a long time.[45] Thus, we speculated that stSCS could also be effective in the treatment of zoster-related pain and that the efficacy of stSCS was relatively lasting, which is consistent with the results reported by Yanamoto et al.[25] We believe that the mechanism of action of SCS can explain the persistence of the efficacy of stSCS. Some studies have shown that the regulation of neurotransmitters in the dorsal horn is also an important mechanism of SCS in the treatment of neuropathic pain. The regulation of neurotransmitters mainly includes the balance of excitatory neurotransmitters and heterogeneous neurotransmitters[46] and the activation of the cholinergic transmitter system.[47] After the electrode was removed, the adjusted neurotransmitter could still decrease the excitability of sensory neurons to relieve pain.

Because postherpetic neuralgia is difficult to treat, more attention has been given to preventing the development of postherpetic neuralgia. At present, the measures to prevent postherpetic neuralgia mainly include the following aspects: herpes zoster vaccine,[48] antiviral drug,[49] glucocorticoids,[50] physical therapy,[51] and nerve block.[52] Among these preventive measures, the herpes zoster vaccine is a relatively recognized method to prevent postherpetic neuralgia, while other preventive measures still need strong evidence to be confirmed. In recent years, several studies have shown that stSCS and PRF may effectively prevent the transition of acute/subacute zoster-related pain to postherpetic neuralgia in addition to treating postherpetic neuralgia. Huang et al[53] conducted a retrospective comparative study of patients with zoster-related pain (including acute herpetic neuralgia, subacute herpetic neuralgia and postherpetic neuralgia) treated with stSCS, and their results showed that stSCS significantly reduced the VAS score of patients with zoster-related pain within 12months after treatment and that stSCS significantly reduced the pain intensity of patients with acute herpetic neuralgia/subacute herpetic neuralgia compared to postherpetic neuralgia patients. Similarly, another retrospective analysis of patients with acute herpetic neuralgia/subacute herpetic neuralgia treated with stSCS also showed that stSCS may be effective for preventing postherpetic neuralgia.[54] In addition, a retrospective study of patients with zoster-related pain showed that the pain intensity of patients with zoster-related pain was decreased after PRF treatment, and the degree of pain reduction in patients who received PRF within 90days after herpes onset was significantly higher than that of patients who received PRF more than 90days after herpes onset, indicating that PRF may effectively treat and prevent postherpetic neuralgia.[55] To compare the efficacy of PRF and stSCS in the prevention and treatment of postherpetic neuralgia, we performed a subgroup analysis for patients with different courses of disease. Our subgroup analysis showed that stSCS is superior to PRF in preventing postherpetic neuralgia within 6months after treatment and relieving pain intensity in postherpetic neuralgia within 2months after treatment. Although PRF has been used to treat zoster-related pain, the ideal parameters for PRF for the treatment of zoster-related pain have not been determined. In our meta-analysis, the PRF parameters reported in the included research were different, especially the voltage setting and the time setting. 42°C, 2Hz, 45 V, and 120s are the standard PRF parameters commonly used in clinical practice. Nevertheless, a randomized, double-blinded, controlled trial showed that high-voltage, long-duration PRF is an effective treatment for patients with acute/subacute zoster-related trigeminal neuralgia.[56] Another randomized controlled trial further suggested that higher voltage settings had superior efficacy in treating postherpetic neuralgia.[57] In addition, Fang et al[58] and Tanaka et al[59] suggested that a longer time setting or a higher voltage setting may be more effective for NPs. In our subgroup analysis, we compared the efficacy of stSCS and the higher-voltage and longer-duration (compared to standard 45 V and 120s) PRF at different follow-up time points, and our results showed that there was no significant difference between the stSCS group and the higher-voltage and longer-duration PRF group in relieving pain intensity at 1week, 1month, 3months and 6 months after treatment.

The studies included in our meta-analysis showed a high degree of heterogeneity. We believe that the following aspects resulted in heterogeneity. First, the characteristics of the patients included in the study were different, such as the course of their disease. Second, the stSCS setting parameters and PRF setting parameters included in the studies varied greatly, which may cause the difference in relieving pain intensity. Finally, the treatments included in the studies were not consistent. Meng et al[28] and Liu et al[31] both used combination drug therapy in both groups. Han et al,[27] Wang et al,[29] Han et al[30] and Yang et al[32] did not report using combination drug therapy in either group.

This review has many limitations, which may affect the accuracy of the meta-analysis results. First, only 6 RCTs with small sample sizes were included in this study. Second, all RCTs were carried out in China, which could affect the generalization of the findings from this study. Thus, an increasing number of large, international, high-quality, long-term, randomized controlled trials is needed to confirm our results. Third, we included patients with herpes zoster mainly located in the trunk. The comparison of the efficacy of stSCS and PRF in the treatment of patients with facial zoster-related pain is unclear in our study. Finally, the follow-up time of the included trials ranged from 2 months to 6months, so we were unable to compare the long-term efficacy of stSCS and PRF for treating zoster-related pain.

5. Conclusion

In this study, stSCS showed a better analgesic effect and higher safety than PRF. Our meta-analysis results suggested that stSCS may be a feasible and safe invasive treatment for zoster-related pain. The results are limited by small sample size and localized population. To further compare the efficacy and safety of stSCS and PRF for zoster-related pain, larger, international, highquality, long-term, randomized controlled trials are needed.
