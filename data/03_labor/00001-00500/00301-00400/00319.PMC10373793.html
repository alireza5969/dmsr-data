Publicly-Funded Services Providing Sexual, Reproductive, and Maternal Healthcare to Immigrant Women in the United States: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Sexual, reproductive, and maternal health (SRMH) care in the US is highly politicized, with restrictions that impede immigrant women’s health. This review describes SRMH outcomes among immigrant women accessing publicly-funded services. We examined articles published from December 2007 to August 2020 in PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science databases, following PRISMA guidelines. Included articles (n = 9) consisted of predominantly Latina immigrant samples. The majority included a subsample of women classified as vulnerable due to low income, low educational attainment, and/or documentation status. Our search strategy included a range of SRMH outcomes; however, the majority of articles focused on prenatal care (PNC). Over half of the articles revealed that underserved immigrant women with access to Medicaid/CHIP during expansion had higher rates of PNC adequacy compared to those without access. There is a need for more research on the impact of publicly-funded services other than Medicaid on outcomes beyond PNC.

Introduction

Throughout their life course, women and girls have unique mental and physical health needs related to puberty, reproduction, and menopause [1]. Ensuring access to consistent and comprehensive healthcare, and specifically sexual, reproductive, and maternal health (SRMH) services is critical to the health and well-being of all women and girls in the United States (US), including historically underserved groups like immigrant women. In this paper, we define access in terms of eligibility for publicly-funded services and the removal or mitigation of other barriers to care, including language or cultural barriers. In order to meet women’s dynamic health needs throughout the life course and ensure bodily autonomy, affordable SRMH services, including family planning, prenatal care (PNC), testing and treatment for sexually transmitted infections (STI), and cancer screenings, must be made widely and consistently available [1–3].

SRMH services, especially preventive services, positively impact women’s health, and promote women’s financial stability, educational attainment, and career goals, which in turn can benefit economic, social, and cultural sectors of the country [4–6]. To realize these benefits, SRMH services must be prioritized [2], but all too often SRMH care is subject to politicization with excessive restrictions that limit and impede women’s health, and contribute to health disparities [7]. The politicization of SRMH issues is particularly detrimental to immigrant women, who constitute 14% of the female population in the US [8]. Immigrant women in the US face multifaceted oppression, reflecting the intersection of gender, race, immigration status, and socioeconomic status [9, 10]. Their access to employment, housing, healthcare, and other social services is restricted by policies that often have language, citizenship, and documentation requirements [9].When immigrant women face threats to their healthcare and do not have access to publicly-funded health insurance or programs that subsidize the cost of healthcare, they use fewer preventive services [7] and often delay care [11], which can lead to severe morbidity, high treatment costs, and even mortality [12].

In the current US sociopolitical climate [12, 13], immigrant women face systemic barriers that limit their access to SRMH services. The implementation of the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (ACA) in 2014 expanded access to SRMH services, including maternity care, contraceptive services, STI screening and counseling, HPV vaccination, and other preventive care [14]. The ACA contributed to closing the health insurance coverage gap between US citizens and legally authorized immigrants by offering similar entitlements to coverage in states that implemented Medicaid expansion and, for low-income individuals ineligible for Medicaid coverage based on immigration status, by subsidizing the cost of private insurance purchased from health insurance exchanges [15]. However, the ACA did not benefit all immigrant groups equally [16]: undocumented immigrants were excluded, and most states upheld a 5-year residency requirement for non-citizen immigrants legally present in the US [15]. Since 2018, lawmakers have weakened ACA coverage of SRMH services including contraception [17], threatened to withhold Medicaid coverage from people who do not fulfill a work or school requirement, and in 2020, threatened to overturn the entire ACA [15].

Historically, numerous policies have denied immigrants access to publicly funded services. For example, the ‘Public Charge’ Rule denies legal permanent residency to immigrants who are dependent or likely to be dependent on publicly funded services [18]. The rule officially went into effect in February 2020 and while it was discontinued in April 2021 [18], it sets a precedent that threatens the future of immigrants who rely on public services [18]. Following the initial proposal of the ‘Public Charge’ Rule change in 2018, the Kaiser Family Foundation (KFF) found that 75% of community health centers reported a perceived increase in the number of immigrant patients who declined to enroll themselves in Medicaid in the past year [19]. Health centers reported that immigrant women who were pregnant initiated PNC later in the pregnancy and attended fewer visits as a result of fears stemming from the new rule [19]. The revised “Public Charge” rule did have exceptions for pregnant women and individuals under age 21, which would offer some protection for access to SRMH services; however, many immigrant women could be unaware of these exceptions or could fear that accessing services would harm their chances of obtaining legal permanent residency [19, 20].

There is evidence that the availability and coverage of SRMH care through publicly-funded services can improve health outcomes as a whole [13]. There is also some evidence documenting immigrant women’s low usage of SRMH services compared to US-born women [7]. Reviews from earlier periods (1990s and 2000s) show the negative effects of anti-immigrant policies on immigrants’ access to health services [21] and the potential for welfare reform to adversely impact women’s health [22]. Yet, to our knowledge, no review to date has specifically focused on how the availability and use of any publicly-funded services affect SRMH outcomes among immigrant women. Thus, this review focuses on the period following 2007, including both the Obama and Trump federal administrations, to capture two different policy environments with potential relevance to the current state of immigration policy and SRMH outcomes. In order to adequately support immigrant women and to propose potential opportunities for improvement in their SRMH care, the impact of publicly-funded services on SRMH outcomes must be well-documented. This review aims to highlight service availability and provision for immigrant women to inform future policies. This systematic review also describes the characteristics and needs of immigrant women served by publicly-funded programs, barriers faced in accessing services, and SRMH outcomes among immigrant women accessing publicly-funded services in the US.

Methods

This systematic review was guided by the standards set by Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [23]. This research is exempt from IRB as it does not meet the definition of human subjects research. The manuscripts are publicly available, and no human subjects data were used.

Search Strategy

The population, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and settings (PICOS) were operationalized to define the parameters of the research question (Table 1). Articles searched were published from December 1, 2007 through August 1, 2020 in order to reflect two different presidential administrations that created different policy environments.

We conducted an initial scoping review to identify review articles and refine the research question and key search terms. We searched three electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and PsycINFO), and the databases of Guttmacher Institute, Ibis Reproductive Health, and Advancing New Standards in Reproductive Health (ANSIRH). References within identified articles were searched for additional articles. Original research articles were included; commentaries, editorials, guidelines, and reviews were excluded.

Articles that met the inclusion criteria (Table 1) consisted of a sample of women, aged 13–49, immigrant, refugee, or undocumented status, describing an intervention of federal, state, or city publicly-funded service or program, and addressing preventable conditions or statuses related to women’s SRMH outcomes, including immunization, immunization-preventable diseases, and PNC, guided by the US Preventative Task Force Grade A recommendations [3]. Since adolescent sexual development can begin as early as age 13 with first menarche and initiation of other sexual behaviors [24], we included ages 13–49 years. Articles with 50% or more of the sample outside of the age range; missing sex-disaggregated data; missing a comparison group; or addressing health outcomes related to breastfeeding, breast cancer (not including mammography), child death, or PNC adherence were excluded. Articles that included only knowledge, attitude, or belief outcomes were also excluded.

The PRISMA diagram (Fig. 1) outlines the process of review and exclusions. The title and abstract review were conducted consecutively by two reviewers (TJ, JL), using a shared database to track exclusion criteria (n = 481). Full article review was conducted by the same reviewers (TJ, JL) with consensus achieved for exclusions (n = 51). At this stage, articles were also excluded if a comparison group (a sample of individuals not receiving federal, state, or city publicly-funded services or programs) was not included. Key data collection domains were identified and synthesis of the (n = 9) articles was initiated.

Data Synthesis

Each article was reviewed to assess the specific intervention and outcome differences among the target population sample and comparison group. Limitations were noted and the risk of bias in each article was guided by the major domains of the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies—of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [25], which was designed to assess the dimension and likelihood of bias in non-randomized studies. Bias due to confounding, selection of participants into the study, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results were evaluated.

Results

Table 2 provides a summary of the nine articles evaluated in this systematic review. Although the publication time period began in 2007, all included articles were published between 2014 and 2019, with 78% published between 2017 and 2019, categorized to reflect before and after the change in the federal administration. More than half of the articles studied the effects in specific states (78%) and over three-quarters of the included articles (89%) used retrospective cohort designs. With the exception of Atkins et al., which used bivariate analyses, all articles employed adjusted logistic or linear regression to compare outcomes between groups, with five articles using causal inference methodologies, specifically, difference-in-difference analyses. The majority of studies focused on PNC outcomes (79%). Measurement of PNC adequacy varied across articles. Five of seven articles used the Kotelchuck Adequacy of PNC Utilization Index. Drewry et al. used a combined index to measure PNC adequacy and Swartz et al. 2017 defined adequate PNC as one visit in the first trimester of pregnancy and nine or more visits overall (Table 3).

The immigrant samples in all of the articles consisted of predominantly Latina women. The majority of the articles (seven of nine) included a subgroup of immigrant women who were classified as vulnerable due to low income (< 200% of the federal poverty level) and/or low educational attainment (less than high school level education) (Table 4) [26–31, 34]. Seven articles included undocumented women (see Table 4) [26, 27, 29–33].

Key Findings

There is evidence that publicly-funded services are positively associated with PNC outcomes [26, 27, 29–33]. Six of the articles indicated that immigrant women of undocumented status or new legal permanent resident women, low-income, and/or low educational attainment with access to Medicaid/CHIP during Medicaid/CHIP expansion had higher rates of PNC adequacy [26, 27, 29, 31, 32] and lower rates of PNC inadequacy [26, 33] compared to women without access to Medicaid/CHIP. In one article by Atkins et al., undocumented immigrant women with access to Medicaid during Medicaid expansion pointed to significantly higher rates of PNC adequacy compared to undocumented immigrant women without access to Medicaid, even after adjusting for key sociodemographic covariates [27]. In Drewry et al., Latina immigrant women with access to CHIP during CHIP expansion pointed to significantly higher rates of PNC adequacy compared to immigrant women without access to CHIP. However, after adjusting for key covariates this effect was only seen for Mexican-born single mothers with less than 8 years of education [29]. Similarly, in Held & Lindley, undocumented immigrant women using government insurance pointed to significantly lower rates of PNC inadequacy compared to undocumented immigrant women who paid out of pocket, even after adjusting for key covariates and Medicaid expansion [30] (Table 5).

Aside from PNC outcomes, one article by Coller et al. assessed unintended births and length of stay in the US among Latina immigrants, and found increased number of unintended births among this group as the number of years living in the US increased, even after adjusting for key covariates [28]. A 2019 study by Swartz et al. showed a positive impact of Emergency Medicaid Plus coverage in the diagnosis of maternal conditions such as gestational diabetes and hypertensive diseases of pregnancy [33]. Evidence of the impact of publicly-funded services on other SRMH outcomes for immigrant women was inconsistent or, for certain outcomes, completely lacking. Infant health outcomes including infant mortality were assessed in two articles, and the findings were inconsistent [31, 32]. Similarly, contraceptive method choice or use was assessed in one article by Tapales et al. [34]. After adjusting for covariates, including insurance status, immigrant women were less likely than US-born women to use any contraceptive method and less likely to use highly effective contraceptive methods; however, findings varied significantly by race and ethnicity [34]. Additionally, our search strategy included a wide range of other SRMH outcomes such as screening and/or treatment of STIs (including HIV, syphilis, HPV, and Hepatitis B), cervical cancer screening, immunizations, tobacco use, and folic acid supplementation—zero articles were yielded.

All of the articles acknowledged barriers preventing immigrant women’s access to SRMH care, including cost of services, Medicaid 5-year waiting period for new citizens, ineligibility for publicly-funded services due to undocumented status, real or perceived threats due to immigration status, lack of knowledge about services, and language barriers. Aside from ineligibility based on immigrant status, which was the focus of six articles [26, 27, 29, 31–33], the effects of these barriers on SRMH outcomes were not examined. While access to or use of publicly-funded programs was not consistently associated with improved SRMH outcomes among all immigrant women, it did improve SRMH outcomes for subgroups of immigrant women who were at increased risk of adverse outcomes due to low income status, low educational attainment, and/or undocumented status [26–31].

Sources of Bias

The assessment of bias identified some limitations in the studies (Table 6). While studies showed improvements in access to PNC through Medicaid and CHIP, these studies were often specific to certain states, and certain immigrant groups. Across all articles, there was risk of bias due to confounding, as no study design used randomization to publicly-funded services. Although individuals must self-select to use publicly-funded services, this potential source of bias was addressed in many articles through the inclusion of a comparison group. Comparison groups consisted of immigrant women who were not eligible for Medicaid either because they lived in a state where Medicaid expansion had not been enacted or, if they lived in a state where expansion had been enacted, because they were pregnant during a period before the policy was enacted or after it was rolled back [26, 27, 29, 31–33]. Five of nine articles conducted analyses that adjusted for key covariates such as demographic variables (education level, age, SES), maternal characteristics, and maternal risks. Existing literature on immigrant health research underscores the need to include key variables such as country of origin, time since migration, and generation, which were largely absent in the articles included in this review [35–37]. Only one of nine articles included both country of origin and time since migration as covariates. One article included only country of origin. Half of the articles addressed non-generalizability of the results to other states [26, 27, 30, 32, 33]. Another common limitation observed was that cross-sectional study designs limited causal inference [26, 27, 30, 32, 33]. Studies that used social security number (SSN) as a proxy for undocumented status faced risk of bias due to selection of participants into the study, as this may not have accurately captured the true undocumented population [26, 27, 30]. In all of the articles, there was also risk of bias due to exclusion of individuals from the sample who were missing data, as they likely differ systematically from those with complete data [34].

Discussion

This review aimed to synthesize information on the impact of publicly-funded services on SRMH outcomes among immigrant women in the US. Existing evidence indicates that immigrant Latina women, especially those who are undocumented, of low-income, and/or low educational attainment, lack access to healthcare services in the US and are therefore at increased risk for adverse health outcomes overall [38]. Evidence on SRMH services and outcomes also aligns with the results from this review, emphasizing that publicly-funded services supporting women regardless of immigration status increased access to and use of affordable SRMH care [7].

This review finds evidence that immigrant women with no documentation status, of low socioeconomic status, and/or low educational attainment who have access to publicly-funded services have significantly better PNC outcomes when compared to their counterparts without access to publicly-funded services. This review indicated that access to and use of publicly-funded services have the potential to reduce disparities in SRMH outcomes between immigrant women and US-born women. Lack of documentation, high costs of healthcare, and lack of knowledge about services among Latinas were factors that impeded access to SRMH care. These barriers aligned with qualitative studies that highlight Latinas’ perceptions and experiences in accessing SRMH care [39, 40]. The findings also elucidated that publicly-funded services together with community outreach programs have the ability to mitigate these barriers.

Policy Considerations

The findings from this review have implications for policymakers and healthcare providers in expansion of services for immigrant women. While expansion of Medicaid was effective in improving the adequacy of PNC for immigrant women, which plays a critical role in reducing maternal morbidity and mortality [40], there continue to be gaps in coverage of pre-pregnancy and postpartum care more broadly. Similar to US-born women, coverage for the continuum of care has the potential to help immigrant women and is critical for improving SRMH outcomes. An example of problematic SRMH policy in the US include the 2002 CHIP unborn child ruling, which revised the definition of a child under SCHIP to include “an unborn child” and therefore allowed states to cover PNC and delivery under SCHIP [41]. While this ruling aimed to expand SRMH coverage for immigrant women, it was restrictive in only covering women when they are pregnant, thus falling short of providing comprehensive SRMH coverage. Expanded coverage is critical to addressing risk factors that develop prior to pregnancy and to mitigating the potential for maternal morbidity and mortality, which can occur up to 42 days after birth [42].

There are several barriers that immigrant women face in accessing PNC. Avoidance of PNC due to cost barriers may cause complications and increase costs later in the continuum of care. Policies such as the Medicaid 5-year waiting period for new citizens cause delays in access to care until lawfully residing citizens have achieved qualified immigration status. States have the option to remove this waiting period for lawfully residing citizens; however, only 29 states have done so as of December 11, 2019 [43]. For undocumented immigrant women, geographical location determines the extent of the barriers faced, as there is significant variation between states’ policies related to PNC access as well as variations in the interpretation of federal policies [27]. Only 16 states provide coverage to all pregnant women under CHIP, regardless of immigration status [7]. While the benefits of PNC are clear, the importance of prevention and early intervention, beyond PNC, remain critically unaddressed by publicly-funded services. Policies that target the determinants of health and the root causes of health disparities such as educational attainment and income can make a difference in immigrant women’s ability to afford PNC and subsequent health outcomes [30].

Program Considerations

Several articles in this review noted the important role of community outreach and enrollment strategies in ensuring that immigrant women are aware of which services they are eligible for, the benefits of these services, how to enroll for these services, and where to seek them [28–30]. Held and Lindley [30] propose that clinicians lead educational information sessions on benefits of PNC in community-based settings. Coller et al. [28] encourage efforts to reach immigrant women outside of the clinical context, listing lay health workers, community leaders, telenovelas, and radio programs as potential entry points for community outreach and education. While expansion of publicly-funded services should be prioritized, there must simultaneously be significant efforts in ensuring that immigrants can utilize these services and understand the potential benefits associated with them.

Future Research

While this review presents strong evidence regarding Medicaid and CHIP services for undocumented women, there is a need for more research on the effects of the wide range of publicly-funded services (e.g. Title X funds, Women Infants and Children Program, Temporary Assistance for Needy Families or Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) on immigrant women’s SRMH outcomes. The restrictions on Title X funds that prohibit abortion providing clinics from accessing federal funds led to nearly one in four Title X clinics forgoing funding in 2019, reducing the capacity for the provision of contraceptive services by 50% or more at clinics in 17 states [44]. This poses a particular threat to immigrant women, as they are more likely than US-born women to obtain SRMH services, including contraceptive care, at publicly-funded family planning centers and to identify such centers as their usual source of care [7].

Additional research is also needed on the impacts of preventive SRMH care beyond PNC, such as abortion, STIs, contraception, cancer screenings, and postpartum health. Ninety percent of women who are sexually active and seeking to avoid pregnancy use at least one form of contraception [45]. Some federal policies do seek to ensure that women can access contraceptives and choose the method that best suits their needs, but loopholes in coverage remain [45]. Only one article in this review assessed contraceptive method choice; another [28] was identified but excluded as immigrant women only represented 2% of the sample. The limited findings from this review on contraceptive method choice and use indicated that more research is needed with larger immigrant samples to understand disparities and promote policies for improved access to contraceptive methods.

Overall, SRMH outcomes impact the long-term health and socioeconomic development of women, their children and families, and the population at large [4, 5]. Further research should elucidate the direct outcomes from preventive publicly-funded services, so that policymakers and advocates can make informed decisions about healthcare reform and outline steps to comprehensively address SRMH care for immigrant women in the US.

Immigrant women are a heterogenous cohort, facing different determinants of health and unique cultural and political barriers to seeking SRMH care [46, 47]. The majority of the articles in this review focus on immigrant women from Latin America. Latin American immigrants comprise the largest immigrant group in the US [46]. It is essential to avoid generalizing findings from these studies that focused almost exclusively on Latina cohorts to all immigrant women—or even to all immigrant Latina women. Future research should explore SRMH service access and outcomes among immigrant women from other regions whose populations are increasing in the US and who face health disparities and health inequities, some of which relate specifically to racism and religious discrimination, notably Arab immigrants and immigrants from East Asian and South Asian countries [48].

While this review did not identify any studies of immigrant women who are incarcerated or detained, it is important to note that these sub-groups of women are highly underserved. Both mass incarceration and immigrant detention violate the core principles of reproductive justice and diminish the right to bodily autonomy for all women, especially women of color, by threatening access to safe preventive SRMH services [49, 50]. Medicaid does not cover outpatient services for incarcerated individuals [51]. Excluding immigrant women who are incarcerated or detained from accessing publicly-funded services may potentially perpetuate disparities for this sub-group of women.

Conclusion

Funding public SRMH services is critical to improving the health of immigrant women, particularly those who are of low socioeconomic status and who lack documentation. Similar to women born in the US, immigrant women with low income and educational attainment would most benefit from publicly-funded programming. This review highlights the need to prioritize research that can inform the development of, and generate support for, health policies that grant immigrant women access to high-quality, evidence-based care.

Atkins et al., 2017 [26] In a sample of undocumented women, women covered by Medicaid (68% of the sample) were less likely to receive inadequate PNC (6.94% vs. 10.28%, p < 0.01) and to be obese prior to pregnancy (17.37% vs. 19.37%, p = 0.05) compared to women not covered by Medicaid.
