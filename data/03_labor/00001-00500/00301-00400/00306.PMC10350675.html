Examining the Relationship between Social Connection and Opioid Misuse: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Rationale:

The serious impact of the opioid overdose crisis requires an understanding of the root causes of opioid misuse. This systematic review assesses evidence for an association between social connection, the subjective experience of belonging, and opioid misuse or opioid use disorder among individuals living in the United States.

Methods:

We used a Boolean search strategy of PubMed, PsycINFO, and Embase to identify eligible publications from January 1990 to March 2020 and narrative analysis to synthesize the evidence.

Results:

The database search identified 1267 independent citations; 29 publications met inclusion criteria. Nearly all the studies demonstrated high risk of bias, most often due to selection and confounding bias. Most of the studies in the review (k = 20, 69%) found at least one significant association between social connection and opioid outcomes. Although no two studies included the same measures of social connection or opioid misuse, and social connection was both positively and negatively associated with opioid misuse, results support that social connection is an important correlate of opioid misuse.

Conclusions:

This review highlights the importance of social connection as a correlate to opioid misuse. However, the extensive variability among research studies points to a need for standardization of measurement and larger studies with diverse populations to allow for consequential recommendations for prevention or treatment of opioid misuse. Evidence regarding the associations between social connection and opioid misuse or disorder is sparse. We provide suggestions for advancing this research, including clarification of the complex influences between social connections and opioid misuse.

Introduction

Opioid misuse and overdose remain significant public health challenges. In the early 2000s, deaths involving opioids started to dramatically increase, at first due to the rapid increase in deaths involving prescription opioids, which was followed by an even steeper rise in overdose deaths involving heroin or illicitly manufactured synthetic opioids (Centers for Disease Control & Prevention, 2011; Hedegaard et al., 2020). In 2019, 10.1 million Americans aged 12 or older (3.7% of the population) had misused opioids in the past year (Substance Abuse & Mental Health Services Administration, 2020). In 2018, more than 67,000 drug overdose deaths occurred in the United States; 69.5% of them involved prescription or illicit opioids (Wilson et al., 2020). The public health community must prioritize research to identify the root causes of opioid misuse to mitigate the devastating trends of the opioid epidemic.

Social connection forms a critical component of individual and community life. Speaking broadly, social connection conceptualizes the subjective experiences and sense of belongingness that individuals feel at interpersonal, community, and societal levels (Van Bel et al., 2009). However, existing literature lacks consensus in defining and measuring social connection (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2017; Lakey & Cohen, 2000). Most researchers tend to either use the term “social connection” as an umbrella term for concepts related to social engagement, networks, and isolation or to focus on one of these concepts to best meet the needs of their research.

Individuals with healthy social connections are more likely to enjoy longer, happier, and more prosperous lives (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2017; Siedlecki et al., 2014). The more individuals connect with other people, the more they trust and collaborate with them on shared goals, furthering societal development (García-Sánchez et al., 2019). The presence of social support systems is also protective against the onset of anxious-depressive symptoms, and it strengthens one’s ability to regulate emotions (Marroquin, 2011; Ozbay et al., 2007). Intentional improvements in social connectedness are thought to reduce loneliness, which is a strong predictor of substance use outcomes, including opioid misuse and disorders (Jeste et al., 2020). Meta-analyses suggest lack of social connectedness increases an individual’s all-cause mortality by 22–26% (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010; Rico-Uribe et al., 2018), influenced by heightened cardiovascular maladies and reduced health-promotive behaviors (House et al., 1988). The absence of connection has also been shown to lead to increased risk of mental illness, particularly when paired with perceived isolation (Cornwell & Waite, 2009).

Given the high rate of comorbid mental illness and substance use disorders in the United States, examining how social connection—defined broadly as the subjective sense of belonging—is associated with substance use outcomes as an upstream variable is a necessary next step in developing prevention- and treatment-based knowledge (Blanco et al., 2020; Conway et al., 2006; Magidson et al., 2012). This systematic review critically examines the relationship between social connection and opioid misuse or opioid use disorder (OUD) in quantitative research studies. The review was designed to answer three research questions:

  1. What are the associations between social connection and opioid misuse and OUD?

  2. Do these associations vary by individual characteristics such as age, gender, race/ethnicity, and socioeconomic status?

  3. Do these associations vary across settings (e.g. rural or urban residence, health care settings, juvenile justice settings)?

Methods

We followed the systematic review best practice guidelines for research syntheses provided by Johnson and Hennessy (2019) and based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Moher et al., 2009). Our team received training from and collaborated with researchers from the RTI International-University of North Carolina (UNC) Evidence-based Practice Center (EPC) throughout the systematic review process. We registered the protocol, which was developed through an iterative process with experts on social connection and opioid misuse, with the Open Science Framework before initiating the review (Cance, 2020).

Search strategy

The initial search for related publications occurred in March 2020 and focused on the PubMed, PsycINFO, and Embase databases. We developed a Boolean search strategy (available upon request) in collaboration with our institution’s library and information services group. Opioid use search terms included opioids, analgesics, heroin, nonmedical prescription opioids, and OUD. Given lack of consensus regarding a definition of social connection as it relates to opioid misuse or OUD, we used a broad search strategy that included social support, social capital, social network, social isolation, social contact, social environment, interpersonal relations, social relationships, collective efficacy, loneliness, and social cohesion.

Table 1 presents the inclusion and exclusion criteria used in the selection of publications from April through July 2020. We restricted the review to publications that (1) contained original research; (2) sampled populations living in the United States that had variation in opioid misuse outcomes; (3) included individuals with variation in degree of social connectedness; (4) assessed a correlation between social connection and opioid misuse variables as either a primary or secondary study outcome; (5) were prospective cohort studies, retrospective case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, or intervention study data from participants not yet exposed to an intervention; and (6) were published in January 1990 or later because of changes in the diagnostic classification of opioid misuse and OUD (DSM III & American Psychiatric Association, 1987).

Because the context of the opioid crisis in the United States is unique, studies that were not published in English and did not sample U.S. participants were excluded. Publications that exclusively used qualitative study designs, intervention study data from participants who had been exposed to an intervention, narrative reviews, editorials, and letters to the editor were excluded. Studies in which outcomes did not distinguish opioids from other drugs (e.g. any prescription drug misuse) were also excluded.

Screening and eligibility assessment

We initially identified 1,270 publications (Figure 1). After removing duplicates, 1,267 remained. All titles and abstracts were imported into DistillerSR, a systematic review software that stores files and organizes agreement between reviewers. Four independent reviewers screened for relevance in three iterative pilot title-abstract screenings (k = 30 for the first pilot, k = 50 for the second and third pilots) to establish reasonable agreement. After the pilot screenings, the remaining publications were randomly divided across the four reviewers (k = 1,137) for two independent reviews of each title-abstract. The overall agreement, including pilot reviews, was 88%; four group discussions, led by the first author, were held throughout the process to resolve disagreements.

A total of 154 publications remained eligible for full-text review. As with the title-abstract review, the four independent reviewers screened the articles via randomization, resulting in two independent reviews of each. Total agreement was 64%; disagreements were resolved by having each reviewer reread the publication, followed by group discussion led by the first author. The most common reasons for exclusion were ineligible outcome (no independent assessment of opioid misuse or OUD; k = 61) and ineligible study design (no direct assessment of the relationship between social connection and opioid misuse or OUD; k = 32).

Twenty-nine publications (three dissertations and 26 peer-reviewed publications) were included in the final data abstraction process. We used Microsoft Excel as the data extraction tool to categorize critical information and data points from each publication. Statistical significance of an association between social connection and opioid misuse was determined using the cutoff of p < .05.

Study quality assessment

To assess the rigor and quality of the abstracted articles, three reviewers received formal instruction on risk of bias (ROB), using materials generated by the RTI-UNC EPC as a guiding framework (Viswanathan et al., 2008). This training provided an overview of different forms of bias in research settings (i.e. selection, information, performance, confounding, and reporting) and equipped reviewers with best practices to assess the presence of bias in articles’ study design, execution, or reporting. Reviewers used a qualitative, dual-independent approach in rating each article’s ROB. Reviewers used a series of signaling questions for each domain of bias and rated each specific domain as having low, moderate, high, or unclear ROB. Using the EPC standards, an article received an overall rating of high ROB if one or more domains were rated as high ROB. For an article to receive an overall rating of low ROB, at least 75% of the domains needed to be rated as low ROB. The registered scoping document excluded any studies at risk of high bias, but this criterion was removed after the full-text review stage because most of the studies had high ROB in at least one domain.

Results

Study and sample characteristics

Selected characteristics of the 29 studies meeting the inclusion criteria are presented in Table 2. Only two articles were published before 2000, and most (k = 21; 72%) were published in 2010 or later. Most studies were based on research with adult samples (k = 21; 72%), with four focused on older adults (ages 50 or older). Nearly all the articles included a mix of genders (k = 26) and races/ethnicities (k = 28), but representation was majority male (k = 20; 69%) and White, non-Hispanic (k = 17; 59%) samples. Studies were predominately cross-sectional (k = 20; 69%) with fewer than 1,500 participants (k = 23; 79%). Nine studies sampled individuals in treatment for OUD, nine were based on community samples, four used current or former opioid misuse as a screening criterion, and seven were conducted with specialized populations (e.g. homeless adolescents, individuals with chronic pain, veterans).

There was extensive variability across the studies in both opioid misuse measures and assessment of social connection (Table 3). Studies most often assessed prescription opioid misuse (k = 13; 45%) or any opioid use (k = 10; 34%) and relied on self-report (k = 22; 76%). Most studies used only one measure of frequency of opioid misuse, typically past-month (k = 11; 38%) or lifetime use (k = 8; 28%). Given the broad variation in social connection measures across the studies, we grouped the measures into three levels of influence on the basis of social ecological theory (McLeroy et al., 1988): intrapersonal (k = 5; 17%), interpersonal (k = 20; 69%), and community (k = 8; 28%). Intrapersonal factors included attitudes and beliefs within the individual such as loneliness, perceived rejection, and social isolation. Interpersonal measures included factors associated with relationships between the individual and family or friends, such as perceived social support, quality and content of social networks, connections to family or significant others, and connections to prosocial agents such as teachers or case workers. Community measures included factors associated with relationships between the individual and organizations or perceptions of individuals’ communities, such as participation in school activities, religious service attendance, social disorganization (i.e. lack of community cohesion), and social capital (i.e. social agency, connection, tolerance, trust, and norms of reciprocity within a community).

Study quality

Nearly all the studies in this review (k = 24; 83%) received a rating of high ROB, most often due to a high risk of confounding and sampling bias (Table 4). All studies received a rating of moderate to high confounding bias, most often because samples were of inadequate size to accommodate more than a few variables in the analysis. In addition, studies often relied on convenience samples—for example, recruiting from locations where opioid misuse or OUD was expected to be prevalent, such as treatment clinics (Polenick et al., 2019; Smith, 2002) or homeless shelters (Rice et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 2018). Most studies also had moderate to high risk of information bias (k = 27, 93%) due to a lack of standardized measures or reliance on self-reported opioid use. Reporting bias was also common across the studies, with most rated as moderate to high ROB (k = 27, 93%). Reporting bias was most often seen in studies based on secondary data analysis, where analyses were conducted on data originally collected for purposes other than examining the relationship between opioid misuse and social connection. These studies were deemed at risk because of the risk of selective disclosure and missing information.

Associations between social connection and opioid misuse

Of the 29 studies included in the review, 20 (69%) found at least one significant association between measures of social connection and opioid misuse outcomes (Table 4). However, identifiable patterns across studies were limited, either by individual characteristics (Research Question 2) or setting (Research Question 3), because of variability in both social connection measures and measures of opioid misuse. Furthermore, nearly all the studies reported only main effects, with exceptions noted below, preventing our ability to assess the research questions within study samples. As such, results are presented by type of social connection measure.

Intrapersonal measures of social connection

The five studies that included intrapersonal measures of social connection offered limited evidence of significant associations with opioid misuse. One cross-sectional study of predominately White females found that individuals with higher levels of perceived rejection had a higher likelihood of lifetime opioid use (Palamar, 2012). Another study of female adults with a history of opioid misuse did not find a significant association between perceived social isolation, or subjective feeling of disconnection from social network members, and daily heroin use (Miller & Paone, 1998). A prospective cohort study among male veterans recruited from treatment clinics (Smith, 2002) found that neither orientation toward social support (intrapersonal) nor perceived availability of social support (interpersonal) were associated with past-month opioid use.

Two studies examined moderating effects rather than main effects between intrapersonal measures of social connection and opioid misuse. Polenick et al. (2019) found the associations between perceived and structural loneliness and illicit opioid use varied by gender among individuals receiving methadone maintenance treatment: females with severe loneliness were more likely to report past-month illicit opioid use, but the opposite was true for males (severe loneliness was associated with lower odds of past-month use). Another study with predominately female chronic pain patients (Morris, 2015) found the relationship between ethnic identity (e.g. affirmation and belongingness; identity achievement) and opioid misuse was moderated by whether the individual currently had a prescription for opioids. Specifically, ethnic identity was not associated with past-month prescription opioid misuse among patients prescribed opioids by their clinicians, but strong ethnic identity was positively associated with past-month misuse among patients not currently prescribed opioids.

Interpersonal measures of social connection

Eight studies examined the association between social support and opioid misuse. Of these, four found protective effects of social support (Bohnert et al., 2010; Tunis et al., 1995; Warren et al., 2007) and four found no significant associations (Hahn, 2017; Hyman et al., 2009; Park & Lavin, 2010; Smith, 2002). Two of the four studies that found protective effects were prospective cohort studies with adults in treatment (Tunis et al., 1995; Warren et al., 2007): Tunis et al. (1995) found high levels of perceived alliance with peers were associated with decreased past-month opioid, and Warren et al. (2007) found an association between frequency of received social support and decreased heroin use. The third was a cross-sectional study of predominately Black males that found that individuals categorized as having high support (measured via social network characteristics) were less likely to have used heroin in the past 6 months than those with low or no support (Bohnert et al., 2010; Morris, 2015). Morris (2015) found that perceived support from one’s social network was not associated with prescription opioid misuse among chronic pain patients currently prescribed opioids but was negatively associated with past-month prescription opioid misuse among chronic pain patients not currently prescribed opioids. Three of the four studies that did not find significant effects were cross-sectional studies conducted with individuals recruited from within treatment facilities; the fourth was a prospective study of all males recruited from within treatment facilities.

Seven studies assessed the relationship between the nature and composition of one’s social network and opioid misuse. Despite extensive heterogeneity regarding sample characteristics, measures of social networks, and opioid outcomes, five of the seven found that aspects of an individual’s social network were associated with a greater likelihood of opioid misuse (Barman-Adhikari et al., 2015; Jonas et al., 2012; Neaigus et al., 2006; Rice et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 2018). Three studies conducted with homeless youth and young adults found that a higher number of substance-using peers or ties within their networks was associated with a greater likelihood of lifetime (Barman-Adhikari et al., 2015; Rice et al., 2011) or past-month (Zhao et al., 2018) heroin use. One study of never- or former-injecting heroin users found that greater exposure to injection drug use or communication promoting injection drug use within their social networks was associated with a greater likelihood of having transitioned to injecting heroin in the past 6 months (Neaigus et al., 2006). Results for social network size (not accounting for network opioid use) were mixed; one study in rural Appalachia found a positive association between effective network size and daily prescription opioid misuse (Jonas et al., 2012), whereas three others (Lofwall & Havens, 2012; Park & Lavin, 2010; Rice et al., 2011) found no association.

Six studies examined associations between presence of or connections to family or significant others and opioid misuse. Three studies examined the influence of spouses or partners, with mixed findings: in a prospective study of individuals receiving treatment, living with a partner was associated with greater past-month heroin use at baseline than being separated, but perceived closeness to the partner was not significant (Heinz et al., 2009); living alone was not associated with past-year opioid misuse among older adults (Day & Rosenthal, 2019); and among a general sample of adults, being divorced was associated with a higher likelihood of past-year prescription opioid misuse than being married (Ford et al., 2018). Hahn (2017) did not find main effects or an interaction between perceived family functioning, frequency of tangible social support from family, and past-month opioid use among adults receiving medication for the treatment of OUD. In contrast, Simpson et al. (2000) found that post-treatment cohesion with family was prospectively associated with a decreased likelihood of past-month opioid use. Donaldson et al. (2015) found no main effects in their examination of the association between perceived parental warmth and monitoring and lifetime prescription opioid misuse among adolescents. Instead, among youth ages 12–14, there was an interaction between parental warmth and monitoring, such that lifetime prescription opioid misuse was highest among youth reporting high monitoring and low warmth and those reporting low monitoring and high warmth.

Two studies with young people found connection to prosocial agents was associated with decreased likelihood of opioid misuse. Among homeless youth and young adults, those with reported connections to the homeless shelter agency staff had lower past-month heroin use (Zhao et al., 2018). A secondary analysis of the 2013 Minnesota Student Survey found that students with higher levels of connection with teachers had lower past-year prescription opioid use (Forster et al., 2017).

Community measures of social connection

Generally, studies that used participation in community-level events as a proxy for social connection did not find associations with opioid misuse. Three of the four studies that assessed the role of religious service attendance found no significant results (Day & Rosenthal, 2019; Ford et al., 2018; Palamar et al., 2014); the exception was the study by Reynoso-Vallejo and Rosen-Reynoso (2018), which found that frequent attendance was negatively associated with lifetime and past-year prescription opioid misuse among older adults. Similarly, a study of adolescents found no association between participation in school activities and prescription opioid misuse in the past 3 months (Lin et al., 2015). A study with lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender (LGBT) adolescents, however, found that participants who knew that their school had a club or group for LGBT students and their allies were less likely to have misused prescription opioids in their lifetimes (Heck et al., 2014).

In contrast, social capital and social disorganization were found to be associated with opioid misuse in all three studies using these measures, but the results were inconsistent. One study of older Hispanic males with a history of heroin use found that social capital, both overall and on most subscales (e.g. community participation, social agency, neighborhood trust/safety), was higher among those actively using heroin than among individuals in methadone maintenance therapy, but similar among current and former users not currently in treatment (Flores et al., 2013). Reynoso-Vallejo and Rosen-Reynoso (2018) found that older adults who believed they lived in a close-knit neighborhood and who had higher levels of social participation were less likely to have misused prescription opioids in the past year or in their lifetime, whereas there was no significant relationship between prescription opioid misuse and perceived trust or norms of reciprocity in the community. Finally, Ford et al. (2017) found that adolescents living in neighborhoods with high social disorganization (e.g. lack of cohesion) and low social capital (e.g. agency, trust, norms of reciprocity) were more likely to report past-year prescription opioid misuse; higher levels of social participation were also associated with greater past-year misuse.

Discussion

Summary of evidence

Social connection has been the focus of research partly because of its potential malleability as an opportunity to alter the trajectory of opioid-related outcomes (Blanco et al., 2020). This review is the first step in synthesizing results from a complex body of literature. While more than two-thirds of the studies in the review (k = 20) found at least one significant association between some measure of social connection and opioid outcomes, the directionality of the relationships was not straightforward: eight found positive associations, eight found negative associations, and four had mixed findings. Overall, the findings support the idea that an individual’s subjective experience of social connection is related to opioid use behaviors and outcomes, but more research is needed to disentangle the relationships.

On the surface, we found limited associations between intrapersonal-level factors of social connection and opioid misuse. However, the findings suggest that the relationship may differ based on gender. Polenick et al. (2019) found perceived and structural loneliness was positively associated with past-month opioid misuse for females and inversely for males. Perceived rejection from friends was associated with past-month opioid misuse in a sample that was predominately female (Palamar, 2012), while the one study with an exclusively male sample found no association between orientation toward social support and past-month opioid misuse (Smith, 2002).

At the interpersonal level, our review supports socialization theories (Battjes, 1984; Hawkins & Weis, 1985; Oetting & Donnermeyer, 1998), which conceptualize social connection as a necessary function to help individuals develop and strengthen their identities. Individuals were more likely to engage in opioid misuse when connected to a social network with norms supporting opioid misuse (Jonas et al., 2012; Neaigus et al., 2006; Rice et al., 2011). In contrast, when significant, individuals were less likely to engage in opioid misuse if they had perceived social support (Morris, 2015; Warren et al., 2007) and connections with prosocial agents (Simpson et al., 2000). In addition, we found alignment with theories that postulate that support from prosocial agents may buffer stressful events, resulting in lower rates of opioid misuse (Forster et al., 2017; Lakey & Cohen, 2000).

Generally, studies that used participation in community-level events, such as religious service attendance, did not find associations with opioid misuse (Day & Rosenthal, 2019; Ford et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2015; Palamar et al., 2014). It is possible that social participation is not a sufficient protective factor without consideration of the subjective evaluation of participation (Lakey & Cohen, 2000). This possibility is further supported by our finding that social capital was consistently associated with opioid misuse. However, social capital was found to be both protective (Ford et al., 2017; Reynoso-Vallejo & Rosen-Reynoso, 2018) and a risk factor (Flores et al., 2013). This finding may seem on the surface to be contradictory but may be a community-level representation of socialization theory. As seen in the interpersonal research, findings suggest an interactive effect between connections to the community and the substance-using norms of that community.

Evidence limitations

This review highlights the lack of standardization in measures of social connection and opioid outcomes. Across the 29 studies included in the review, no two studies used the same social connection measure and opioid outcome. This lack of consistency likely contributed to the mixed results reported in this review, but it also precludes our ability to determine whether the association between social connection and opioid use varies by contextual factors. The inconsistency in measurement of opioid use across the studies also makes it difficult to assess whether the influence of social connection varies based on the type of opioid (i.e. prescription opioids, heroin, synthetic opioids) or trajectories of use. Without a clear and consistent operationalization of social connection and standardization in measurement of opioid use outcomes, it is nearly impossible to interpret studies in aggregate and develop generalizable recommendations for policy makers, prevention scientists, treatment providers, and harm reductionists.

It is important to note that a plethora of foundational research uses qualitative interviewing and ethnographic methods to investigate ties between social connectedness and opioid misuse. Such research focuses on a variety of social connection constructs, including the influence of community and economic variables, the impact of digital clinical interventions, the effects of stigma and social norms, and the quality of romantic and familial relationships (Applewhite et al., 2017; Thompson et al., 2020; Tofighi et al., 2017; Young & Heinzerling, 2017). These studies emphasize contextualizing the experiences of marginalized peoples (racial/ethnic minorities, women, formerly incarcerated individuals, etc.) and demonstrate a need to explore the unique interplay of social connection and opioid misuse. This literature provides a strong conceptual foundation but not the large sample sizes or quantitative methods that would produce the generalizability of findings or assessments of statistical significance of associations needed to provide clear evidence for broader recommendations.

Implications

The systematic review approach allowed for a structured focus to further refine and help prioritize areas of future research that will be needed to fill gaps in existing literature regarding the links between social connection and opioid use. The most obvious challenge is disentangling the heterogeneity in the construct of social connection. For many of these studies, effects might not have been revealed because of variability in the subconstructs measured, imprecise or inconsistent measurement of social connection, or both. This review highlights the need to standardize interpersonal- and community-level measures of social connection that tap into the pathways by which social connection is expected to promote or protect against opioid misuse. Although it will be difficult to fully standardize measurement, an important first step is for researchers to use widely used instruments that fit their research questions. For example, a recent systematic review by Cordier et al. (2017) lists several measures of social inclusion for consideration. In addition, national epidemiological studies could include some of these instruments in subsequent data collection to better capture these relations.

Another gap in the literature is related to the measurement of opioid misuse. Nearly all studies relied on one measure of opioid misuse; these measures varied on frequency of use (e.g. lifetime, past year, past month), opioid type (prescription opioid, heroin, illicit synthetics), and mode of assessment (urinalysis, self-report). Reliance on a single measure may meet a practical need to reach an adequate sample size in studies, particularly when conducting research with the general population or adolescents. However, it limits the ability to draw conclusions regarding specific relationships between opioid misuse and social connectedness. One study (Reynoso-Vallejo & Rosen-Reynoso, 2018) that used more than one opioid misuse measure found differential results between the measures, suggesting that future research would benefit from including multiple opioid misuse outcomes.

These limitations notwithstanding, results from this systematic review point to areas in which practitioners can use the positive and negative aspects of social connection within intervention delivery. For example, an understanding of a patient’s prosocial social network and ways in which to incorporate those people’s influence into the recommended treatment regimen can enhance adherence to intervention protocols. Of similar importance would be an understanding of potential negative social influences that may block treatment progress. Often, practitioners focus assessment and intake on individual factors; although those are critical to understand, results from this review reveal how important positive and negative social connections can be in outcomes. Additional intervention research further examining these factors as prevention and treatment ingredients is needed. However, results from this review point to the need for practitioners to widen the lens to incorporate more information about social connections to reduce opioid misuse.

Limitations of the systematic review

Although our review was conducted according to best practice standards for systematic reviews, decisions made during the design of the scoping document likely influenced the findings. The review was limited to articles published in English and with participants living in the United States, given the different contexts of both social connection and opioid misuse in the United States than in other countries. The inclusion of additional studies may have reduced the apparent heterogeneity of measures and populations. The review was designed to exclude qualitative articles because the focus was to assess the directionality and strength of association between social connection and opioid misuse. However, during the title and abstract review, the research team identified an extensive body of qualitative literature. A scan of a subset of these studies found that the qualitative literature had a more in-depth focus on social connection, including disentangling the theoretical relationships between social connection and opioid misuse. A future systematic review of the qualitative literature could inform recommendations for the measurement of social connection.

Conclusion

This systematic review summarized the quantitative literature regarding the association between social connection and opioid misuse published in the past 30 years. Results point to a need for more rigorous studies, inclusion of more than one assessment of opioid misuse in individual studies, and a standardization of social connection measures. Although the limited breadth and existing heterogeneity within the research preclude clear conclusions, results suggest some promise in targeting meaningful connections to prosocial agents. The recommendations for future research provide direction for researchers and for practitioners working to reduce opioid misuse and associated harms through impacts on social connection.

Table 1.

Review inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria

  1. Original research

  2. Any population living in the United States that includes individuals with variation in opioid misuse outcomes

  3. Any population that includes individuals with variation in levels of social connection (alienation, civic engagement, collective efficacy, detachment, interpersonal relations, isolation, loneliness, neighborhood cohesion, psychosocial deprivation, social exclusion, social network, social participation, social capital, or solitude)

  4. Analyses correlate social connection and opioid use variables in meaningful way

  5. Prospective cohort studies, retrospective case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, intervention study data from participants who have not been exposed to intervention (e.g. baseline data only, comparison group only)

  6. Published in January 1990 or later
Exclusion criteria

  1. Study not published in English

  2. Qualitative studies, intervention study data from participants who have been exposed to intervention, narrative reviews, editorials, and letters to the editor

  3. Outcomes do not distinguish opioids from other drugs
