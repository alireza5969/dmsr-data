Abstract

AIM

To summarize developmental delay among infants and toddlers with sickle cell disease (SCD).

METHOD

This systematic review included studies that reported developmental outcomes of children with SCD between 0 months and 48 months of age and followed standards set forth by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

RESULTS

Ten studies were included, describing 596 unique developmental assessments. The rate of developmental delay ranged from 17.5% to 50% and increased with age. Cognition was the only domain included in all studies and the most frequently identified delay. One study reported that more severe SCD genotypes predicted worse development, while five studies reported no difference in rates of developmental delay across genotypes.

INTERPRETATION

These findings emphasize the need for standardized screening to identify children with SCD at risk of delay at a young age to facilitate appropriate referrals for therapeutic intervention. Frequent and comprehensive developmental screening is necessary among all SCD genotypes.

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is one of the most common genetic disorders of childhood, affecting 1 in every 365 African American births in the USA.1 SCD is typically diagnosed at birth and is a chronic condition characterized by anemia, strokes,2 and cognitive impairment.3,4 Symptoms of SCD emerge in the first year of life and can include pain,5 dactylitis (swelling of the extremities),6 reduced splenic function,7 and cerebral infarction.8 Compared with typically developing individuals, children with SCD are more likely to have a lower IQ, irrespective of the presence of cerebral infarcts, and cognitive abilities continue to decline with age.9,10 School-aged children with SCD have a higher utilization of special education services and lower academic attainment than age-matched comparison individuals.11,12 Learning disabilities are also more common and adversely affect academic performance and employment opportunities in adulthood.13,14 Additionally, children with SCD and lower IQ are more likely to have behavioral challenges that can affect development.15

There is an early onset of developmental delay among children with SCD.16,17 The degrees of anemia, oxygen saturation, and elevated cerebral blood flow velocity have been associated with lifelong delays.18 Other studies have demonstrated that the child’s home environment also influences early childhood development.19 Delays identified through screening of language and motor development in toddlers are associated with lower academic performance at school age, earlier onset of academic challenges, and an increased likelihood of repeating a grade.20 It is well recognized that cognitive deficits affect school-aged children and adults with SCD, but the impact of SCD on infant and toddler development is less defined.21

The purpose of this systematic review is to summarize the known research on development and the prevalence of delays, particularly cognitive impairment, among infants and toddlers (aged 0–48mo) with SCD. Cognitive ability among infants and toddlers with SCD is highly influenced by their home environment and early social interactions.12,22 Early identification of cognitive deficits is crucial to allow for timely interventions that could diminish the long-term effects of disability and maximize function in everyday activities.23,24 Caregiver-directed interventions in the first years of life can positively influence the developmental trajectory of children with SCD.22 A summary of research on infant/toddler development among children with SCD is a necessary first step to provide the background for recommendations for screening and inform intervention and future research.

METHOD

As a systematic review, ethics approval was not required.

Protocol and registration

All search strategies and written methodology were created using the standards and guidelines for conducting and reporting systematic reviews set forth by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines,25 the National Academies (IOM) Standards for Systematic Reviews,26 and the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic reviews.27,28 This study was registered with PROSPERO in July 2020 with number CRD42017072027. The literature review was completed with assistance from a medical librarian at Washington University School of Medicine.

Eligibility criteria

We included studies published in peer-reviewed journals that reported developmental outcomes of children with any genotype of SCD between 0 months and 48 months of age. All studies had to report standardized developmental testing in the domains of cognition, behavior, motor, or communication. Additional criteria are listed in Table S1 (online supporting information). Commentary or review articles, case reports, dissertations, systematic reviews, and abstracts were excluded. Although systematic reviews were excluded, their reference lists were reviewed for articles meeting inclusion criteria.

Search strategy

A medical librarian searched the literature in March 2020 for records including the terms ‘education’, ‘sickle cell disease’, ‘developmental delay’, ‘cognitive defect’, ‘cognition’, ‘neurodevelopment’, ‘language development’, ‘executive function’, ‘motor skills’, and ‘children’. The librarian created search strategies using a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary in Embase (1947–present), Ovid Medline (1946–present), Scopus (1823–present), Cochrane Databases of Systematic Reviews (CDSR), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature Plus (CINAHL Plus; 1937–present), The American Psychological Association’s PsycINFO (1800–present), and ClinicalsTrials.gov (1997–present). The full reproducible search strategies for each database are included in Appendix S1 (online supporting information).

Study selection

Article selection was completed by two trained graduate students (JE, NH). Students completed each stage of the review independently and met to discuss findings. Any disagreements were discussed with a third member of the research team (CRH) to reach consensus. In the first stage, manuscript titles were independently reviewed and excluded if the title did not indicate a report of child development and/or SCD. In the second stage, abstracts were reviewed; papers were excluded if the abstract indicated that the study did not include children with SCD between 0 months and 48 months of age or did not measure children’s development. The full texts for remaining articles were obtained and reviewed. Articles were subsequently excluded if they were not peer-reviewed studies, did not include children with SCD, or did not include outcomes from a standardized assessment of child development. All papers that met inclusion criteria were reviewed by a third reviewer (CRH) to reduce the risk of inclusion bias.

Data collection process

Data from all studies that met inclusion criteria were extracted independently by two authors (CRH, TEV) and compared for consensus using Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia; Table S2, online supporting information). Any interventions included in the study were recorded, and summary statistics for each primary outcome related to child development were extracted. If the article included summary statistics including older children with SCD, the corresponding author was contacted to determine whether data for the children aged under 48 months were available. Because of the heterogeneity of developmental assessments and domains studied, we were unable to conduct a complete meta-analysis of all developmental outcomes.

Risk of bias in individual studies

The methods for each study were analysed independently using the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool to assess risk of bias by two authors (CRH, LL).29 The QUIPS tool was developed to assess risk of bias in prognostic research in six categories: the proportion of eligible persons who participated in the study (study participation), study attrition, prognostic factor measurement, outcome measurement, study confounding, and statistical analysis and reporting. For articles included in this study that were not prognostic studies, the authors assessed measurement in patients’ and caregivers’ characteristics for the prognostic factor measurement domain. Risks for bias were categorized as low, moderate, or high on the basis of the QUIPS tool, and additional operationalizations were established a priori or during the consensus-reach process for risk assessing. Any disagreements were discussed by the two authors (CRH, LL) until consensus was achieved.

Summary measures

The primary indicator of developmental delay in young children is a below average score on a standardized assessment. Our primary outcome was to synthesize the results of testing related to child development.

Risk of bias across studies

There is limited research on this topic, and all studies were included in the search regardless of language. However, all studies included in this review were published in the UK or USA, which may have contributed a degree of bias in the study methodology or reporting. We had fewer than 10 studies included in the meta-analysis, so a funnel plot to evaluate bias across studies was not needed.30

Synthesis of results and additional analyses

On the basis of the heterogeneity of assessment tools included in this sample, the overall proportion of children identified with a delay was described for each study. Cognition was the only developmental domain described in every study and was used for comparative analysis across studies. The Bayley Scales of Infant Development, Second Edition (BSID-II)31 and the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (Bayley-III)32 were used in half of the studies. In these two versions of the Bayley there are composite scores for cognitive and language skills. In the BSID-II, the composite is the Mental Developmental Index (MDI). In the Bayley-III, the composite is the cognitive language composite (CLC). Following published methods, we used the CLC as an equivalent for the MDI.33 To compare the risk of cognitive deficits across studies using the same standardized test, we calculated the odds ratio on the basis of the standard US population rate of developmental delay of approximately 13%.34

RESULTS

Study selection

A total of 3573 results were found and exported to End-note; 1025 duplicate records were deleted, resulting in a total of 2548 unique citations included in the project library.35 Following title review, 173 articles were identified for abstract review. Sixty-five articles were excluded on the basis of the abstract. The full texts of 108 papers were thoroughly reviewed and 13 met inclusion criteria. Most were excluded because (1) findings were not published in a peer-reviewed journal (n=20) or (2) the population did not include children aged between 0 months and 48 months (n=62). One study was excluded because the population tested had sickle cell trait, not disease. The remaining 12 papers were excluded because the study design did not include developmental outcome measures or the study population included a duplicate cohort that had already been represented in previous included studies. The reference lists for systematic reviews and all eligible studies were screened, but no additional studies were identified. Of the 13 papers meeting inclusion criteria, two were excluded because the corresponding author indicated that data from developmental measures were not accessible for participants in our age range. One study was excluded because it was a follow-up report of children that also included their developmental data at a younger age that was a duplicate of an included study.20 Figure S1 (online supporting information) illustrates the PRISMA study selection process.

Study characteristics

In total, 10 studies published from 2002 to 2020 were included in this review of child development among children 48 months or younger with SCD. Most studies (n=8) were conducted in the USA; the remaining two were conducted in the UK. A summary of each study included in this review is outlined in Table S3 (online supporting information).

Risk of bias

Risk of bias was independently evaluated by two authors (CRH, LL) and reviewed until consensus was reached. A summary of the risk of bias for each included study is provided in Figure 1.

We determined a low risk of bias among all studies included in this review for three QUIPS domains: study participation, study attrition, and prognostic measurement (participant characteristics measurement for one study including interventions).22 All papers answered clear research questions and provided a description of the study population, and the assessments used. For the study attrition domains, the authors assessed risks on the basis of the description of missing data in study samples for half of the studies (n=5) that are cross-sectional. Most studies (n=9) did not include a direct intervention. For the outcome measurement domain, moderate risk of bias was determined for the one study with an intervention, because the same individual completed the intervention and outcomes testing and not all participants returned for follow-up assessment.22 Other reasons to identify studies as moderate risks in the outcome measurement domain included concerns with assessment procedures or assessments were not blinded. None of the studies had a clear definition of confounders and risk of biases were assessed on the basis of whether studies have included additional measures (home environment, parenting styles, or parent stress). High risk of bias on the statistical analysis and reporting domain was determined for two studies because of selective reporting of outcomes, and one study for concerns of statistical power owing to a small sample size.36–38 Among the 10 included studies, there were three instances where two publications referenced the same cohort of children. Hogan et al.39 described an analysis of a different assessment but with the same cohort as Hogan et al.17 Schatz et al.38,40 were from the same research group and probably had overlapping cohorts. Neither the Hogan et al. nor Schatz et al. cohorts were evaluated using either the Bayley-III or BSID-II measure, and thus were not included in comparative analysis. Finally, Fields et al.22 described a pre-/post-intervention study for which the baseline data were reported by Drazen et al.41 However, Fields et al. included a comparison cohort of children with SCD who did not take part in the intervention. To eliminate the risk of duplicating information, this cohort was used for comparative analysis in this review.22,41 These results are limited because of the lack of heterogeneity of participants.

Results of individual studies

Studies included in this review described 596 developmental assessments of children between 0 months and 48 months with SCD. Three studies included only children with hemoglobin (Hb)SS or HbSb0 thalassemia; children with these genotypes generally had more severe disease with lower hemoglobin and greater risk of cerebral infarction.17,39,42 All studies used a convenience sample of participants who were recruited by healthcare providers or within research universities.

Participants’ characteristics

Most studies (8 out of 10) reported that 100% of their population identified as African American or Afro-Caribbean; the remaining two studies reported that 4% to 5% of their sample identified as Hispanic.42,43 Most studies (9 out of 10) limited participants to those who spoke English, while one included non-English-speaking participants if a translator was available.42 As outlined in Table S3, three studies from the USA reported that 63% to 77% of children relied on public insurance,22,40,41 and three studies reported that 40% to 76% of participants had a household income of less than US$20 000.38,40,42 Studies conducted in the UK also indicated that their populations lived in districts associated with lower socioeconomic status.17,39 Educational attainment of the primary caregiver was reported in seven studies; 30% to 76% of caregivers in the USA16,22,41–43 and 21% of caregivers in the UK reported the highest level of education as a high-school diploma or equivalent.17,39 One study reported that approximately one-third of the children in their cohort lived in single-adult households.40 Nine studies reported sample sizes of less than 100, while one reported a sample size of 193.42

Biological variables associated with SCD that were reported included haematocrit,16,38,40 hemoglobin,17,37,39,43 middle cerebral artery velocity as measured by transcranial Doppler,17,38,39 and the proportion of the cohort who had SCD-related hospitalizations.38,40,41,43 Other SCD-related complications such as splenomegaly, dactylitis, and acute chest syndrome were reported by three studies.17,39,43

Outcome measures

All studies included assessments of child development that were completed by a trained healthcare provider. Assessments included the Ages and Stages Questionnaire, Third Edition,37 the BSID-II,16,42,43 the Bayley-III,22,41 the Bayley Infant Neurodevelopmental Screener,17,39 the Denver Developmental Screening Test, Second Edition,38,40 A-not-B task,39 Object Retrieval,39 Communicative Development Inventory,40 and the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales.38,40 Half of included studies also evaluated parent stress and characteristics of the home environment.16,22,38,40,41

Child development in SCD

For studies using outcome measures with normative scores, children were characterized as having a developmental delay if they scored at least 1SD below the mean. Most studies (n=9) identified developmental delay in some children. One study excluded children with low developmental scores;42 the remaining studies identified developmental delay in 17.5% to 50% of children with SCD. Across studies, the rate of developmental delay increased with age.16,17,37,39,40,42 Cognition was the only domain included in all studies and the most frequently identified developmental delay.16,17,22,37,39,41

One study reported that HbSS or HbSβ0 thalassemia genotype predicted worse development,16 while five studies reported no difference in rates of developmental delay among SCD genotypes.22,38,40,41,43 Only one study compared children with SCD with typically developing individuals and found that children with SCD were at greater risk of delay.17 All studies reported biological variables associated with SCD; one study found an association between the incidence of pain crises and delay,43 and three studies reported no significant association between reported SCD variables and development on the Bayley-III or BSID-II.22,41,42

Cognitive development is most frequently reported

Half of the studies used the BSID-II (n=3) or the Bayley-III (n=2) to describe cognition, although several other assessments were used. Table 1 compares the cognitive outcomes from all studies. For studies that assessed participants’ cognition at multiple time points, we listed only the cognition scores from the last time point in an effort to avoid duplicative data and to attempt to match participant ages across studies. In one case, we used data from the comparison cohort22 because the study sample received an intervention, and baseline data from the study sample were already reported in a previous included publication.41 Figure 2 depicts the five studies that reported cognitive outcomes using the BSID-II or Bayley-III and the relative odds ratio for a developmental delay in cognition among children with SCD. Of note, it has been reported that the CLC from the Bayley-III is inflated compared with the MDI from the BSID-II.33,44 It is recommended that scores less than 80 on the Bayley-III CLC be considered below average (compared with <70 on the BSID-II MDI).33,44

Environmental variables affect child development

Although this review intended to look at developmental outcomes directly measured among infants and toddlers with SCD, we also noted the important contribution of the home environment to child development. Two studies used the Infant-Toddler HOME Inventory45 and reported an association between the total score and the number of learning materials with higher scores in cognitive development on the Bayley-III.22,41 Two studies used the Home Screening Questionnaire46 and reported a higher-quality home environment (e.g. available learning materials, care-giver involvement) to be associated with greater communication development.38,40 The fifth study used the Knowledge of Infant Development Inventory,47 the Family Environment Scale,48 and Brief Symptom Inventory49 to describe the caregiver relationship component of the child’s environment and found that poorer caregiver knowledge of child development was associated with increased levels of caregiver distress.16

DISCUSSION

This review examined the research literature on development of young children (0–48mo) with SCD and identified that developmental delays are more prevalent than in normative populations in the first years of life. Developmental delays were primarily recognized in cognition and secondarily in language/communication. The studies included in this review report that developmental delay is identified in the first year of life and becomes increasingly apparent by 2 years of age. Most studies reported no significant difference in the frequency of delay across SCD genotypes.

Among children with SCD, the home environment has been strongly associated with caregiver stress,50 and stress has been linked to caregiver knowledge of child development.16 Notably, several studies described an association between components of the child’s environment and developmental progress.16,22,40,41 Thus, there is a strong need for family-centered assessments and interventions focused on education regarding both SCD and child development.

At this time, screening for developmental delay is not broadly integrated into standard care for SCD. The specific deficits in cognition and language indicate that developmental screening for children with SCD should start at birth and continue routinely throughout childhood. Recently published guidelines by the American Society of Hematology endorse surveillance and screening of this vulnerable population,51 consistent with the guidelines of the American Academy of Pediatrics.52

Only one study reported an intervention for developmental delay in SCD; this intervention focused on homebased caregiver education.22 Given the early onset of developmental delays and their association with academic outcomes, more work is needed to develop interventions as well as to identify the best methods for screening and referring children to therapeutic interventions.20

Current developmental screening recommendations support the use of caregiver report measures,53,54 yet no studies included in this review used a caregiver report assessment. Two studies used a screening tool (rather than a more comprehensive assessment), but these were administered by a trained provider and not solely caregiver report.17,37 Screening instruments may not have the sensitivity required to identify early indicators of developmental delay, and more thorough evaluation (i.e. using tools such as the Bayley-III) may be necessary. Future research in early childhood development in SCD should consist of both caregiver report screening tools as well as a more comprehensive measure to determine the most efficient and thorough approach. Establishing the validity of developmental screening and evaluation measures for children with SCD before they reach school age is an instrumental first step to enable providers to better appreciate the mechanisms attributed to developmental delay and provide targeted intervention. Additional areas of screening and assessment should include caregiver mental health, SCD knowledge, and the home environment.

Results from this review indicate that children with SCD are at an increased risk of developmental delay, and early and frequent screening is necessary. To address this need, we propose a screening protocol based on guidelines from the American Society of Hematology to standardize measures used across studies to allow for improved comparison and longitudinal analysis.51 On the basis of the current available evidence and considerations for the caregiver and child, we recommend regular screening or evaluation of young children with SCD using the Ages and Stages Questionnaire37 or Bayley-III.16,22,41–43 Additional measures such as the Parenting Stress Index50 may be used to evaluate the magnitude of stress in the parent–child relationship. A screening tool, such as the Ages and Stages Questionnaire, Third Edition, could be used to efficiently describe early developmental milestones in the first years of life.37 A more comprehensive measure (such as the Bayley-III) may be required for older children or to more formally evaluate development if the screening tool identifies concerns. Developmental screening alone will not improve outcomes for children with SCD. Children with SCD who are identified as at risk of developmental delay on the basis of screening should be expediently referred to appropriate early intervention services. Currently, referrals to early intervention remain low among children with SCD,55 and most eligible children do not receive these beneficial services.34,56

We believe our literature search identified nearly all the studies relevant to child development among young children with SCD. No additional studies were identified through secondary sources, but we acknowledge that some studies may have been overlooked. We included studies that included a developmental outcome measure with children between 0 months and 48 months of age with SCD. We excluded titles on the basis of the type of publication (abstracts and dissertations), which may have limited additional data sources. Several papers included in this review reported data from the same cohort of children, introducing a possible risk of bias. Multiple assessments from the same cohort are a considerable limitation of this review. Additionally, in most of the included studies, it was not possible for those administering assessments to be blinded to the child’s SCD diagnosis and is another substantial risk of bias that must be considered when interpreting findings. Although we followed published guidelines for converting Bayley-III scores to be equivalent to the MDI of the BSID-II,33 there is considerable concern that the MDI of the Bayley-III substantially underestimates the rate of developmental delay.33,57 In addition, one study included in this analysis had a larger sample than other studies and excluded children with substantial developmental delays.42 Therefore, it is possible that the rate of cognitive delay among children with SCD is actually higher than indicated by our results.

CONCLUSION

Our review identified 10 studies addressing development in children between 0 months and 48 months of age with SCD and five that could be included in this analysis. Most studies documented a larger proportion of developmental delay in this population than is seen in normative or reference populations for standardized measures. Results from this study implore clinical providers to adopt routine and standardized screening and referral strategies to identify children with SCD at risk of delay at a young age and provide appropriate referrals to therapeutic intervention.

What this paper adds

  • Developmental delay occurs in up to half of children with sickle cell disease (SCD).

  • Cognitive delay is the most commonly identified type of developmental delay across all SCD genotypes.

  • Caregiver mental health and the home environment are associated with developmental achievement across all SCD genotypes.
