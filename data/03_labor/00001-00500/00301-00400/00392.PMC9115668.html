Risk Factors for Hypervascularization in Hepatobiliary Phase Hypointense Nodules without Arterial Phase Hyperenhancement: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

Abstract

Rationale and Objectives:

To perform a systematic review and meta-analysis to determine risk factors for hypervascularization in hepatobiliary phase (HBP) hypointense nodules without arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE) in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

Materials and Methods:

Pubmed and EMBASE databases were searched up to May 7, 2020. Studies which evaluated radiologic and clinical risk factors for hypervascularization in HBP hypointense nodules without APHE were included. Hazard ratios were meta-analytically pooled using random-effects model. Methodological quality of included studies was assessed using Quality in Prognostic Studies (QUIPS) tool.

Results:

Sixteen studies with 934 patients were included. HBP hypointense nodules without APHE with baseline size greater than 10 mm, T2 hyperintensity, and restricted diffusion showed risk for hypervascularization with pooled HRs of 2.95 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.94–4.20), 4.21 (95% CI, 1.15–15.40), 5.83 (95% CI, 1.42–23.95), respectively. Previous HCC history contributed to hypervascularization of the nodules with hazard ratio of 2.06 (95% CI, 1.23–3.44). T1 hyperintensity, intralesional fat, Child-Pugh Class B, sex, alfa-fetoprotein, hepatitis B or C infection were not significant risk factors for hypervascularization (p ≥0.05). Study quality was generally moderate.

Conclusion:

HBP hypointense nodules without APHE on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI with baseline size greater than 10 mm, T2 hyperintensity, restricted diffusion and previous hepatocellular carcinoma history pose higher risk for hypervascularization. Proper patient management in patients with HBP hypointense nodules without APHE on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI may need to be tailored according to these risk factors.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common cancer and the second largest cause of cancer mortality worldwide (1). Imaging surveillance for HCC is vital in the management of patients with chronic liver diseases as HCC can be diagnosed solely on the basis of imaging criteria (2). Recently, guidelines such as European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) (3) and American Association for the Study of Liver Disease (AASLD) (4) were updated to include the use of gadoxetic acid-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in the diagnostic algorithm for HCC.

Gadoxetic acid (Gd-EOB-DTPA, Primovist or Eovist, Bayer Healthcare) is a gadolinium-based MRI contrast agent which is used to acquire hepatobiliary phase (HBP) imaging in addition to conventional dynamic postcontrast phases. HBP is a unique postcontrast phase enabling sensitive detection of hepatic lesions such as HCCs that lack normal hepatocytes, appearing as hypointense lesions (5). HBP hypointense nodules without arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE) have been an area of active research and studies have demonstrated that not only do these generally correspond to a wide spectrum of hepatic lesions from high-grade dysplastic nodules to even progressed HCCs on histopathology (6–8), but that they also are markers for increased development of HCC elsewhere in the liver (9), and a risk factor for recurrence after curative treatment for HCC (10).

Currently, there is controversy on what the proper management of the HBP hypointense nodules without APHE is (i.e., watchful surveillance, biopsy, or active treatment). Nevertheless, it is clear that hypervascularization occurs in non-negligible (nearly one third) portion of these nodules with increasing cumulative incidence rates over time (11) and that hypervascularization, at least in part, reflect neoarterialization and progression to overt HCC (6,12). In the same context, Liver Imaging-Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS), a comprehensive system for standardized interpretation for liver imaging endorsed by the American College of Radiology, require APHE as an essential feature to diagnose any hepatic lesion as a definite HCC (13).

Identifying risk factors for hypervascularization in HBP hypointense nodules without APHE will be beneficial in improving assessment of patients at risk for HCC, for example, by tailoring optimal follow-up intervals or the necessity of additional diagnostic workup or even for prompt treatment depending on the calculated risk (14). Initial nodule size greater than 10 mm is one of the better known imaging-based risk factors for hypervascularization (15–21). There are a few other potential risk factors that are less well established (13,16,22–28) as they were only evaluated in a few studies with a small number of patients, some even with conflicting results between different papers. Therefore, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to determine the risk factors for hypervascularization in HBP hypointense nodules without APHE.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was performed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (29). A research query was formulated based on modification of the patient, index test, comparator, outcome, and study design (PICOS) criteria as follows: “What are the risk factors for hypervascularization in patients with HBP hypointense nodules without APHE, in original research articles?”

Literature Search

A systematic search in PubMed and Embase databases up to May 7, 2020 was conducted. Keywords and their synonyms or relevant descriptions were included in the search terms as the following: (gadoxetic OR gadoxetate OR “gadolinium ethoxybenzyl diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid” OR “gd-eob-dtpa” OR “hepatobiliary agent” OR Primovist OR Eovist) AND (“non-hypervascular” OR nonhypervascular OR hypovascular OR hypointense OR hypodense). The bibliographies of included studies were reviewed to find other eligible studies. No limitation to any particular language was applied.

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they fulfilled the following PICOS criteria (30): (1) patients with HBP hypointense nodules on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI; (2) index test as radiologic factors on MRI or clinical factors (i.e., serum alfa-feto protein [AFP] or, history of previous HCC); (3) no comparator relevant to this meta-analysis; and (4) outcome as hypervascularization; and (5) publication original research articles.

Exclusion Criteria

The studies were excluded if (1) study population less than ten patients; (2) overlap in study population; (3) publication other than original articles; (4) gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI used as HCC surveillance, but focused on other topics; and (5) insufficient data to find association between risk factors and hypervascularization. If studies with an overlapping population were identified, study providing a greater number of relevant data in regards to the risk factors for hypervascularization was included.

Two reviewers (T.H.K with 2 years and S.W. with 4 years of experience in meta-analysis, respectively) independently conducted the literature search and study selection. When disagreement was present, consensus was reached with a third reviewer (J.M.L with more than 20 years of experience in liver imaging).

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

The following data regarding patient, study and MRI characteristics were extracted using a standardized form: (1) patient characteristics – total number, mean age and range of patients, underlying risk factor for HCC development, number of total HBP hypointense nodules without APHE and those which showed subsequent hypervascularization, mean size and range of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE, and overall cumulative hypervascularization rates at 1, 2, and 3 years. (2) study characteristics – origin of study (authors, institution and country), publication year, duration of patient enrollment, study design (whether they were retrospective, performed at multiple centers, or consecutively enrolled), and follow-up duration. (3) MRI characteristics – magnet field strength, scanner manufacturer and model, MRI interpretation (number and experience of MRI readers), method of diffusion weighted image (DWI) sequence and whether restricted diffusion was determined by a quantitatively or qualitatively, and the assessed risk features were included.

Quality in Prognostic Studies (QUIPS) tool was used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies (31). Both data extraction and quality assessment were performed independently by the same two reviewers (T.H.K. and S.W.) with disagreement resolved by a third reviewer (S.H. with 3 years of experience in meta-analysis).

Data Synthesis and Analysis

The outcomes were hypervascularization of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE and hazard ratios (HRs) were used as the effect sizes. Risk factors were either stratified as radiological or clinical risk factors and only those which were investigated in more than three studies were meta-analytically pooled. For each risk factor, the HRs for hypervascularization with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from Univariate Cox regression were collected. When the Kaplan-Meier curves were provided, indirect calculation of HRs and their CIs was performed using Engauge Digitizer (32), based on the methodology proposed by Tierney et al (33). Random effects model for meta-analytic pooling of HRs and the Higgins I2 (34) for heterogeneity between studies were used, respectively. Statistical analyses were conducted using the “meta” package in R software version 3.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with p < 0.05 showing statistical significance.

RESULTS

Literature Search

We identified 473 articles from the initial systematic literature. After removing 168 duplicates, screening of the 305 titles and abstracts yielded 44 potentially eligible studies. Full-text reviews were conducted and 27 studies were not included for the following reasons: not in the field of interest (n = 17), insufficient data (n = 7), and patient overlap (n = 3). Extended search from bibliographies of included studies yielded one additional study eligible for our meta-analysis (23).

Total of 18 original articles (13,15–28,35–37) which evaluated the risk factors for hypervascularization in HBP hypointense nodules without APHE were included for the qualitative synthesis and among them, two studies evaluated unique risk factors not dealt in other studies (superparamagnetic iron oxide uptake (36) and anti-HCV therapy (37), and therefore were excluded in the quantitative synthesis. Finally, 16 original articles (13,15–28,35) were analyzed for quantitative synthesis in the current meta-analysis. The detailed study selection process is shown in Figure 1.

Characteristics of Included Studies

Patient characteristics are described in Table 1. Total number and mean age of patients ranged from 24 to 135 and from 56.9 to 71.4, respectively. The underlying risk factor for HCC included alcohol, hepatitis B virus or C virus infection (HBV/HCV), liver cirrhosis and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis. The proportion of hypervascularization of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE ranged from 11.9% to 81.6%. Mean size of initial HBP hypointense nodules without APHE ranged from 7.8 to 11.5 mm. Median and range of overall cumulative hypervascularization rates at 1, 2, and 3 years were 16.2% (3.2%–73.5%), 27.8% (11.1%–92.9%), and Study 35.0% (15.9%–57.7%), respectively

Study characteristics are described in Table 2. All studies characteristics were retrospective in design, consisted of an Asian cohort, and had consecutive enrollment of the patients (centers13,15–28,35) Only one study was performed at multiple centers (24); others were conducted at single centers. The median of follow-up duration ranged from 180 to 886 days. All studies assessed hypervascularization in either dynamic computed tomography (CT) or MRI and five studies further included other modalities such as CT arterioportography, CT hepatic arteriography, or contrast enhanced ultrasound (18,19,21,24,28).

MRI characteristics and assessed imaging and clinical risk features are described in Table 3. In all but one study (17), more than two readers assessed the imaging features. Diverse methods for DWI were used among four studies which evaluated DWI for the hypervascularization risk assessment; not specified (22), free breathing single shot echo planar sequence (35), respiratory-triggered single shot echo plannar sequence (26), navigator-triggered prospective acquisition correction or free breathing method (16). Restricted diffusion was determined qualitatively in two studies (16, 26) and not explicitly explained in other two studies (22,35). The assessed MRI imaging features varied among studies with the size of nodules being the most commonly analyzed feature in 14 studies (13,15–22,24,26–28,35), followed by intralesional fat in eight studies (16,23–28; 35), T1WI (16,22,23,26,27) and T2WI (13,22,24,26,35) in five studies each, and restricted diffusion in four studies (16,22,26,35). Features which were evaluated in less than two studies were hypointensity on portal venous phase, multiplicity of nodules, gadoxetic acid contrast-enhancement ratio, noise-to-liver contrast on T1WI or HBP, degree of hypointensity at HBP, and growth rate (23,24,26,35).

Quality Assessment

The risk of bias evaluation using QUIPS tool is presented in Figure 2. For study participation domain, one study showed a moderate risk as it included two patients whose HBP hypointense nodules without APHE were not defined in MRI but in dynamic CT (17). Low risk of bias was assigned in all studies for study attrition domain. Regarding prognostic factor measurement domain, seven studies showed moderate risk as these studies were unclear whether MRI analyses were performed in a blinded manner (13,17,18,20,22,25,28). For outcome measurement domain, moderate risk in 15 studies (13,15–19,21–28,35), and high risk in one study (20) was assigned for the following reasons; all included studies did not specify whether hypervascularization was determined without prior information on the radiological or clinical risk factors, therefore, raising concerns for incorporation bias, and one study defined malignant transformation of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE as nodules showing not only hypervascularization, but also enlargement during follow-up (20). In terms of study confounding domain, four studies which did not perform multivariate analysis showed moderate risk of bias (15,17,19,21). For statistical analysis and reporting domain, moderate risk was assigned in two studies which included all variables in multivariate analysis, raising concern for the possible multicollinearity (18,22).

Radiological Risk Factors for Hypervascularization

Pooled HRs of the radiological risk factors for hypervascularization are summarized in Figure 3. The pooled HRs were 2.95 (95% CI, 1.94–4.20; p < 0.01) in seven studies that evaluated baseline size of the nodule, six of which used a cutoff of 10 mm (15,17–19,21,35) and the other using 9 mm (20), with insignificant heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 20%).

Risks for hypervascularization were shown in nodules with T2 hyperintensity (4.21 [95% CI, 1.15–15.40; p = 0.03]), or with restricted diffusion (5.83 [95% CI, 1.42–23.95; p = 0.01]). There was no statistical significant risk for hypervascularization in nodules with T1 hyperintensity (1.76 [95% CI, 0.98–3.16; p = 0.06]) or with intralesional fat (1.44 [95% CI, 0.84–2.46; p = 0.19]). Considerable heterogeneity was present among studies assessing radiological risk factors other than size for hypervascularization (I2 = 46%–96 %).

Clinical Risk Factors for Hypervascularization

Figure 4 summarizes the pooled HRs of the clinical risk factors for hypervascularization. HBP hypointense nodules without APHE in patients with previous HCC history showed higher risk for hypervascularization (HR = 2.06 [95% CI, 1.23–3.44; p < 0.01]). There was no statistically significant risk for hypervascularization in patients with HCV (1.47 [95% CI, 0.99–2.18; p = 0.05]) or Child-Pugh Class B (1.47 [95% CI, 0.68–3.21; p = 0.33]) in five studies (16,18,24,27,35). The results for other clinical risk factors showed no statistical significance (p = 0.39–0.80); high AFP (1.27 [95% CI, 0.74–2.21]) in three studies (18,24,35), HBV infection (0.85 [95% CI, 0.26–2.83]) in three studies (18,27,35), and male sex (1.21 [95% CI, 0.53–2.75]) in three studies (13,22,35). Heterogeneity varied among studies assessing clinical risk factors for hypervascularization (I2 = 0–75 %).

DISCUSSION

In this meta-analysis, we systematically reviewed and analyzed proposed risk factors in the literature for hypervascularization in HBP hypointense nodules without APHE. We found that the nodules with size greater than 10 mm, T2 hyperintensity, or restricted diffusion on MRI and previous HCC history were significant risk factors for hypervascularization. HBP hypointense nodules without APHE has been known to represent mostly borderline nodules including precancerous lesion (i.e., high-grade dysplastic nodule), and early HCC, but may include even progressed HCC on pathology (6–8). Although previous studies demonstrated that the presence of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE were a significant risk factor for recurrence in patients who underwent surgery or radiofrequency ablation for HCC (10), there is no consensus as to how to manage these nodules on gadoxeticacid MRI and pathologic diagnosis for these nodules is not always feasible as biopsy carries risks, including hemorrhage, infection or needle tract seeding of cancer. Considering APHE on imaging is essential in the definite diagnosis for HCC, recognizing risk factors for hypervascularization of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE would support clinicians to decide either modified follow-up strategies or prompt treatment according to the risk stratification.

MRI is useful for the diagnosis of HCC as it provides additional information regarding tissue components and cellularity of hepatic nodules compared to other imaging modalities such as CT or ultrasound (38). For instance, T2 hyperintensity, which reflects dilated intratumoral sinusoids in HCC (39), and restricted diffusion, which correlates with hypercellularity and tumor grade (6,40), were significant risk factors for hypervascularization in HBP hypointense nodules without APHE, in concordance with previous studies (13,16,24,26,35). Mild to moderate T2 hyperintensity and restricted diffusion are ancillary features suggesting malignancy according to LI-RADS, and many investigators have tried to differentiate HCC from dysplastic nodule using these imaging features (41,42). Therefore, when HBP hypointense nodules without APHE accompany intermediate T2 hyperintensity or restricted diffusion, meticulous and close observation is warranted not only as these are features suggesting malignancy but also with high risks for subsequent hypervascularization.

In this meta-analysis, we found that nodule size greater than 10 mm at baseline was a significant risk factor for hypervascularization (HR = 2.95 [95% CI, 1.94–4.20; p < 0.01]). Our result is in agreement with a recent meta-analysis which reported the pooled proportion of hypervascularized HBP hypointense nodules without APHE was 19.5% in nodules smaller than 9 mm and 33.7% in nodules larger than 9 mm, respectively (11). Similarly, higher proportion for the hypervascularization was reported in HBP hypointense nodules with more than 10 mm in the initial MRI examination in additional studies that were not included in our meta-analysis as they did not provide HRs (43,44). This may reflect the fact that active proliferation owing to dedifferentiation and unpaired artery development occurs after HCC reach certain tumor size (45). In this regard, increased growth rate of the nodule during follow up has also been reported to be a significant risk for hypervascularization, as tumor growth is a hemodynamic and pathological progression of multistep hepatocarcinoagenesis (23,26). Therefore, when encountering HBP hypointense nodules without APHE more than 10 mm in size, tailored approaches with shorter term around 3-month follow-up may be required as it poses higher risks for hypervascularization (4).

There was no statistical significance regarding increased risks for nodules with T1 hyperintensity (p = 0.06) or intralesional fat (p = 0.19) in current meta-analysis. During hepatocarcinoagenesis, T1 signal change and fat accumulation are known to be dynamic processes (46); DNs usually show T1 hyperintensity owing to iron or copper accumulation and T1 signal drops to iso-to hypointensity in early HCCs and progressed HCCs. Fat accumulates focally or diffusely in low grade DN, high-grade DN, and peaks in the early HCC and regresses in the progressed HCC. Considering wide range of pathologic diagnosis of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE from benign nodules to even progressed HCC (6–8), statistical insignificance may be due to varying signal intensities regarding T1 or fat within different pathologies.

There were a few clinical features evaluated as risks for hypervascularization and previous history of HCC was the only clinical risk for hypervascularization in HBP hypointense nodules without APHE in current study (HR = 2.06, p < 0.01). Levels of AFP (high vs. low) failed to be a significant risk for hypervascularization in current study. Although AFP is widely used as a biomarker for HCC surveillance, it has poor sensitivity for HCC detection (49%–71%) and considering hypervascularization is part of a dynamic carcinogenesis, strategy of assessing trends of AFP measurements may be superior to a one-time measurement (47). Furthermore, the cutoff value of AFP varied among the three studies (20, 50, and 100 ng/mL) (18,24,35). No statistical difference in hypervascularization of the nodules was found between patients with HBV or HCV infection and those without. As there are other etiologies contributing to HCC development, such as alcohol or fatty liver disease, further studies assessing the risks for hypervascularization in those etiologies may give further insight for further risk stratification.

Our meta-analysis had some limitations. First, although we analyzed the risks for hypervascularization in HBP hypointense nodules without APHE, exact pathological explanation for the hypervascularization of the nodules may not be clear yet. Most of previous studies have equated hypervascularization of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE as development of HCCs from premalignant lesions such as high grade DNs (13, 15–17, 19–21, 23, 24, 26, 28, 35). In line with this, biopsy results confirmed all nine HBP hypointense nodules which showed hypervascularization to be HCCs in one study (24). However, according to the recent study by Joo et al. (8), substantial portion of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE (44%, 147 of 334 nodules) were already progressed HCCs and nodules with T2 hyperintensity or restricted diffusion or hypointensity on T1-weighted image were more likely to be progressed HCC. Considering the technical challenges of gadoxetic acid enhanced MRI in acquiring high quality arterial phase imaging, due to transient dyspnea or lower contrast dose compared with extracellular gadolinium contrast agent (48), we cannot rule out the possibility that nodules which showed subsequent hypervascularization were already HCCs which failed to demonstrate APHE initially for technical reasons. Furthermore, in patients with prior HCCs, it may be intuitive to assume that hypervascularization correlates with HCC development from premalignant lesions from increased background hepatocarcinoagenesis, but another possibility includes intrahepatic HCC metastases that initially manifested as HBP hypointense nodules without APHE (49). Therefore, to elucidate the pathologic explanation for the hypervascularization of HBP hypointense nodules without APHE, whether it is progression to HCC from premalignant lesions or manifestation of progressed HCC or intrahepatic HCC metastases which failed to show APHE on the initial MRI scan, additional imaging technique such as CEUS with higher sensitivity to detect APHE may have a role in the near future. For instances, when CEUS demonstrate APHE among HBP hypointense nodules without APHE, prompt biopsy may be required in order to rule out HCCs which failed to show APHE on gadoxetic acid MRI scan. Second, the clinical significance and implications of hypervascularization of these nodules remain unclear. Although several translational studies have reported that angiogenesis or vascularity is related to aggressiveness and prognosis of HCC (50), we cannot assume that this process is equivalent to the hypervascularization of HBP hypointense nodules. Furthermore, we do not know if earlier diagnosis or treatment of these nodules at higher risk of hypervascularization will result in better clinical outcomes. We expect a prospective clinical trial where HBP hypointense nodules without APHE with imaging risk factors for hypervascularization are biopsied or ablated in patients with prior HCC history, to find the clinical significance and the impact on the managements of patients harboring these nodules at risk. Third, there was considerable heterogeneity among included studies, except for nodule size and HCV infection. We speculate that part of this heterogeneity may stem from inter-reader variability. For instance, T1 hyperintensity, intralesional fat, moderate T2 hyperintensity or restricted diffusion are subjective whereas size is relatively more objective. Indeed, inter-reader agreement is almost perfect regarding tumor size, compared to other categories, in previous studies evaluating inter-reader variability of LI-RADS categories (51, 52). Heterogeneity in meta-analysis may prohibit general application of the results and therefore, interpretation should be made cautiously. Lastly, all included studies (n = 16) were retrospective in design based on only Asian cohorts. Considering expected widespread use with the recent approval for gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI in the routine HCC diagnosis in western guidelines such as EASL and AASLD (3, 4), further prospective studies with non-Asian cohorts may be needed to confirm our results.

In conclusion, HBP hypointense nodules without APHE on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI with baseline size greater than 10 mm, T2 hyperintensity, restricted diffusion and previous history of HCC are significant risk factors for hypervascularization. These risk factors could be taken into account when planning a proper patient management in patients with HBP hypointense nodules without APHE on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI.
