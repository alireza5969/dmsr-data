Changes From Baseline in Patient-Reported Outcomes at 1 Year Versus 2 Years After Rotator Cuff Repair:

A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

Abstract

Background:

Most orthopaedic journals currently require reporting outcomes of surgical interventions for at least 2 postoperative years, but there have been no rigorous studies on this matter. Various patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures (PROMs) have been used to assess the status of the shoulder after rotator cuff repair (RCR).

Hypothesis:

We hypothesized that the mean shoulder-specific PROMs at 1 year improve substantially over baseline but that there is no clinically meaningful difference between the mean 1- and 2-year PROMs after RCR.

Study Design:

Meta-analysis; Level of evidence, 2.

Methods:

We conducted a systematic review of published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective cohort studies (level of evidence 1 and 2) reporting the shoulder-specific American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES), the Constant, or the Western Ontario Rotator Cuff (WORC) Index scores at baseline, 1 year, and 2 years after RCR. The methodologic quality of studies was assessed. Also, the random effects meta-analyses of changes in PROMs for each of the first and second post-operative years were conducted.

Results:

Fifteen studies (n = 11 RCTs; n = 4 cohort studies) with a total of 1371 patients were included. Studies were highly heterogeneous, but no visual evidence of major publication bias was observed. The weighted means of the baseline PROMs were 46.2 points for the ASES score, 46.4 points for the Constant score, and 38.8 points for the WORC Index. The first-year summary increments were 41.1 (95% CI, 36.0–46.2) points for the ASES score, 34.2 (95% CI, 28.8–39.6) points for the Constant score, and 42.9 (95% CI, 37.3–48.4) points for the WORC Index. In contrast, the second-year summary increments were 2.3 (95% CI, 1–3.6) points for the ASES score, 3.2 (95% CI, 1.9–4.4) points for the Constant score, and 2 (95% CI, −0.1 to 4) points for the WORC Index.

Conclusion:

All PROMs improved considerably from baseline to 1 year, but only very small gains that were below the minimal clinically important differences were observed between 1 year and 2 years after RCR. This study did not find any evidence for requiring a minimum of 2 years of follow-up for publication of PROs after RCR. Our results suggest that focusing on 1-year PROMs after RCR would foster more timely reporting, better control of selection bias, and better allocation of research resources.

Rotator cuff tears are a significant cause of shoulder pain and dysfunction and are present in approximately 30% of patients aged 70 years.45,55 Rotator cuff repair (RCR) is often recommended for full-thickness and high-grade partial-thickness rotator cuff tears that fail to respond to nonoperative treatment. More than 250,000 RCRs are performed annually in the United States,10 with the number of cases steadily increasing over the past 3 decades.13 Both imaging-based structural outcome measures and clinical outcome measures can be used to assess surgical outcomes after RCR, but imaging is costly and therefore not routinely performed as the standard of care. Furthermore, with emphasis on the standardized assessment of the outcome and value-based health care, there has been a shift in focus toward patient experience and engagement in the evaluation of surgical outcomes. As a result, reporting and emphasis on patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) has become commonplace.

PROMs offer a means to reliably quantify a patient’s assessment of his or her mental, physical, and/or functional condition. Over 30 different PROMs have been developed and validated to specifically assess the status of the shoulder,2,18 of which the American Shoulder and Elbow Society (ASES) score, the Constant-Murley (often referred to simply as Constant) score, and the Western Ontario Rotator Cuff (WORC) Index18,31,39 have been most commonly used in the rotator cuff literature. The ASES score, which has been adopted by the ASES, combines a physician assessment portion (that assesses range of motion, clinical signs, strength, and instability) with a patient self-reported portion (that assesses shoulder pain and function), but most recent studies have used the patient self-reported portion alone. The Constant score is another frequently used shoulder-specific PROM that assesses shoulder pain, activities of daily living, range of motion, and shoulder strength.31,39 Similarly, the WORC Index is a rotator cuff disease specific PROM that assesses physical symptoms, sports/recreation, work, and lifestyle aspects related to the shoulder and appears to have the best psychometric properties among shoulder-specific PROMs.18 The smallest detectable change in a PROM score that is deemed relevant or meaningful is called its minimal clinically important difference (MCID). Changes in PROMs over time are often benchmarked against the MCID when evaluating interventions, although reported MCID values for these shoulder-specific PROMs after RCR have varied widely, from 11.1 to 26.9 points for ASES scores,11,14 4.6 to 10.4 points for Constant scores,11,27 and 13.4 to 28 points for the WORC Index.14

Currently, most orthopaedic journals require the reporting of PROMs and other clinical outcomes at least 2 years after any surgical intervention. However, collecting outcomes beyond a year of follow-up is expensive and difficult,54 with high rates of attrition,9,23 and there have been no rigorous studies to address whether the extended follow-up duration is indeed needed. Most retears after RCR occur in the first 6 postoperative months, and by 1 year most patients reach plateaus in range of motion and function.24,35 Studies in other orthopaedic specialties have shown no clinically meaningful differences in PROMs between 1 year and 2 years after lower extremity arthroplasty43 and anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction,49 and suggest that when PROMs are of primary clinical interest, 1-year scores may be sufficient for treatment evaluations.

We hypothesized that, as seen after other orthopaedic repair procedures, PROMs at 1 year after RCR substantially improve from baseline but that no further clinically meaningful improvement occurs between 1 year and 2 years. To test our hypothesis, we conducted a systematic review of articles reporting on at least 1 of the above 3 shoulder-specific PROMs at baseline, 1 year, and 2 years after RCR, and performed meta-analyses of changes in PROMs for each of the first and second postoperative years.

METHODS

This study was designed and conducted in accordance with the 2009 PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement.34

Search Strategy

PubMed, Web of Science, and EMBASE databases were searched in June 2020 for prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on RCR that obtained at least 1 of the following scores: ASES, Constant, or WORC. The search was conducted using key words “prospective,” “rotator cuff repair,” “ASES,” “Constant,” and “WORC,” and a hedge term for RCTs was added in a second PubMed search. The search was limited to studies of humans published in English. All titles and abstracts were reviewed, and full articles were retrieved for all RCTs and prospective cohort studies, including any clinically relevant outcome.

Eligibility Criteria

We included RCTs and prospective cohort studies (level of evidence [LOE] 1 and 2) investigating RCR, published in peer-reviewed journals, with (1) minimum follow-up duration of at least 2 years, (2) minimum study size of at least 15 patients in each treatment group, and (3) reporting of means and standard deviations of ASES, Constant, or WORC scores for each treatment group at baseline, 12 + 3 months, and 24 + 6 months after operation. Studies that failed any of these inclusion criteria were excluded, as were unpublished abstracts.

Study Selection

The titles and abstracts of all papers that met the database search criteria were initially triaged for inclusion, with uncertainty resolved by full-text assessment. Two reviewers (S.S., V.E.) then independently assessed the full report of each potential study to make a final determination as to whether inclusion criteria were met. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with the senior statistician author (P.B.I.), when necessary. The name of journals, authors, and institutions was not masked at any stage.

Data Extraction

The relevant data from each included study were extracted and recorded into worksheet tables by 1 reviewer (S.S.) and subsequently verified by a second reviewer (V.E.). Data collected in the worksheets included the first author, the journal, and the year in which the study was published, study type, LOE, follow-up duration, percentage follow-up, number of patients in each group at each time point, patient demographic data (number of men and women, mean age), tear size (however reported), repair type, and additional surgical procedures (eg, arthroscopic or open RCR, single- vs double-row repair, additional acromioplasty, patch, or platelet-rich plasma [PRP] augmentation), the mean and standard deviation of relevant PROMs at baseline, 1-year, and 2-year follow-up, and a comments section for any other relevant data particular to each study.

Assessment of methodological quality

Three authors (S.S., M.S., V.E.) independently reviewed and scored the methodologic quality of included studies using the Checklist to Evaluate A Report of a Nonpharmacologic Trial (CLEAR-NPT)5 for RCTs and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for prospective cohort studies.7 Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. The CLEAR-NPT scale assesses 10 features (1. allocation sequence; 2. allocation concealment; 3. intervention description; 4. between comparator similarity of expertise; 5. compliance assessment; 6. participant blinding; 7. provider blinding; 8. outcome assessor blinding; 9. between-comparator similarity of follow-up; 10. Intention-to-treat analysis [“yes,” “no,” or “unclear”]). The NOS uses a star system (out of 9 stars) to allow a semiquantitative assessment of study quality for the following domains: selection (0–4 stars), comparability (0–2 stars), and outcome/exposure (0–3 stars). “Yes” scores on items and higher total scores on each scale represent higher study quality.

Statistical Analysis

Meta-analyses were performed on each PROM, treating parallel treatment arms as independent and presuming that missing data due to attrition and other mechanisms were “missing at random,” meaning that the process that determines whether a value is observed or missing is independent of its actual value. We summarized each measure at each annual examination across studies by baseline sample size weighted means to compensate for differential attrition and other missing data. For each study and outcome, we estimated the mean baseline to 1 year and 1- to 2-year differences as the respective differences in sample means between the later and earlier times. Standard errors of these differences were estimated by extrapolating Pearson correlation coefficients of consecutive annual assessments of each outcome in the same patients from correlations of sums of the Penn Shoulder Score (PSS) pain and function subscores analogous to the ASES, Constant, and WORC scores29 of a cohort of 312 RCR patients in a prospectively enrolled institutional database (Outcomes Management and Evaluation [OME]).37,47,48

A random-effects meta-analysis with the Sidik-Jonkman51,52 heterogeneity estimator was used for summarization of differences over time, with the HartungKnapp-Sidik-Jonkman16,26,50 standard error adjustment in test statistics and confidence intervals. Forest and funnel plots were used respectively to summarize the meta-analytic synthesis and to screen for publication bias. We explored sensitivity of results to changes in the Pearson correlation coefficients extrapolated from the OME cohort PSS (pain + function) subscores by repeating the analyses with alternative values of 0.5 and 0.5 + 0.4 × sgn (r = 0.5), where r is the observed value of the correlations of PSS (pain + function) subscores in the OME database as described above. Analyses were performed using the metafor56 R Version 3.5 (R Core Team).42 Significance tests were performed at the α = .05 level.

RESULTS

Literature Search

The literature search identified 1072 studies (Figure 1), of which 691 remained after removal of duplicate reports, and 288 after review of titles and abstracts. Full-text review yielded 16 studies, of which 1 study23 was excluded during data extraction because of internal inconsistencies in the published results, and the remaining 15 were included in the meta-analysis.

The 15 studies included 11 RCTs (8 LOE-1, 3 LOE-2) and 4 cohort studies (all LOE-2) with a total of 1371 patients. Table 1 summarizes their key characteristics. Patients in the studies from 10 countries had mean ages ranging from 54 to 68 years, varying tear sizes, and predominantly underwent single- or double-row repairs using an arthroscopic technique, although 1 study35 included open transosseous repairs. ASES scores were reported in 10 studies, Constant scores in 12, and WORC scores in 3. The fractions of patients lacking ASES, Constant, and WORC scores were small to modest at 1-year (4%, 4%, and 20%, respectively) and 2-year (4%, 7%, and 14%, respectively) follow-up.

Quality Assessment of Included Studies

Quality assessment of the 11 included RCTs using the CLEAR-NPT criteria is summarized in Appendix Table A1 (available in the online version of this article). Studies generally incorporated appropriate generation and concealment of allocation sequence, reporting of intervention details, similar expertise in each arm, participant blinding, outcome assessor blinding, and similar follow-up in each arm. However, most did not use intention-to-treat analysis. Assessment of treatment adherence was not relevant for most studies (because of the single-shot nature of RCR surgery), and surgeons were not blinded in any study (because of the obvious nature of surgical intervention and additional treatments, such as acromioplasty,1,30 patch augmentation,3,6 and PRP administration).21,32,40,44

Quality assessment of the 4 included cohort studies using the NOS criteria is summarized in Appendix Table A2 (available online). These generally exhibited appropriate exposure ascertainment, exclusion of those with prevalent outcomes at baseline, exposed cohort representativeness, nonexposed cohort selection, cohort comparability, and appropriate follow-up coverage, duration, and outcome assessment.

Differences in Baseline vs 1-Year and 1- Year vs 2-Year PROMs

Table 2 summarizes the shoulder-specific PROMs values (mean, SD) and patient numbers at baseline, 1 year and 2 years across the 12 studies. Overall, the reported group means of baseline PROMs ranged from 25 to 55 points for the ASES score, 30 to 48 points for the Constant score, and 35 to 48 points for the WORC Index in the patients investigated in these studies; the weighted means of the baseline PROMs were 46.2, 46.4, and 38.8 points, respectively. Correlations extrapolated from PSS subscores in the prospective database were 0.27 for baseline to 1 year and 0.74 for 1- to 2-year PROMs, leading to sensitivity analysis of 0.1 and 0.5 for the first-year and 0.5 and 0.9 for the second-year changes.

Figures 2 through 4 display forest plots summarizing meta-analyses of the mean differences between baseline and 1-year and between 1-year and 2-year for the ASES, Constant, and WORC scores, respectively, from the subsets of studies reporting each. The summary mean differences between baseline and 1-year PROMs were 41.1 (95% CI, 36 to 46.2) points for the ASES score (Figure 2A), 34.2 (95% CI, 28.8 to 39.6) points for the Constant score (Figure 3A), and 42.9 (95% CI, 37.3 to 48.4) points for the WORC Index (Figure 3A). In contrast, the mean differences between 1-year and 2-year PROMs were 2.3 (95% CI, 1.0 to 3.6) points for the ASES score (Figure 2B), 3.2 (95% CI, 1.9 to 4.4) points for the Constant score (Figure 3B), and 2 (95% CI, −0.1 to 4) points for the WORC Index (Figure 4B).

Heterogeneity was statistically significant and extraordinarily high for differences between baseline and the 1-year ASES score (I2 = 95%; P < .001) and the Constant score (I2 = 98%; P < .001), but not the WORC Index (I2 = 38%; P = .24). Similarly, heterogeneity was also statistically significant and extremely high for differences between the 1-year and 2-year ASES score (I2 = 72%; P < .001) and the Constant score (I2 = 89%; P < .001), but not the WORC Index (I2 = 7%; P = .83). For example, most prominently, 2 studies showed low interpatient variability, and particularly large baseline to 1-year improvements in Constant6,40 and ASES40 scores P, and 1 study23 showed both the smallest first-year and the largest second-year change in the Constant score. Each PROM, however, showed considerably larger first- than second-year changes in all arms of all studies.

The funnel plots shown in Appendix Figures A1–A3 (available online) demonstrate no visual evidence of major publication bias for 1-year versus 2-year changes of any PROM, with data points being reasonably symmetrically distributed around the mean differences, although they exhibit unusually large study-to-study variability among the highest precision studies, reflecting the heterogeneity noted above. The funnel plots of baseline versus 1-year ASES and Constant scores are difficult to interpret. In each, the 2 or 3 studies with the largest improvements and the least internal variability deviate substantially from the rest.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses using ρ of 0.1 and 0.5 for the first-year and 0.5 and 0.9 for the second-year changes did not meaningfully alter the results, which still showed large differences between mean baseline and 1-year PROMs (~41 points on the ASES score, ~34 points on the Constant score, and ~43 points on the WORC Index), and much smaller differences between mean 1-year and 2-year PROMs (~2 points on the ASES score, ~3 points on the Constant score, and ~2 points on the WORC Index). (See Appendix Figures A4–A6 [available online] for forest plots from the sensitivity analyses for ASES, Constant, and WORC scores.)

DISCUSSION

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs and prospective cohort studies reporting any of 3 shoulder-specific PROMs at baseline, 1 year, and 2 years after RCR. Fifteen studies (11 RCTs and 4 cohort studies), with 1371 patients, were included. As hypothesized, we observed large improvements in PROMs (~41 points on the ASES score, ~34 points on the Constant score, and ~43 points on the WORC Index) from baseline to 1 year, but much smaller gains (~2 points on the ASES score, ~3 points on the Constant score, and ~2 points on the WORC Index) between 1 year and 2 years. The increases in PROM values from baseline to 1 year after RCR are larger than the largest proposed MCIDs for the respective PROMs (11.1 to 26.9 points for ASES scores,11,14 4.6 to 10.4 points for Constant scores,11,27 and 13.4 to 28 points for the WORC Index14). Such a significant improvement in PROMs in the first year after RCR has been consistently documented and supports the strategy of surgical treatment of RCTs. On the other hand, the small changes (2–3 points) in PROMs between 1 year and 2 years after RCR are below the smallest proposed MCIDs and therefore likely not clinically important.

The PROMs investigated in our meta-analysis assess pain, function, activities of daily living, range of motion, and shoulder strength. Similar to the trends we observed, several previous studies have shown that the majority of improvement in patients’ pain level, range of motion, and strength occurs in the first postoperative year after RCR, with relatively smaller changes occurring thereafter.4,8,36,46,58 For example, Moosmayer et al36 showed that improvement in shoulder pain, range of motion, and strength largely occurs during the first year after open repair of small and medium-sized tears. Berglund et al4 showed that pain improvement largely occurs during the first 3 months after arthroscopic RCR, earlier than recovery of range of motion and function. Similarly, Charousset et al8 showed that pain, range of motion, and strength largely improve by 3 to 12 months after arthroscopic RCR and stabilize thereafter. Wolfgang58 and Rokito et al46 showed that the most gain in strength is observed in the first 6 months after RCR and that strength reaches a plateau by 1 year.

Longitudinal imaging studies of rotator cuff integrity also reveal that the majority of retears occur during the first 3 to 6 months after RCR.19,25,33 For example, Kluger et al25 performed serial ultrasonography on 107 patients for 8 years after arthroscopically assisted miniopen RCR of large tears and identified 35 (33%) retears with 26 (74%) occurring in the first 3 months and 4 (11%) from 3 to 6 months after surgery. Miller et al33 performed serial ultrasonography on 22 patients for 2 years after arthroscopic RCR of large tears and identified 9 (41%) retears with 7 in the first 3 months and the remaining 2 by 6 months after surgery. In a prospective multicenter study, Iannotti et al19 performed magnetic resonance imaging on 113 patients up to 1 year after arthroscopic RCR and identified 19 (17%) retears, of which 18 occurred within 6 months. These data indicate that the majority of structural failures occur during the first 6 months after surgery and suggest that failures are rare beyond 1 year, further supporting the case for using 1-year follow-up as the preferred time point for assessing outcomes of an RCR episode of care.

Both follow-up duration and fraction retained are important for adequate outcome assessment in longitudinal studies. Loss to follow-up may introduce selection bias into study results and compromise validity of findings,12,57 particularly when participants lost to follow-up are notably different from those retained. A systematic review53 of orthopaedic clinical trials has shown that studies with longer follow-up periods are at higher risk for loss to follow-up. In the 3 studies in the current meta-analysis that report detailed follow-up data, follow-up rates dropped from 71% to 66% (Gulotta et al15), from 92% to 81% (Lapner et al28), and from 98% to 75% (Avanzi et al3) from 1 year to 2 years after RCR. Rigorous participant-retention strategies, such as simplifying study design and data collection tools, focusing on remotely collectible data, less frequent examinations, a multidisciplinary approach to contacting patients, and reward for participation, have been employed in clinical trials in attempts to improve follow-up rates, but such strategies require significant additional resources, have higher costs, and impose a heavy burden on clinical and research teams.38,54 Using 1-year follow-up for assessing outcomes of an RCR care episode mitigates the potential selection bias associated with patient attrition at 2 years and does not require use of additional resources for later but arguably less informative examinations.

This study has some noteworthy limitations. First, we only included studies with patients undergoing RCR and our results cannot be extrapolated to patients undergoing nonoperative management who may be at risk for tear progression and worsening of symptoms.41 Second, we aggregated studies using 3 commonly used shoulder-specific PROMs over 2 postoperative years. Our results may not extrapolate to other PROMs, or structural outcomes, or over longer terms. Third, we excluded small studies and the meta-analysis included relatively few studies for each PROM. This highlights the paucity of high-quality long-term studies in rotator cuff research. Finally, we observed high heterogeneity across the studies in this meta-analysis, particularly for ASES and Constant scores. Although caution is necessary in interpreting meta-analytic summaries from highly heterogeneous studies,20 such heterogeneity is not problematic in the current meta-analysis because the disparity between substantial baseline to 1-year changes and clinically minor 1- to 2-year changes reflected in these meta-analytic summaries were seen for each PROM in virtually all the included studies.

The lack of additional value in capturing 2-year PROMs over 1-year PROMs has also been demonstrated in meta-analyses of studies of patients undergoing other orthopaedic procedures, such as lower extremity arthroplasty43 and anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction.49 These results suggest that PROMs are clinically stable between 1 year and 2 years after at least these surgical procedures, implying that outcomes of the index surgery can be accurately assessed at 1 year, and additional follow-up for 2 years has little incremental advantage.

CONCLUSIONS

All PROMs improved considerably from baseline to 1 year, but only very small gains, below MCIDs, were observed between 1 year and 2 years after RCR. This study did not find any evidence for requiring a minimum of 2-year follow-up for publication of PROs after RCR. Our results suggest that focusing on 1-year PROMs after RCR would foster more timely reporting, better control of selection bias, and better allocation of research resources.
