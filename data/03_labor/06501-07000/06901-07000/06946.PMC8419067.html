Abstract

Context:

Psychosocial behavioral interventions (PBIs) that target patients with cancer and their caregivers face challenges in participant enrollment and retention.

Objectives:

1) describe characteristics of the patient-caregiver PBI studies; 2) examine participant enrollment and retention rates; 3) identify factors influencing participant enrollment and retention rates; and 4) explore the strategies to promote enrollment and retention rates.

Methods:

We identified randomized controlled trials that tested PBIs among adult patients with cancer and caregivers in five electronic databases. We conducted narrative and quantitative analyses to synthesize our findings.

Results:

Among fifty-five qualified studies reviewed, most tested the efficacy of PBIs (n=42) and used two study arms (n=48). In-person meeting was the most common PBI delivery mode. The primary outcomes included quality of life, physical health, and symptoms. The average of enrollment rates of patient-caregiver dyads was 33% across studies (range=8% - 100%; median = 23%). The average retention rate at the end of follow-ups was 69% (range=16%−100%; median = 70%). The number of study arms, recruitment method, type of patient-caregiver relationship and intervention duration influenced enrollment rates. Study design (efficacy vs pilot), follow-up duration, mode of delivery, type of relationship, and intervention duration influenced retention rates. Sixteen studies reported retention strategies, including providing money/gift cards upon study completion and/or after follow-up survey, and excluding patients with advanced cancer.

Conclusion:

Researchers need to incorporate effective strategies to optimize enrollment and retention in patient-caregiver PBI trials. Researchers need to report detailed study process and PBI information to improve research transparency and increase consistency.

Editorial note: David Hui, MD Senior Associate Editor

Recruitment and attrition issues are common in interventional clinical trials involving patients with cancer, often resulting in underpowered samples and incomplete data, making it difficult to interpret the study findings. In this systematic review that included 12,388 patients from 55 randomized trials examining various psychosocial biobehavioral interventions, Song and colleagues reported an average enrolment rate of 33% and retention rate of 69%. Importantly, these numbers were achieved after the investigators had given their best to troubleshoot and optimize the study procedures. These figures are important because they help us to understand what can be expected for similar interventional trials and have potential implications for future study design, sample size calculation and grant budgeting. The investigators also reported several practical recruitment and retention strategies that may be helpful for investigators planning their next study.

INTRODUCTION

International Agency for Research on Cancer reported that there were 19.3 million new cancer cases worldwide in 2020.1 The negative short and long-term impacts of cancer and related treatment often go beyond the patients and extend to their families.2,3 Psychosocial behavioral interventions (PBIs), defined as nonpharmacologic interventions that include psychological, educational and behavioral components, such as education/information sessions, relaxation training, cognitive and behavioral coping strategies, and group social support,4 play an integral role in helping patients with cancer and their families adjust to cancer diagnosis and treatment; cope with treatment-related symptoms and side effects; increase adherence to treatment regimens; promote health behaviors; and improve health/treatment outcomes.5,6 While most PBIs have focused exclusively on either the patients with cancer or their caregivers such as spouses or family members,2,7 increasing research evidence supports that, compared to individual-based interventions, PBIs that target both the cancer patients and their caregivers (i.e., patient-caregiver PBIs) may more effectively manage the health issues and quality of life challenges that the patients and their caregivers face. For example, different randomized trials have reported that dyadic interventions were associated with improvements in overall quality of life and multiple psychosocial outcomes compared to usual care or interventions targeting only patients or caregivers.8,9 Patient-caregiver PBIs also improve communication between patients and caregivers about complex issues and encourage families to work together as a team.10 Patient-caregiver PBIs often address issues related patient caregiving (e.g., medical and psychosocial information, identify symptoms and their severity, and skills to help manage patient’s care needs); marital/family care (e.g., maintaining good communication, intimacy, dealing with sexual concerns, Improve family functioning, manage uncertainty as a team, joint coping, and sexual counseling); and caregiver self-care (e.g., apply skills for self-care, increase self-confidence, acquire problem-solving skills, and self-efficacy for patient care).11

Patient-caregiver PBI research, despite their potentials, often face challenges that are related to participant enrollment and retention in clinical trials due to patients’ poor functional status, high symptom burden, and deterioration in health.12–14 Compared to studies that target individual patient or caregiver, patient-caregiver dyadic PBI research typically has more complex study designs, implementation strategies and increased costs, which all creates additional barriers to participant enrollment and retention.12–16 For example, the challenges of participant enrollment and retention in clinical trials of patient-caregiver PBIs are multiplied when both patients with cancer and their caregivers must meet eligibility criteria.15–17 Another challenge in patient-caregiver PBIs arises from maintaining satisfactory retention for both patients and caregivers in order to achieve a sufficiently powered sample size.18,19 These challenges can be significant barriers to successful study implementation, and ultimately affect the scientific rigor and financial viability of patient-caregiver research.20 Effective enrollment and retention strategies in clinical trials can facilitate adequate participation of patients with cancer and their caregivers and help obtain a sufficient, representative sample, which enhances the statistical power of these trials and the generalizability of the research findings.21 However, there has been limited research to systematically examine the enrollment, retention, the factors influencing enrollment and retention rates, and related strategies in clinical trials that evaluate patient-caregiver PBIs.16,19 To fill these gaps, this systematic review aimed to 1) describe the characteristics of the patient-caregiver PBI studies; 2) examine the enrollment and retention rates of patients with cancer and their caregivers in clinical trials testing patient-caregiver PBIs; 3) investigate whether the characteristics of study design, participants, and PBIs influenced participant enrollment and retention rates; and 4) explore the commonly used enrollment and retention strategies.

METHODS

We developed a comprehensive systematic review protocol based on the Cochrane Collaboration and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.22 This review has been registered on PROSPERO, the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (Registration number CRD42020154230, review protocol can be accessed at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/#loginpage,). The inclusion criteria were detailed according to the Population, Interventions, Comparator, Outcomes and Study design(s) (PICOS) framework.23 To meet the inclusion criteria, research publications must have: (1) targeted adult patients diagnosed with cancer and their adult family caregivers (>=18 years of age); (2) included patient-caregiver psychosocial or behavioral interventions (defined as an intervention in which counseling or behavior management targeted both the patient and their caregiver(s) to alleviate symptoms and/or promote well-being for the patient and/or their caregiver, or an intervention targeted one member of the dyad to improve the outcomes of both the patient and their caregivers); (3) reported information about participant enrollment and/or retention; (4) used a randomized controlled trial (RCT) design; and (5) were published in English between July 1, 2009 and June 30, 2019 (updated October 1, 2020). Studies were excluded if they (1) focused on participants with diseases other than cancer; (2) included participants who had various of illnesses but did not report the breakdown information on patients with cancer and their caregivers; (3) did not report participant enrollment or retention; or (4) did not include a control/comparison group.

We developed the search terms in consultation with a university Health Sciences Librarian. The key search terms included “family caregiver,” “cancer patient,” “psychosocial” and “behavior.” Appendix A contains the example of full electronic search strategy for the PubMed. We adjusted these keywords with vocabulary and syntax across commonly used information databases including Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Embase, PsycINFO, and Scopus. To identify studies that might have been missed in the electronic search, we conducted forward and backward citation chaining. Reference lists of the retrieved articles were examined for additional relevant publications, and cited articles were hand-searched to maximize the number of research projects to be included in this review. We used Covidence™ - a web-based tool that can support efficient production of systematic reviews24 - for data cleaning and management. After two researchers reviewed and eliminated the duplicated records and cross-checked the results, four researchers independently screened the titles and abstracts and then full text of all identified articles. Ongoing team discussions resolved any disagreement.

Data extraction, synthesis, and analysis

Four coauthor researchers independently extracted data from the studies that met the inclusion criteria. Data were extracted and analyzed in Microsoft Excel. The extracted results were compared, and discrepancies were resolved through ongoing team discussions. The merged data were then checked again by an independent researcher. A meta-analysis of the outcomes was deemed impossible due to incomplete and heterogeneous information reported in these studies (e.g., populations, intervention and follow-up). We conducted narrative analysis to synthesize our findings. In addition, we conducted weighted descriptive analysis and Quasi-Poisson regression (for count data with over-dispersion) to examine the relationships between enrollment and retention rates and the characteristics of study,25 participants and intervention based on the limited available data. We used QAIC as an indicator for model selection and obtained the final parsimonious model that only included significant covariates.26 We used the overall enrollment and retention rates as the outcomes because these rates did not vary significantly between patients and caregivers and between groups (i.e., control vs intervention).

To assess study quality, two authors independently reviewed the papers based on the criteria that we adapted from Foster et al’s recommendation.27 In their original risk of bias assessment tool, Foster and colleagues recommended five items to assess study enrollment. We added five criteria to examine participant retention. We assigned a value of 1 (presence of the information) or 0 (absence of the information) for each criterion, and then calculated the total score of all items.

RESULTS

As shown in Figure 1, our initial search of the electronic databases (n=2,958) and our hand search (n=2) yielded a total number of 2,960 records. After removing duplicates, we identified 1,763 articles for title and abstract review, of which 125 were retained for a full-text review. After removing the studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria, we included in this review fifty-five articles that reported on independent PBIs.28–82

Regarding the study quality (Table 1), only five studies reported all required information about enrollment and retention. All studies (n=55) reported the targeted population, where participants were recruited, and the number of participants included in data analysis. Most of the studies also reported length of their follow-ups (n=54), the number of dropouts (n=52), the reasons for dropouts (n=52), recruiters (n=40) and the time spent on recruitment (n=32). Forty-nine studies included an enrollment and retention flowchart. Only sixteen studies reported the strategies used for enhancing retention.

Overview of The Characteristics

1.

Study Characteristics

1.1

All studies were RCTs (Table 2) that examined intervention efficacy (n=42) or acceptability and/or feasibility (n=13). The studies were conducted in the United States (n=35), European countries (n=10), Australia (n=4), China (n=3), Canada (n=2), and Nigeria (n=1). The RCTs used 2-arm (n=48), 3-arm (n=6) and 4-arm (n=1) designs. The control groups included usual care (n=26), waitlist (n=9), attention control (n=2), or education or informational support (n=11) or psychosocial support (n=7) that was different from the intervention groups.

Participant Characteristics

1.2

Patient participants had diagnoses of mixed types of cancer (n=18) or one homogenous type of cancer (n=37), e.g., prostate (n=12), breast (n=9), lung (n=9), brain (n=2), head and neck (n=1), ovarian (n=1), colorectal (n=1), hematologic cancers (n=1) at varying stages (Table 2). One study targeted patients with Melanoma. The mean age of patients across all studies was 59.3 years old (SD=6.3; range= 40.7–75.0; median= 60.5); the mean age of caregivers was 56.5 years old (SD= 6.2; range= 24.0–69.7; median=56.7). Twelve studies included male patients only and ten studies included female patients with breast and ovarian cancer. The other 33 studies included patients of both genders, but with either female (n=20) or male (n=13) patients dominating the study populations. In the forty-six studies that reported the gender of caregivers, thirty-five studies included caregivers of both genders but with females (n=27) and males (n=8) being the majority of the caregivers. Seven studies included female caregivers only and four included male caregivers only. The caregivers were the spouses and partners of patients in approximately two-third of the studies (n=37). The rest of the studies included other relatives as the patients’ caregivers, including adult children (n=11), parents (n=6), relatives (n=12), siblings (n=3), friends (n=9), or anyone who provided support (n=6). Furthermore, only twenty-six studies mainly from USA (n=24) and UK (n=2) reported participants’ race, and approximately 75.9 % of the patients and caregivers were Caucasians.

Intervention Characteristics

1.3

The interventions (Table 2) included psychosocial support, education and skills training; family coping facilitation; and symptom management. Most of the studies have used more than one type of intervention components. These interventions were mainly delivered through in-person meetings (n = 24), phone calls (n = 11), videoconferences (n =3), online (web-based or mHealth app) (n=2), material provision (n=1) or a combination of in-person meetings, teleconferencing (phone calls, or videoconferences), material provision (booklets, fliers, audios/videos, and CD/DVDs), and online formats (web-based or mHealth app) (n=14). Fifty-three studies reported the intervention duration, which ranged from 3 days to 8 months. Most of the interventions lasted less than 8 weeks (n=23) or between 8 weeks and 16 weeks (n=22). Eight interventions lasted 16 weeks or more (n=8).

The number of intervention sessions (Table 2) ranged from 1 to 42, with twenty-five studies having less than 6 sessions and twenty-six having more than 6 sessions. The length of each intervention session ranged from 5 minutes to 360 minutes in the forty-five studies that reported this information. Specifically, fourteen studies had sessions longer than 60 minutes; twenty-eight studies had 30- to 60-minute session(s); and three studies had sessions shorter than 30 minutes.

Fifty studies reported the intervention delivery settings (Table 2), including homes (n= 34), outpatient clinics (n=12), community settings (n=2), rehabilitation center (n=1), and research center (n=1). The interventionists were nurses or nurse practitioners (n=15), mental health specialists (n=18), physical therapists (n=10), and social workers (n=5). Other studies used physicians (n=2), researchers (n=1), nutritionists (n=1), dance or exercise instructors (n=2), or peer support volunteers (n=1) as the interventionists.

RCT Primary Outcomes

1.4

Thirty-three studies reported psychological outcomes (e.g., perceived stress, depression, anxiety, psychological well-being, mood disturbance, fear of progression, meaning of life, life satisfaction, and perceived self-disclosure). Other primary outcomes included quality of life (n=19); physical health and symptoms (n=19) (e.g., symptoms, physical function, comorbid conditions, sleep, fatigue, and fall incidence); behavior-related outcomes (n=13) (e.g., self-efficacy, attitudes, motivation, and adherence); coping (n=8) (e.g., coping strategies, psychological adjustment, avoidance, and illness/care needs appraisal); sexual function (n=6) (e.g., sexual adjustment, masculine self-esteem, intimacy, sexual behaviors, sexual well-being, sexual satisfaction, communication about sex, and sexual distress); and social support (n=5).

Eleven studies reported caregiver outcomes, including burden, strain, attitudes, skills and self-efficacy, preparedness/mastery for caregiving, and psychological well-being. Nineteen studies also included relationship quality-related primary outcomes, e.g., marital adjustment and coping, relationship satisfaction, relationship quality, relationship function, marital/caregiver-patient communication, partner support, and couples’ trust.

RCT Follow-up Duration

1.5

Fifty-four studies reported their follow-up duration, ranging from 3 weeks to 19 months. The studies had follow-ups shorter than 3 months (n=20), between 3 and 6 months (n=15), and 6 months or longer (n=19).

Enrollment and Retention Rates

2.

Enrollment Rate

2.1

Overall Enrollment Rate and Duration.

The total number of eligible patients and caregivers across all trials was 37,196 (mean=676; range=41–5,800; median=282). Of these, 12,388 were enrolled (mean=225; range=34–988; median=128). The average of enrollment rate across all studies was 33% (range=8%−100%; median=23%) (Tables 3 and 4). The average patient and caregiver’s enrollment rates across all studies were 33% (range=8%−100%; median: 23%).

Thirty-two studies reported the time used to enroll participants, with an average enrollment duration of 21.5 months (SD=10.4). Twenty-three studies reported the sample sizes based on power analysis (mean=136 dyads). We were unable to examine the relationship between enrollment duration and enrollment rates because only less than a third of the studies (n=17) reported the time spent on enrollment. However, the average enrollment duration was shorter for the studies that reported an enrollment rate of 50% or greater than those that had an enrollment rate less than 50% (i.e., 20.5 months vs 25 months, respectively). (Table 3).

Retention Rate

2.2

Overall Retention Rate.

A total of 8,508 participants (mean = 155; range = 29–694; median = 98) among the 12,388 enrolled patients and caregivers remained at the end of these studies (Tables 3 and 4). Thus, the average retention rate across these studies was 69% (range=16%−100%; median: 70%). The average patient’s retention rates across all studies were 69% (range=19%−100%; median: 69%) and 70% (range= 13%−100%; median: 70%) for intervention and control groups, respectively. The average caregiver’s retention rates across all studies were 68% (range= 19%−100%; median= 69%) and 69% (range= 13%−100%; median= 70%) in the intervention and control groups, respectively.

The characteristics of study design, participants, and interventions that influenced enrollment and retention rates

3.

Enrollment rate was significantly influenced by the number of study arms (2 VS 3 arms or more) (p<0.01), recruitment method (single vs multiple) (p<0.01), type of patient-caregiver relationship (spousal vs non-spousal) (p<0.001), and intervention duration (<8 weeks, 8–16 weeks, vs >16 weeks) (p<0.05). Studies had higher enrollment rates when they had more than two study arms; used single recruitment method; engaged non-spousal caregivers (different types of caregiver); and had 16 weeks or longer intervention duration (see Table 5).

Retention rate was significantly influenced by study design (efficacy vs non-efficacy) (p<0.05), type of relationship (spousal vs non-spousal) (p<0.001), mode of delivery (in-person, teleconferencing, material provision, vs more than two modes) (p<0.05), follow up length (<3 months, 3–6 months, vs >6 months) (p<0.01), intervention duration (<8 weeks, 8–16 weeks, vs >16 weeks) (p<0.05). Specifically, studies had higher retention rates when they examined PBI efficacy; targeted patient-spousal caregivers; used material provision and multiple modes of PBI delivery; had less than 3 months follow-up; and provided a PBI longer than 8 weeks (Table 5).

Enrollment and Retention Strategies

4.

Enrollment Strategies

4.1

All studies used a convenience sampling method to select participants because of their convenient proximity and accessibility for enrollment. As displayed in Table 3, the main enrollment settings included outpatient (n=38) and inpatient (n=13) clinics, and community (n=12). Enrollment methods included referrals from healthcare providers (n=9), in-person invitations (n=32), invitation letters (n=15), phone calls (n=5), and advertisements in public settings such as flyers and TVs (n=9) (Table 3). However, no study reported the specific strategies that have been used to enhance enrollment.

Retention Strategies

4.2

Reasons that participants withdrew before and/or during interventions.

1.

Fifty out of fifty-five studies reported the reasons that participants dropped out of the studies before and/or during interventions. First, participants-related reasons included patient/caregiver illness or poor progression of disease (n=28), and patient/caregiver death (n=11). Second, intervention-related reasons included intervention complexity or burden (n=8). Third, circumstance-related reasons included participants’ lack of time (n=23), and distance of the intervention sites (n=4). Fourth, study-related reasons included patient/caregiver became ineligible (n=4), patient/caregiver enrolled in another trial (n=1), and survey was too complicated (n=2). In addition, many studies also reported that participants withdrew for unknown reasons (n=35), or participants were no longer interested in the studies (n=31). Five studies did not report the reason for participant withdrawal prior to intervention.

Reasons that participants withdrew from the follow-up surveys.

2.

Studies have also reported the following reasons that participants withdrew from the follow-up surveys. The reasons for subjects-related attritions included patient’s illness or poor progression of disease (n=24), psychological distress (n= 5), patient’s death (n=27), caregiver’s illness (n=5), caregiver’s death (n= 4), and marital or relationship conflict (n=3). The reasons for intervention-related attritions included intervention not meeting expectation (n=7), dissatisfaction with group allocation (n=4), and intervention burden (n=2). Third, other reasons for attritions included conflicting demands and life events (n=4), inability to establish contact (n=11), lack of time (n=13), and impacts of natural disasters such as Hurricane (n=3). Finally, many studies reported that participants dropped out for unknown reasons or lost to follow-up (n=28), patients or caregivers did not complete the follow up surveys (n=15), or participants were no longer interested in the studies (n=11). Despite the challenges, only sixteen studies reported the strategies to enhance retention during follow-ups. The strategies were limited to giving money/gift cards upon each follow-up survey (n=11), or upon study completion (n=5), and excluding patients with stage IV cancers (n=1).

DISCUSSION

This review is notably different from the 2010 meta-analysis by Northouse et al,11 the 2017 review by Ferrell and Wittenberg,83 and the 2020 review by Gabriel et al on psychosocial interventions for patients with cancer and/or their family caregivers.21 This review has comprehensively analyzed the enrollment and retention data of the RCTs that tested PBIs among both patients with cancer and their family caregivers or family caregivers between 2009 and 2020, an important aspect of family-based cancer research that has not been previously reported. To our knowledge, this is the first review that focuses on enrollment and retention in patient-caregiver PBI RCTs in the cancer context. Participant accrual and retention are integral to the success of PBI RCTs, but a challenge to researchers, especially in family caregiving studies. In this review, the overall quality of reporting the enrollment and retention data varied significantly across the fifty-five RCTs that tested PBIs among adult patients with cancer and their family caregivers. Most research participants were White females, and few studies had focused on racial/ethnic minority or under-served populations. The average enrollment and retention rates of patient-caregiver dyads across all studies was 33% and 69%, respectively. We also identified the factors that influenced participant enrollment and retention, including study design, recruitment methods, patient-caregiver relationship, mode of PBI delivery, length of follow-up, and intervention duration. These patient-caregiver PBI RCTs reported various challenges in enrolling and retaining patient-caregiver dyad participants. However, no study reported strategies used to enhance enrollment, and only sixteen studies reported limited information about retention strategies.

The Study Population In Cancer Patient-Caregiver PBI Trials

Our review demonstrates ongoing gaps in the study population in cancer patient-caregiver PBI trials.11,83 Although the RCTs targeted patients with different types of cancer of varying stages, majority of the participants were white females, and racial and ethnic minorities were less represented. Females may be more likely to participate in clinical trials than males because females often actively seek information and help whereas males equate help-seeking as a sign of weakness or as a lack of emotional control.84,85 Our findings indicate persistent lack of representation of racial and ethnic diverse patients with cancer and their caregivers. Cancer affects all population groups, but certain racial/ethnic minority groups may experience a disproportionate burden.86–88 For example, Black Americans have the highest mortality and the shortest survival among all racial and ethnic groups in the US for most cancers.89 Meanwhile, non-White patients and their family caregivers have sociocultural beliefs and practices that may be different from those of Whites, which are associated with challenges receiving standard care that may not be culturally competent.90 In addition, non-White patients and their caregivers report unmet needs for care and services in the community 91 and experience barriers to research participation.92 To enhance supportive care for patients with different backgrounds and overcome the well-known challenges in enrolling diverse participants in research,93 researchers need to not only develop culturally appropriate PBIs but also intentionally design and implement the RCTs to best meet the needs of diverse participants and increase their participation.94,95

Enrollment Rate

In this review, patients and caregivers had similar enrollment rates that did not differ by their intervention/control group assignment. Studies had higher enrollment rates when they had more than two study arms, used single recruitment method, engaged non-spousal caregivers (e.g., children, siblings, friends), and had 16 weeks or longer intervention duration. A previous review also found that participant enrollment rates were positively related to more than 2-study arms and longer intervention durations.16 One possible explanation was that the intervention trials of longer duration were better funded and staffed.16 It is also possible that participants thought they would benefit more from the interventions that had longer durations because these PBIs tend to cover more topics and address various issues. We found enrollment rates were higher in studies that engaged non-spousal caregivers than those in studies focused on spousal caregivers only. Caregiver burden is higher among spousal caregivers than non-spousal caregivers,96 and non-spousal caregivers usually report better mental functioning than spousal caregivers.97 Involving patients’ children, siblings, friends and other relatives in PBI RCTs, thus, may allow patients with cancer more flexibility when their spouses were not available or when they don’t have a spousal caregiver. Inviting non-spousal caregivers to participate in a cancer-related PBI study and involve in a patient’s care and management may also promote these individual’s sense of altruism and trust - the important influences on individuals’ decisions about research participation.98

Our findings of the positive relationships between enrollment rate and single recruitment method is different from an earlier review of strategies of recruitment of participants into RCTs.99 The researchers reported that use of more than one method of recruitment was associated with higher participant recruitment into a real or mock RCT that targeted individual participants.99 In our current review, most studies enrolled participants from outpatient and inpatient settings, and the most commonly used recruitment method was in-person invitation. These results may suggest that, although using different enrollment methods may provide more individuals the opportunities to learn about a research project and participate, pulling all resources and focusing in-personal recruitment at in- and/or outpatient settings can make the recruitment of cancer patient-caregiver participants more targeted and more efficient. In-personal recruitment may take different formats such as network enrollment. For example, a smoking cessation trial used female family members and friends as agents to achieve enrollment rates of 48.6% and 37.4% for lung cancer patients and their family members/friends, respectively. This network enrollment was highest among patients who were White and had late-stage disease, and family and close friends enrolled were most likely to be female, a birth family, immediate family, or close friend, and live in close geographic proximity to the patient.84 In contrast, a recent study used electronic medical record-based secure messaging to recruit research participants for different studies at a large academic health network only achieved an average response rate of 12.9%.100,101 Researchers, thus, need to adopt different strategies, especially in-person approaches, to successfully enroll patient-caregiver dyads.

The studies in the review did not report the strategies that have been used to enhance participant enrollment. However, increased attention has been paid to develop strategies to recruit diverse patient and caregiver dyads into cancer research. Strategies that have been shown to improve recruitment include enlisting immediate female family members and friends who live close to the patient as recruitment agents;84 referral from affiliated healthcare providers; mass mailing and media coverage; trial-specific training of site recruitment staff; and provision of study information to prospective participants at a multidisciplinary, group information session.102 Community outreach activities such as displaying posters and attending local community events have not been shown to be effective in recruiting male research participants. Future research needs to continue to explore and evaluate strategies to identify prospective participants, as well as to improve the processes for assessing participant eligibility and providing information and seeking consent to facilitate recruitment of diverse patient-caregiver dyads (e.g., rural and. Urban, racial/ethnic and culturally) into cancer survivorship clinical trials.

Retention Rates

In this review, retention rates of patient with cancer and caregivers were similar and did not differ by their group assignment. Similar to patients’ and caregivers’ reasons for declining participation in an RCT,85 the reasons for attrition in the reviewed studies include health-related (e.g., death, illness, psychological distress), intervention-related (e.g., intervention does not meet expectation, frustration with group allocation, intervention burden) and other reasons (e.g. lack of time, unable to establish contact). The studies also reported unknown reasons for participant withdrawal or lost to follow-up. Our findings are consistent with the reasons that have been reported in a meta-ethnographic synthesis of studies exploring participants’ non-retention in RCTs.100

In addition, we found that studies had higher retention rates when they examined PBI efficacy, targeted patient-spousal caregivers, used material provision and multiple modes of PBI delivery, had less than 3 months follow-up, and provided longer than 8 weeks intervention duration. Little research is available to verify our review findings. It is likely that RCTs had more funding when they tested PBI efficacy (vs pilot feasibility testing). Therefore, more resources and personnel may have been available to promote participant retention. In fact, the reviewed studies had higher retention rates when they used multiple modes of PBI delivery or provided materials (vs in-person PBIs), and when their PBI had longer than 8 weeks duration (vs less than 8 weeks), all of which has high demands on resources and personnel. Similar to the findings of Northouse et al.’s11 and Ferrell and Wittenberg’s reviews,83 the patient-caregiver PBIs in this review predominantly focused on psychosocial care, social support, and education and skills training; facilitate family coping; and enhance symptom management. The interventions were delivered by various health care providers through in-person meetings, phone calls, videoconferences, material provision (booklets, fliers, audios/videos, and CD/DVDs), online formats, or a combination of different modes. Participants dropped out before and/or during the interventions because of patient/caregiver’s illness or poor progression of disease, patient/caregiver death, intervention complexity or burden, participants’ lack of time, distance of the intervention sites. Therefore, it is likely that PBI delivery using multiple modes and providing materials have provided patients and caregivers more flexibility and convenience than in-person PBIs, which facilitated participant retention.

Unlike the positive relationships between enrollment rates and non-spousal caregivers, involving spousal caregivers in PBI RCTs was associated with better participant retention than involving non-spousal caregivers. Previous research among breast cancer survivors has shown that caregiver participation is more likely among caregivers who were partners.103 However, two recent multinational Alzheimer’s disease registration trials reported that the drop-out rates were similar among participants with spouse, adult child and other study partners (i.e., 35%, 38% and 36%), respectively. Nonetheless, the differences in retention rates disappeared after adjusting for the effects of age, suggesting participant age plays a significant role in trial incompletion.104 Although further research is needed to verify our results, these findings may indicate the need for retention strategies to retain non-spousal family caregivers and older participants in PBI trials.

Despite the challenges in retaining the patient-caregiver participants, only less than a third of the reviewed studies (n =16) reported retention strategies, which included providing money/gift cards upon survey return and study completion and excluding patients with advanced cancer. The limited retention strategies reported in the studies have made it impossible to evaluate their effects on participant withdrawal. Previous research has shown that financial incentives improve retention rates.105,106 However, excluding patients with advanced cancer may be impossible in certain clinical trials, such as palliative care studies.107 Additional strategies need to be developed to enhance participant retention in this type of research.108 The following strategies have been recommended to enhance retention of advance patients with cancer and their caregivers, e.g., adopting flexible interventions; reducing treatment time periods and sessions; reducing the number of study follow-up assessments, especially those that are invasive or time consuming; and investigating the best possible time for enrollment in the course of the illness.108,109 Interestingly, research has also shown that employing a larger number of strategies may not be associated with improved retention in trials; adopting strategies that aim to reduce participant burden (e.g., flexibility in data collection methods, health care access) might be most effective in maximizing enrollment and retention.16 In addition, as the number of online PBI programs increases, researchers need to consider the need to improve computer literacy and ensure technologies are accessible and affordable for participants, which are critical for implementing online PBIs trials.110 Researchers also need to ensure that online PBIs are theoretically informed and attractive to the target population; has strong perceived usefulness and usability; and integrates digital tools (e.g., email or text message prompts) and human facilitation or coaching.111 To enhance retention of patient-caregiver dyads in cancer survivorship PBI trials, researchers may also use the effective retention strategies that a systematic review of healthcare studies has identified, including use of multiple strategies that build interpersonal relationship, e.g., obtaining multiple contacts; enlisting cooperation of family and friends; providing flexible appointments, letters of appreciation, reminders, and information resources.112 Future research needs to report detailed information to enable examination of the relationship between engaging these strategies and the retention rates in PBI trials.

Finally, the limited, heterogeneous, and sometimes missing information in the studies reviewed has constrained our data analysis and ability to draw a definitive conclusion of the findings. Although all studies reported information about the target population and the enrollment setting, fifteen studies did not report who conducted the enrollment; twenty-three studies did not report the time spent conducting enrollment; and thirty-nine failed to report how participants were retained. Although the majority of RCTs (n=49) included a flowchart, the information in the flowcharts varied significantly across studies, and some did not include the components that CONSORT guidelines recommend. For example, some papers used different terms at the enrollment stage, including “assessed for eligibility,” “referred to study,” and “consented,” which has made it difficult to calculate valid refusal rates for these trials. Our findings of the study quality are consistent with what have been reported in a systematic review of behavioral trials, e.g., no report of target sample size, time to complete recruitment, number of dyads screened, and sample sizes at each follow-up time point. Researchers have suggested that flowcharts need to specify the number of participants invited to attend the eligibility screening and the number of participants consented.113 High quality reporting is critical to improve research transparency and consistency, which will help enhance the rigor of patient-caregiver PBI research and optimize intervention strategies to maximize the effects of PBIs on patient and caregiver outcomes. Researchers should consistently report RCTs based on standardized guidelines (e.g., CONSORT) to avoid faulty result interpretation and facilitate transparency and reproducibility in sciences. Transparent, consistent report of patient-caregiver PBI RCTs will also enable systematic examination of the influence of intervention characteristics on enrollment and retention of patients with cancer and their family caregivers, or vice versa.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the heterogeneity of participants’ characteristics (cancer types, stages, caregivers), recruitment and intervention settings, and PBIs types in the studies limited our analysis and generalizability of our findings. We have used quantitative and qualitative methods to synthesize the data to help provide information that is useful for researchers to design, develop and implement future PBI trials among patients with cancer and their caregivers. Second, the lack of standardization and use of various terms in PBI research may lead to oversight of some research projects in the literature. Terms such as “psychosocial or behavioral intervention” may exclude studies used predefined intervention names. Third, the lack of standardization in the use of term of enrollment have led to inconsistency in reporting enrollment rate and reasons of declining enrollment. For example, some studies deemed the participants who were eligible and met the inclusion criteria as enrolled participants, while other studies counted participants who were consented or randomized as enrolled participants. Third, we only quantitatively analyzed the influence of limited number of characteristics of study design, participants, and PBIs on enrollment and retention rates because many reports did not include information of the potential confounders.

Recommendations

In order to facilitate future analysis and identify effective strategies for enrollment and retention of patients with cancer and their family caregivers in PBI RCTs, we make the following recommendations.

  • Conceptualize multiple RCT implementation factors at different levels (e.g., a complex mixture of individual patient with cancer, caregiver, provider, and healthcare system) that synergistically affect enrollment, engagement, and retention of diverse research participants (e.g., gender, age, race and ethnicity, and sex orientation).

  • Design and implement PBIs to account for the synergistic effects of factors at different levels (e.g., contents, mode of delivery, duration) that affect the health outcomes and RCT implementation. When implementing web-based PBIs trials, addressing differing health and digital literacy levels and ensure technologies are accessible and affordable for participants are critical.110

  • Empirically test the effects of influencing factors on enrollment and retention as a path toward achieving PBI efficacy in improving patient and caregiver outcomes.

  • Consistently report the recruitment and retention data (e.g., including a detailed flowchart, strategies) of RCTs based on standardized guidelines will enhance scientific transparency and reproducibility.

  • Transparently report PBIs, including intervention type, interventionist, mode of delivery, dosage, duration, and delivery setting.

CONCLUSION

Understanding and addressing participant enrollment and retention in patient-caregiver PBI RCTs are critical issues. The current systematic review examined the enrollment and retention rates of patients with cancer and their caregivers in PBI clinical trials, as well as how participant enrollment and retention rates varied by the characteristics of study design, participants, and PBIs. We summarized strategies used to enhance enrollment and retention rates as they were reported. This review highlighted a number of research gaps and identified future research needs. We recommend future PBI RCTs that target patients with cancer and their family caregivers to take into account the synergistic effects of multiple factors at different levels on enrollment and retention, which will ensure that supportive care research can achieve the ultimate goal of improving the health outcomes of cancer patients and their caregivers. We also suggest consistent and transparent report of PBIs and RCT processes to facilitate future analysis and identification of the factors influencing enrollment and retention of patients with cancer and their family caregivers.

Key Message

This review examined participant enrollment and retention rates in randomized clinical trials that tested the efficacy or feasibility of psychosocial behavioral interventions among patients with cancer and their family caregivers, identified the influencing factors, explored the strategies to promote participant enrollment and/or retention, and made recommendations for future research.
