Primary cytoreductive surgery for advanced stage endometrial cancer: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Objective:

Endometrial cancer uncommonly presents at an advanced stage, and little prospective evidence exists to guide management. We aimed to summarize evidence on primary cytoreductive surgery (PCS) in the treatment of advanced stage endometrial cancer.

Data Sources:

MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus databases were searched from inception to September 11, 2020, using search terms representing the themes “endometrial cancer,” “advanced stage,” and “primary cytoreductive surgery”.

Study Eligibility Criteria:

We selected for English full-text reports including ≥10 stage IV patients undergoing PCS for advanced stage endometrial cancer and reporting outcomes of incidence and survival by extent of residual disease after cytoreduction.

Study appraisal and synthesis methods:

Two reviewers independently screened studies, with disagreements resolved by a third. Data were extracted using a standardized form. Percent of cases reaching maximal (no gross residual disease; NGRD) and optimal (<1 cm or <2 cm residual disease) cytoreduction were assessed by summing binomials proportions, and association with survival assessed with inverse variance-weighted meta-analysis of logarithmic hazard ratios.

Results:

From 1,219 unique records, we selected 34 studies for inclusion. Studies consisted of single or multi-institutional cohorts of patients collected over 6–24 years, including varying mixes of histologies (endometrioid/serous/clear cell/carcinosarcoma) and stages (III/IV). In meta-analysis of extent of residual disease after PCS, we found 52.1% of cases reached NGRD (n=18 studies, 1,329 patients), and 75% reached <1 cm (n=27 studies, 2,343 patients). The proportion of cytoreduction to both thresholds was lower for studies of stage IV vs. stage III-IV disease (41.4% vs. 69.8% NGRD, 63.2% vs. 82.2% <1 cm), but did not vary notably by histology. In meta-analysis of reported hazard ratios (HRs), submaximal (any gross residual disease vs. NGRD), and suboptimal (≥1 cm vs. <1 cm) cytoreduction were associated with worse progression-free survival (submaximal: HR=2.16, 95%CI 1.45–3.21; I2=68%; suboptimal: HR=2.55, 95%CI 1.93–3.37 I2=63%) and overall survival (submaximal: HR=2.57, 95%CI 2.13–3.10; I2=1%; suboptimal: HR=2.62, 95%CI 2.20–3.11; I2=15%). Sensitivity analyses limiting to high quality studies demonstrated consistent results.

Conclusions:

Among cases of advanced stage endometrial cancer undergoing PCS, a significant proportion of patients are left with residual disease, which is associated with worse survival. Further investigation of the roles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and PCS in prospective trials is warranted in this population.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women in the United States (US), with over 60,000 new diagnoses annually.1 About 80–90% of cases are diagnosed at early stages, where prognosis, given appropriate surgical treatment and staging evaluation, is typically excellent.2,3 The management of early stage endometrial cancer has been extensively studied with large randomized studies of both primary surgical management and subsequent adjuvant treatment.4–6 However, in the uncommon case where a patient presents with advanced stage disease with pathologic spread beyond the uterus, providers have limited prospective evidence by which to guide initial management, particularly in the setting of bulky or stage IV disease.7–9 Similar to advanced ovarian cancer, the primary treatment has traditionally been with surgical cytoreduction.10

Over recent years, primary management of advanced ovarian cancer has been extensively studied. Use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by interval cytoreductive surgery has been found to have similar survival outcomes to optimal primary cytoreductive surgery (PCS) in randomized trials,11,12 and is now recommended in published guidelines for cases with disease precluding optimal cytoreduction to <1 cm.13,14 This treatment paradigm has not been studied prospectively in advanced endometrial cancer, but providers have increasingly utilized NACT in recent years, and retrospective data demonstrates comparable survival.15 These trends prompt a reconsideration of the cumulative retrospective data on primary management of advanced endometrial cancer in order to best guide current practice and determine the utility for prospective and randomized assessment of NACT versus PCS in advanced stage endometrial cancer.

Objectives

Our primary objective was to compile the descriptive literature on primary cytoreductive surgery for advanced stage endometrial cancer in order to (1) summarize reported proportions of maximal (no gross residual disease; NGRD) and optimal (<1 cm or <2 cm residual disease) cytoreduction, (2) estimate respective associations with progression-free and overall survival, and (3) describe patient characteristics predictive of ability to reach these cytoreduction thresholds.

Methods

Eligibility criteria, information sources, search strategy

A standard methodology was used to perform our search and analyses and report our findings, following published Cochrane methodological guidelines, and MOOSE (Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) and PRISMA (Preferred Reporting In Systematic review and Meta-Analysis) reporting guidelines.16–18 A study protocol is available on request. A biomedical librarian was consulted for development of the search strategy (S.J.K.). The MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, and Scopus databases were queried from inception through September 11, 2020. Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms and keywords were used to populate thematic sets related to ‘endometrial cancer,’ ‘advanced stage,’ and ‘primary cytoreductive surgery,’ then the Boolean term “AND” was used to find the intersection. See Appendix for included MeSH headings and keywords by theme, and search strategies. Limits or restrictions on time or language were not used during the initial search process. Conference abstracts and non-human studies were removed.

We required that studies meet the following eligibility criteria: (1) the study represented a retrospective or prospective clinical trial, cohort study, or case series (study type); (2) the study included 10 or more patients with stage IV endometrial cancer undergoing primary cytoreductive surgery, including endometrioid, clear cell, and serous carcinoma, and carcinosarcoma histologies, and excluding cases of leiomyosarcoma and endometrial stromal sarcoma (study population); (3) the study reported on extent of cytoreduction and/or survival outcomes by extent of cytoreduction (study outcome); (4) the study was published in manuscript format since 1990, was available in English, and did not represent duplicative data with other included studies.

In cases of outcomes reported including multiple stages, we included the study and extracted data when at least one outcome was reported specifically for stage III-IV or stage IV alone, but excluded studies reporting outcomes only summary outcomes that include stage I-II cases. In cases of fully overlapping data, the study with more recent or completely reported outcome data was retained, while all studies with partial overlap were retained and sensitivity analyses used to assess robustness of results to removal of all potential of the same patient being represented in the data more than once. References from included studies were manually assessed for additional unique eligible studies.

Study selection, data extraction, and assessment of study quality

After removing duplicate records from the initial search, title and abstracts were manually reviewed and clearly irrelevant studies excluded by two independent reviewers (two of B.B.A., K.M., R.A.P.). Full text was then independently reviewed for all potentially relevant studies by the same investigators to determine final eligibility. Disagreements at both stages were settled by consulting a third independent reviewer (B.A.D.). Data extraction was completed using a standardized form by B.B.A., with any uncertainties resolved by consultation with R.A.P. Study authors were not contacted to obtain additional unpublished data.

Methodological quality assessment was performed independently in duplicate by B.B.A. and B.A.D. using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for assessment of cohort studies in meta-analyses, with disagreements resolved by R.A.P.19 We only considered 1 possible point for comparability of cohorts, assigned as 1 for multivariable adjusted survival comparison by extent of residual disease, leading to a maximum of 8 points. For the definition of adequate follow-up we utilized reported median follow-up of ≥18 months, and for the definition of adequate assessment, we utilized a definition of <10% missing data on the exposure (residual disease after cytoreduction).

The primary outcome was relative incidence of maximal or optimal cytoreduction (vs. submaximal or suboptimal). Maximal cytoreduction was defined as disease reduction to no gross residual disease (NGRD). Optimal cytoreduction definitions varied by study, with most reporting <1 cm residual disease, and a few studies using the <2 cm threshold. One such study cited inclusion criteria for GOG 122 as the basis for this less stringent definition.20 We performed meta-analysis of proportions by summing Ns of total patients and those with maximal and optimal cytoreduction, combining studies based on the described and reported thresholds of residual disease.

Secondary outcomes of overall or progression free survival by submaximal vs. maximal or suboptimal vs. optimal cytoreduction were variably reported as median survival by group, or univariate or multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (HR) from Cox proportional hazards models. In data extraction and meta-analysis, we favored use of multivariable-adjusted HR, followed by univariate HR, then median survival. In cases in which there was no comparison reported by extent of residual disease, or where no measure of statistical certainty (either CI or p-value) was specified (not reported or stated generally as >0.05), survival data was not extracted, and the study was excluded from the respective survival meta-analysis.

Data synthesis

For meta-analysis of survival outcomes, heterogeneously reported data were transformed to a standard format. In cases of reported median survival only, these data were transformed to HRs by dividing median survival among maximal/optimal cytoreduction cases by median survival among submaximal/suboptimal cytoreduction cases. If median survival was presented for two groups requiring combination for meta-analysis (e.g. NGRD and <1 cm residual disease combined for comparison to ≥1 cm), we calculated weighted averages across the combined groups. All HRs and confidence limits were then transformed to the logarithmic scale. Log-scale CIs or p-values were used to derive log-scale standard errors. Meta-analysis was performed using inverse variance-weighted averages of logarithmic HRs.18,21

For systematic review management, we utilized the Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). For pooling of survival outcomes, RevMan 5.3 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, DK) was used to create summary estimates, forest plots, and funnel plots.22 Due to the more conservative assumptions about the similarity of included studies and expected variation in type, length, and completeness of follow-up across studies, we used random effects modeling to minimize the risk of type 1 error. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed with Higgins I2.18 Funnel plots were examined to assess for publication bias.

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess for differences by reported cytoreduction threshold of residual disease (NGRD, <1 cm, <2 cm), included stages and histology, and US versus international studies. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the consistency and reliability of results for each outcome, including analyses excluding lower quality studies scoring <8/8 on the Newcastle-Ottawa assessment, analyses excluding studies to eliminate all potential patient overlap/duplicate representation, and analyses limiting to studies reporting adjusted/multivariable hazard ratios for survival.

Results

Study selection

A total of 2,146 records of potential interest were identified in the initial search of electronic databases. After eliminating 927 duplicate records, 1,219 unique records were screened for eligibility. A total of 1,121 records were excluded by title and abstract review, with the majority found to be the wrong study type (review papers, case reports), or wrong population (ovarian/cervical cancer, sarcoma). Among 98 studies assessed for inclusion with full text review, 34 were identified as meeting all inclusion criteria.18–51 The majority of exclusions were for lack of reporting any relevant outcomes (n=30), or for including fewer than 10 stage IV patients (n=20). No additional relevant studies representing unique data were identified for inclusion from reference review. See Figure 1 for PRISMA flow diagram of study selection.

Among 34 included studies, 10 were marked as potential for partial, but not complete, overlap due to contemporaneous inclusion of patient cohorts of the same histology treated at the same institution. Five of these studies were selected for exclusion in sensitivity analysis, in which all potential overlap was eliminated (see Appendix Table A.2.).

Study characteristics

The 34 studies meeting inclusion criteria are summarized in Table 1 and Table A.2. All studies represented institutional or multi-institutional cohorts of patients collected over a range of 6 to 24 years of time. Most (n=31) studies included patients from one to three collaborating institutions, while 3 study cohorts were collected from national study group consortiums of a larger number of hospitals. Publication dates were evenly split between pre-2010 and 2011–2020, with only one study from before 2000. The majority of studies were from the US (n=22, 64.7%).

The breakdown of represented histology included 2 reports of endometrioid cancers, 13 reports of serous cancers, 2 of clear cell cancers, 2 of carcinosarcomas, and 15 studies including multiple histologic sub-types of endometrial cancer (3 including and 12 excluding carcinosarcoma). Half of studies (n=17) were focused exclusively on stage IV disease, while 12 included stages III-IV and 5 stages IIIC-IV, among which 3 also reported some outcomes separately within stage IV cases. The type of adjuvant treatment received varied between studies. Chemotherapy was given to over 50% of patients in 88% of studies, including all published since 2014, and the vast majority of patients received some adjuvant treatment.

Quality of included studies

Methodological quality of included studies was assessed with the Newcastle-Ottawa scale and assessments of included studies are shown in Figure A.1. Studies were generally of high quality, with all studies grading at least 6 out of 8 points. Fifteen studies did not report survival outcomes with statistical adjustment for confounders, twelve studies did not report follow-up or reported <18 months, and three studies had ≥10% of included patients with missing data on extent of residual disease after cytoreduction.

Synthesis of results

All included studies reported incidences of maximal and/or optimal cytoreduction. Utilized definitions of optimal cytoreduction included <1 cm (n=27) or <2 cm residual disease (n=3). Some studies reported maximal cytoreduction to no gross residual disease (NGRD) alone (n=4), and some reported NGRD in addition to one of the aforementioned optimal definitions (n=14).

Analysis for outcomes in relation to optimal by the <2 cm definition was limited, given only 3 studies reported threshold, with the most recent published in 2010. Among 163 patients in these studies, 69.3% had primary cytoreduction to <2 cm of disease. Only Ueda, et al. reported impact of suboptimal cytoreduction on PFS, while the meta-analysis of three studies showed significantly inferior overall survival (HR=3.61, 95%CI 2.06–6.32; see Figure A.2).

The remainder of the reporting below describes reports of maximal (NGRD) cytoreduction (n=18) and optimal (<1 cm residual disease) cytoreduction (n=27). We present meta-analysis of proportion of maximal and optimal cytoreduction by threshold in Table 3. Across 18 studies of 1,329 patients, we found that 52.1% were successfully reduced to NGRD. Cytoreduction to <1 cm residual disease was achieved more frequently, with 75% of 2,343 patients over 27 studies reaching this optimal threshold. More advanced stage was associated with lower proportion of cytoreduction to both NGRD (41.4% stage IV vs. 69.8% stage III-IV) and <1 cm residual disease (63.2% stage IV vs. 82.2% stage III-IV). We were limited in ability to assess for differences in histology by the heterogeneous reporting across studies, but we noted no major differences in maximal or optimal cytoreduction between reports limited to serous/clear cell cancers (n=13 studies; n=710 patients; 72% to <1 cm) versus those of endometrial cancer generally, which tended to include a majority of endometrioid carcinoma (n=13 studies, n=1,121 patients; 76.7% to <1 cm residual disease). Sensitivity analyses showed similar overall proportions of maximal and optimal cytoreduction, in both the analysis excluding studies with potential patient overlap (54.2% NGRD, 73.7% <1 cm residual disease), and limiting to high quality studies scoring 8/8 on the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (53.2% NGRD, 75.2% <1 cm).

We present raw extracted data for impact of maximal and optimal cytoreduction on progression-free and overall survival in Table A.3, and primary meta-analysis results as forest plots in Figure 2 and Figure 3. Overall, we found that any gross residual disease is associated with worse PFS (n=5 studies; HR=2.16, 95%CI 1.45–3.21; I2=68%) and worse OS (n=12 studies; HR=2.57, 95%CI 2.13–3.10; I2=1%). We estimated similar hazards for the difference between <1 cm and ≥1 cm residual disease, with suboptimal cytoreduction by this threshold associated with worse PFS (n=12 studies; HR=2.55, 95%CI 1.93–3.37; I2=63%), as well as worse OS (n=18 studies; HR=2.62, 95%CI 2.20–3.11; I2=15%). Study heterogeneity was greater for reports of PFS. Funnel plots demonstrated no clear pattern of publication bias, with close grouping of HR estimates across studies for each comparison (see Figure A.3).

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis estimates for relative hazards with suboptimal cytoreduction to ≥1 cm residual disease are shown in Table 4, and submaximal cytoreduction with any gross residual disease in Table A.4. We found no significant differences in HR estimates for impact of suboptimal cytoreduction by stage for either PFS or OS (stage III-IV HR=2.43, 95%CI 1.79–3.29, versus stage IV HR=2.83, 95%CI 2.26–3.55). Hazard ratio estimates were similar between studies of serous cancers alone versus endometrial cancers collectively. Sensitivity analyses also showed stable HR estimates. This included analysis limiting to studies that reported adjusted/multivariable HRs, typically including adjustment for histology, stage, and adjuvant treatment (PFS HR=2.74, OS HR=2.68), analysis limiting to studies with no potential patient overlap (PFS HR=2.27; OS HR=2.41), or analysis limited to high quality studies with 8/8 scores on the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (PFS HR=2.61; OS HR=2.79). Hazard ratios for the comparison of NGRD to any gross residual disease were similar to respective HRs for the comparison of ≥1 cm to <1 cm residual disease.

We sought to collect outcomes of predictors of the ability to achieve optimal cytoreduction in primary surgery for advanced endometrial cancer, but found that no included studies focused on these outcomes, and few studies reported them (n=7). Two studies reported that upper abdominal disease was associated with lower likelihood of optimal cytoreduction, but did not specify locations.24,29 Lambrau, et al. also reported that patients presenting with abdominal symptoms were more likely to have suboptimal primary surgery.29 The 2001 study from Bristow, et al. found lower proportion of optimal cytoreduction if 3+ anatomic sites of disease vs. 1–2 (33% vs. 67%, p=0.04).25 Patsavas, et al. found that Black patients and obese patients more frequently underwent suboptimal surgery.41 Two studies reported trends over time, with one series showing no changes and one showing improvement in optimal cytoreduction over time.42,48

Comment

Main findings

In this study, we present a systematic and meta-analytic summary of 34 cohorts of advanced stage endometrial cancer patients treated with primary cytoreductive surgery. We found approximately half of patients had surgery to maximal cytoreduction (no gross residual disease), while about three in four had surgery to optimal cytoreduction (<1 cm residual disease). The ability to achieve maximal or optimal cytoreduction was lower with cases of higher stage disease, but we did not find evidence of variation by histology. Patients who met maximal or optimal cytoreduction thresholds had clear, statistically significant improvements in both progression-free and overall survival with HR estimates centering around 2.6 and ranging from 1.7 to 4.1, depending on the subgroup considered. We find no clear evidence of variation in relative hazards of suboptimal cytoreduction by stage or histology. A series of sensitivity analyses demonstrated stability of estimates. Due to limited reporting, we were unable to offer in depth analysis of predictors of ability to achieve optimal primary cytoreduction.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study are in its systematic approach to identifying relative literature, including following published guidelines, searching multiple databases to query the international literature, and performing study selection in duplicate by independent reviewers to rigorously assess studies for inclusion. We utilized advanced methodology for meta-analysis of time-to-event data supported by the Cochrane collaboration. Lastly, we present meta-analysis results within multiple subgroups and include sensitivity analyses that demonstrate stability of our estimates to the exact cohort specification.

The main weakness of our study is tied to the observational nature of the collected data. Included studies did not report outcomes entirely consistently, and there was little reporting of patient characteristics predictive of extent of cytoreduction, or relative frequency of perioperative complications between cases of optimal and suboptimal cytoreduction. The included studies also were heterogeneous in stages and histologies represented, as well as practice patterns that are tied to time period and location of practice. We did not seek out unreported data from study authors. However, we included data for the primary outcome of overall survival from 26 of 34 studies, representing 88% of included observations. Given the general consistency of study results, addition of unpublished data would be highly unlikely to alter the findings. Perhaps most importantly, we cannot account for differences across time and geography in the choice of which patients underwent PCS as opposed to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT). We recognize that recommendation of PCS vs. NACT in this patient population is likely to vary significantly across institutions and providers, with variable surgeon skill and training. However, the overall consistency of our estimates implies some level of consistency in practice, and speaks to the importance of optimal residual disease independent of the decision to undergo primary surgery.

Comparison with existing literature

These findings provide an updated and complete summary of the treatment strategy of primary cytoreductive surgery for advanced stage endometrial cancer. Barlin, et al. previously summarized cohorts assessing cytoreductive surgery in cohorts of patients with advanced/metastatic or recurrent endometrial cancer.9 This meta-analysis, which included 9 of 34 studies presented here, used study-level meta-regression to show that an increase of 10% in proportion of complete cytoreduction was associated with an increase of 9.3 months in median overall survival in the study. However, in addition to the limited interpretability of this finding, this prior study did not use a systematic approach to its literature search.

The importance of optimal cytoreduction in histologically similar epithelial ovarian cancers has been extensively studied. A series of retrospective cohorts of PCS were summarized in a 2013 meta-analysis.57 This study showed pooled estimates of 25.9% of cases reduced to NGRD and 62.3% with optimal cytoreduction. They also found significantly worse survival among advanced stage ovarian cancer patients with increasing levels of residual disease after PCS (study-level meta-regression showing 2.3 months increased median survival per 10% increase in the proportion of maximal cytoreduction). Subsequent meta-analysis showed NACT followed by interval cytoreductive surgery led to improved rates of optimal cytoreduction.58 Such findings have motivated extensive study of NACT in prospective trials, which have demonstrated comparable survival outcomes,11,12 while providers have increasingly utilized this treatment strategy in practice.59 Understanding the implications on oncologic outcomes, researchers have trialed systems to predict ability to achieve optimal cytoreduction based on pre-operative clinical assessment and imaging,60,61 biomarkers,62,63 tumor characteristics,64,65 and laparoscopic assessment.66 A quasi-experimental study of retrospective data showed reduced 3-year mortality with regional adoption of NACT.67

Conclusions and Implications

Patients with advanced stage endometrial cancer with suboptimal residual disease after primary cytoreductive surgery are at higher risk of recurrence and death. This finding leads to speculation about the role of NACT in this disease. Combining our findings with extrapolation from the ovarian cancer literature, we postulate that patients may benefit from NACT, particularly in the setting of disease that cannot be completely or optimally resected. In combination with the findings of our paired systematic review of NACT in this disease setting, we recommend consideration of applying an ovarian cancer treatment paradigm to this population. This may be applied with pre-operative imaging and/or laparoscopic assessment of the disease burden prior to laparotomy and consideration of NACT if disease is felt to be unresectable. Ideally, this should be studied in prospective and randomized trials in the future, and include assessments of perioperative complications and patient reported outcomes. Until better evidence is available, patients should be well counseled on the goals of cytoreductive surgery and the risks and benefits of this treatment based on the available data.

AJOG at a Glance

Why was the study conducted?

A.

While primary cytoreductive surgery (PCS) remains the mainstay of upfront treatment of advanced endometrial cancer, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) is being increasingly utilized. We sought to review reported rates of maximal (no gross residual disease) and optimal (<1 cm residual disease) cytoreduction and associated impacts on survival.

What are the key findings?

B.

In meta-analysis of advanced stage endometrial cancer treated with PCS, we found that about 50% and 75% of cases reached maximal and optimal cytoreduction, respectively. Cases with submaximal or suboptimal cytoreduction were at significantly higher risk of recurrence and death.

What does this study add to what is already known?

C.

Our review suggests that a significant portion of advanced stage endometrial cancer patients undergoing PCS receive suboptimal cytoreduction and have inferior oncologic outcomes. Strategies for identification of patients with unresectable disease and the role of NACT warrant further consideration and evaluation in prospective trials.
