Use of geriatric assessment in cancer clinical trials: A systematic review

Abstract

Background:

Older adults are underrepresented in cancer clinical trials despite accounting for most of the disease burden. Geriatric assessment (GA) could be used in clinical trials of cancer drugs for older adults to improve the clinical evidence for cancer drug use among older adults.

Objective:

To examine patterns of use of GA in cancer clinical trials.

Methods:

We undertook a systematic review of the studies reporting use of GA in a clinical trial setting for all cancer types and published between January 2010 and January 2020. Characteristics of GA use were extracted for each study, along with study phase, cancer type, and participant age (PROSPERO: CRD42020170584).

Results:

We identified 320 studies and 63 studies met the final inclusion criteria. Among 74 purposes of GA use, the most common was to examine the association between impairments in GA domains and clinical outcomes (28/74, 38%). Among 258 GA domains assessed across 63 studies, physical status (59/258, 23%) and comorbidities (50/258, 19%) were most often evaluated. There was significant heterogeneity in the instruments used to assess physical function (n = 16) and mood disorders (n = 7). Most studies were phase 2 (32/63, 51%).

Conclusions:

GA is most often used in clinical trial settings to examine associations between GA-identified deficits and clinical outcomes. Significant heterogeneity exists in the GA instruments used across trials. Comprehensive and consistent incorporation of GA into future cancer clinical trial designs could help collect more older adult-specific clinical information and adjust trial eligibility criteria to increase representation by older adults.

Introduction

1.

Older adults with cancer have an increased prevalence of aging-associated conditions such as physical and cognitive decline and comorbidities that can influence their response to cancer therapies [1,2]. Therefore, incorporating assessments of aging-related vulnerabilities is increasingly important in cancer care [3]. Clinical evidence on new cancer therapies, however, has been primarily generated in younger and healthier patient populations, even though older adults account for the majority of the adult population with cancer [4,5]. Underrepresentation of older adults in cancer clinical trials has led to a failure to assess drug effects in patients who are more reflective of the real-world population and measure clinical endpoints that matter most to them. Moreover, in trials where older adults are adequately represented, there have been minimal efforts to collect geriatric-specific information [5,6]. The limited older adult-specific clinical information has led providers to rely on suboptimal evidence [7].

Geriatric assessment (GA), if incorporated into the evidence-generating process, may help collect clinical information more specific to the older patient population and broaden the application of trial results by adjusting trial eligibility to mirror the fitness of real-world patients. GA is a global assessment of older adults’ medical, psychosocial, functional status, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, cognitive function, nutritional status, and medications [8,9,10]. Major cancer networks, such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) have recommended performing GA for a comprehensive set of domains when making treatment decisions in order to facilitate identification of deficits not otherwise captured by routine cancer treatments.

A recent recommendation published by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) noted that using tools such as GA in a trial setting can help collect information specific to older adults; capable of informing clinical decision-making and treatment-related outcomes [6]. Eventually, incorporating GA into cancer clinical trials may lead to practice changes toward a more patient-centered approach, given that cancer clinical trials serve as a foundation of clinical guidelines and practice.

Despite these potential benefits of the GA its use in cancer clinical trials has been limited [11]. Earlier studies have examined use of the GA in populations with cancer [11,12,13,14,15,16]. However, there is a lack of a systematic evaluation in the literature about the use of the GA among cancer clinical trial participants. Examining how GA has been used in existing cancer clinical trials may improve understanding of its potential usage and benefits and thus facilitate its incorporation into the evidence-generating process. Therefore, we aimed to synthesize the literature in this area by examining recent trends in GA use in cancer clinical trials.

Methods

2.

Data Sources and Study Selection

2.1.

This systematic review (PROSPERO registration number: CRD42020170584) was based on a comprehensive search of two databases: OVID MEDLINE and OVID EMBASE. Included studies were: (1) cancer clinical trials (e.g., randomized trials, single-arm trials, or post-hoc analyses of trial data), (2) studies that were published in a peer-reviewed journal between January 2010 and January 2020, (3) studies that included at least one patient aged 65 years or older, (4) studies that used any type of geriatric assessment tool for any purpose, and (5) studies written in English and available as full text. Studies that collected GA data outside of a cancer clinical trial setting, editorials, case studies, reviews, expert opinion papers, trial protocols, and studies that were published as an abstract only were excluded. In case there were multiple publications from one study, we considered them as the same study and only the primary publication was included.

The sets of keywords for the literature search were developed by an experienced biomedical and translational sciences librarian at the University of Washington for MEDLINE and EMBASE, respectively (Supplement 1). Studies were screened for inclusion in two steps. In the first step, titles and abstracts were reviewed based on the inclusion criteria. The full text was further reviewed when the authors were uncertain whether the article fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Two investigators (W. L. and S.C.) conducted abstract and full text screening. Differences in included articles between the two independent reviewers were evaluated by the primary author (W.L), and reasons for exclusion were documented.

Data Abstraction

2.2.

Data abstraction was conducted using a standardized data abstraction form for both reviewers created with Excel software (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) (Supplement 2). Data were abstracted from the review of the full text and supplemental materials of the articles. The data abstraction form consisted of three sections that included a series of items. The information abstracted in the first section included the timing of GA use (e.g., before, during, and after the treatment) and the purpose of GA use. In the second section, domains measured in GA and the instruments used for each GA domain were abstracted. The third section included items related to trial characteristics section such as year of trial publication, type of study (e.g., randomized trials, single-arm, or post-hoc analyses of primary trial data), phase of the trial, cancer type, and median (or mean) age of participants. Both reviewers (W.L. and S.C.) independently completed the standardized form for each of the included studies, and results were compared and consolidated into one final data set.

Outcomes and Analysis

2.3.

The purpose of GA use in each study was examined based on the review of study purpose and methods. Similar purposes were grouped based on reviewers’ judgment – the groups were generated as they were emerged during the abstraction process (the final groups are listed in the results section). In studies for which GA was used for multiple reasons, we counted each GA purpose separately (i.e., one study could have multiple reasons for the GA use). Timing of GA was assessed to evaluate when the included studies utilized GA tools relative to the purpose of GA use. The timing was categorized as before, during, and after based on the included studies’ methods.

We examined GA domains and instruments based on the review of study methods. There are 14 domains of GA that are recommended by major clinical guidelines: functional status, comorbidities, psychological disorders, cognitive ability, nutrition, social and financial support, polypharmacy, falls/imbalance, urinary, vision/hearing, delirium, sexual function, dentition, goals/preferences of care [8,9,10]. Therefore, we classified GA domains found in the included studies into these 14 different categories. The unique instruments used to measure the GA domains were identified and categorized into the corresponding GA domain.

In terms of trial characteristics, we assessed trial phase (in case of post-hoc studies, the phase of trials that the primary data originated from), study design, cancer type, and median or mean age of participants based on the review of study methods and results sections. The trial phase was classified into 7 categories (i.e., phase 1, 1/2, 2, 2/3, 3, 4, and no specific phase) and study design was classified into 3 categories (i.e., randomized trial or multi-arm trial, single-arm trial, and post-hoc analysis of trial data). Median age of study participants was abstracted whenever available. Otherwise, mean age was abstracted. All outcomes were described through frequency distributions.

Quality Assessment

2.4.

One reviewer (W.L.) completed the quality assessment for all studies included in the review (n = 63). A second reviewer (S.C) conducted a quality assessment of one-third of the studies included in the review (n = 22). The 22 studies assessed by the second reviewer were randomly chosen using Excel software, stratified by study type (e.g., randomized trials, single-arm trial, and post-hoc studies). Quality assessment was completed using widely accepted tools for each of the included study types. Randomized trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool, version 2 (ROB-2), single-arm studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for Cohort Studies (NOS), and prognostic studies using clinical trial data were assessed using the Quality in Prognostic Studies (QUIP) tool. Evidence grades were applied to each of the studies based on the results of each tool, but no studies were excluded based on quality assessment results to provide a comprehensive review of GA use in the cancer clinical trial setting. Results of the quality assessment are provided in Supplement 4.

Results

3.

Reviewed Studies

3.1.

A total of 320 studies were identified in EMBASE and MEDLINE (Fig. 1). Following the initial study screening, a total of 63 studies were eligible for inclusion in this systematic review. An overview of all included studies can be found in Supplement 3.

Purpose and Timing of GA Use

3.2.

Seven distinct reasons for the use of GA in the cancer clinical trials were identified (Table 1). These reasons were to (1) examine the association between GA and clinical outcomes, (2) recruit study participants based on the frailty level or to identify frail patients who would or would not benefit from the proposed treatment, (3) examine the effects of GA-guided interventions, (4) assess treatment responses based on GA, (5) examine the feasibility of administering GA, (6) examine the clinical validity of GA, and (7) others. A total of 74 reasons for GA use were calculated as some studies utilized GA for more than one purpose. The most frequent reasons for GA use were to examine the association between GA and clinical outcomes (28/74, 38%) and to narrow the inclusion criteria based on the frailty level or to identify frail patients who would or would not benefit from the proposed treatment (18/74, 24%).

Regardless of the purpose of GA use, GA was performed before the start of the treatment in the majority of included studies (49/74, 66%) and was less often used during (0/79, 0%) or after (3/79, 4%) the treatment (Table 1). Twenty-one studies (28%) performed GA at multiple timings.

Domains and Instruments of GA

3.3.

Across the 63 studies, a total of 258 GA domains were assessed. The two most frequent GA domains assessed were physical status (59/258, 23%) and comorbidities (50/258, 19%) (Fig. 2). Goals/preferences of care and dentition domains were not evaluated in any of the studies.

The GA domain with the highest number of unique instruments was functional status, followed by mood disorders, cognitive abilities, and comorbidities. A total of sixteen unique instruments that measured functional status were identified (Table 2). We identified seven, six, and five unique instruments for mood disorders, cognitive ability, and comorbidities, respectively. There were 141 uses of instruments for measuring functional status. The two most frequently used tools to assess functional status were the Lawton and Brody Instrumental Activity of Daily Living (IADL) scale (43/141, 31%) and the Katz et al. Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (ADL) (39/141, 28%). For mood disorders, the tools with the highest frequency of use were the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) (23/35, 66%) and the Mental Health Inventory-17 (MHI-17) (4/35, 11%). In terms of assessing cognitive abilities, the Mini-mental state examination (MMSE) (17/33, 52%) and Blessed Orientation Memory Concentration (BOMC) test (8/33, 24%) were most frequently used. The comorbidities domain was most frequently assessed by the Charlson comorbidity index (18/52, 35%) and Cumulative Illness Rating Scale for Geriatrics (CIRS-G) (18/52, 35%). A single GA instrument was identified for each sexual function (International Index of Erectile Dysfunction) and Falls (number of falls in the last six months) domain.

Characteristics of Trials That Used GA

3.4.

The most common study phases of included studies were phase 2 trials (32/63, 51%) and trials without any specific phase (19/63, 30%) (Fig. 3). In terms of study design, single-arm trial was most frequent (27/63, 43%), followed by randomized trial (22/63, 36%). The most frequently observed cancer type among those enrolled in the reviewed studies was lung cancer (13/63, 21%) and lymphoma (12/63, 19%). Additional types of cancer types that utilized a GA tool in clinical trials between 2010 and 2020 include breast cancer, colorectal, prostate, ovarian, myeloma, leukemia, stomach, endometrial, and cervical cancer. A number of trials (9/63, 14%) did not define a specific cancer type for the trial. The median or mean age of participants fell within 74–77 years for most studies (34/63, 54%).

Quality Assessment

3.5.

Out of a total of 63 included studies, 22 studies were trials that were randomized. When utilizing the ROB-2 tool, fourteen (63%) studies were categorized as being at some risk, five (23%) were categorized as being at high risk, and three (14%) studies had a low risk of bias (Supplement 3). Of the post-hoc prognostic studies included in this review, we categorized 100% (13/13) of the trials as being at high risk per the QUIP tool. Twenty-eight single-arm studies were assessed for risk of bias using the NOS. Sixteen studies (58%) received a score of 6 and twelve (43%) studies received a score of 4 or 5 and thus all were categorized as having a high risk of bias.

Discussion

4.

This systematic review examined patterns of GA utilization across cancer clinical trials. The GA was most often used to examine the association between GA deficits and clinical trial endpoints. Functional status and comorbidities were the most commonly assessed. There was heterogeneity in the GA tools used across all studies. There was a relatively small number of later-phase clinical trials (i.e., phase 3 or 4) that incorporated GA.

Our findings highlight the predictive and prognostic value of the GA for the examination of survival outcomes (e.g., progression-free and overall survival), or treatment-related toxicities [17–20]. Although identified relationships were mostly associative and exploratory, the findings can help frame a hypothesis for subsequent studies (e.g., additional subgroup analysis or subgroup-specific clinical trial). From a clinical practice point of view, knowing specific GA domains that predict major health outcomes may help reduce the time for a GA by allowing patients and clinicians to focus on domains with a stronger association with the outcomes of interest. Furthermore, the associative information can be used to predict the tolerability and estimated life expectancy, which is of paramount importance in treatment decision-making [21].

However, studies that include GA still need some improvement for the findings to be more useful. First, studies using GA are significantly heterogeneous in terms of study design, methods, and reported outcomes. Future studies need to collect and report data in a more standardized way so that the evidence synthesis (e.g., meta-analysis) becomes more feasible. Moreover, it is unclear whether the findings from studies using GA are generalizable to the real-world older adult patient population. Clinical trials are often subject to the lack of generalizability (e.g., healthier or younger patients are over-sampled), and the same issue may hold for trials that incorporate GA. If generated among an unrepresentative trial sample, the findings on GA may not be applicable to real clinical practice.

Our study results also suggest a possible role of the GA as an eligibility criterion for the characterization of the fitness of an older adult. Prior studies have shown that biological age (i.e., physiological age) is a more reliable measure for estimating disease prognosis and treatment outcomes compared to chronological age (i.e., numerical age) [22,23]. However, the restrictive eligibility criteria based mainly on age and performance status still exist [24]. By incorporating GA into the eligibility criteria, researchers will be able to assess drug efficacy in relation to one’s biological age, which can facilitate treatment decisions that are more based on functional reserve, estimated life expectancy, or risk of treatment complications rather than the simple criterion such as chronological age. In addition, our findings showed the potential for GA to be incorporated into the outcome assessment, which may allow evaluation of endpoints that are more relevant to the preferences of older adults (e. g., quality of life vs. survival) and address their unique care needs [25]. Especially, a longitudinal use of GA can provide information about patient treatment experience (e.g., trajectories of functional or cognitive level) throughout the course of treatment. Considering the changing paradigm of outcome assessment from objective clinical examinations to incorporating patient experience, GA can potentially play a bigger role in collecting patient-reported data in cancer trials. Collecting such data from the trials would inform treatment decision making among older patients, optimizing their experience during the treatment.

Our results also show that GA domains were often assessed incomprehensively in cancer clinical trials – domains other than functional status and comorbidities were often omitted. In general, cancer clinical guidelines recommend performing comprehensive GA (CGA) that includes an extensive set of domains when making treatment decisions [8,9]. One reason for incomprehensive assessment could be that performing GA for all domains is often challenging due to time constraints and limited resources [26,27]. Depending on the purpose of use and available resources, therefore, it should be examined whether an abbreviated version of GA can be considered in a trial: abbreviated tools (e.g., Geriatric 8 and Vulnerable Elders-13 Survey) that are often used for a prescreening for CGA or tools that are more targeted to specific treatment outcomes (e.g., Cancer and Aging Research Group chemotherapy toxicity tool) [28–31]. These shorter assessment tools are mostly self-administered and have been assessed for its psychometric properties in several patient populations [28,32–35]. Future studies need to rigorously compare CGA and abbreviated GA tools in terms of their time- efficiency or cost-effectiveness with limited healthcare resources, especially when used in clinical trial settings.

Lastly, the variability in the instruments used to evaluate each GA domain suggests a lack of consistency in terms of which tools are used and in what combinations. Recommendations regarding the GA tools in oncology clinical practice are not well defined, probably due to the large number of validated tools available for each domain. That being said, major guidelines such as NCCN and ASCO recommend the use of one or two tools with a well-demonstrated association with major health outcomes (e.g., survival and toxicity) and good administration characteristics (e.g., the time it takes to complete the tool) [8,9]. Researchers should try to follow these evidence-based recommendations to reduce the existing heterogeneity. In addition, research should be continued on whether certain instruments are more predictive of clinical outcomes and less burdensome to available resources as instruments continue to be developed and improved. Increased consistency between GA tools may also increase the consistency of GA-guided treatment outcomes and decrease barriers to implementation of GA in clinical practice.

This study has a few limitations. We included studies across all types of cancer and thus cannot draw cancer type-specific conclusions. In addition, the frequency distribution of trial characteristics (e.g., phase of a trial and cancer type) cannot be used to infer any trend of GA use across different types of trials. For example, lung cancer trials were most commonly observed in this study, but it may be simply because the total number of lung cancer trials is greater than any other cancer type. Lastly, we could not find a quality assessment tool specifically designed for single-arm clinical trials, and thus used the one for cohort studies (i. e., Newcastle-Ottawa scale). Therefore, there may be additional domains that should be considered in the risk of bias assessment of single-arm trials. Also, we might have rated some assessment domains low simply because they were not reported or not stated clearly in the published article.

Conclusion

5.

We examined how GA has been incorporated in contemporary cancer clinical trials. We found that GA has been most frequently incorporated into trials to examine how GA domains are associated with the treatment outcomes and to recruit study participants based on frailty level. Generally, only some of the recommended GA domains – functional status and comorbidities – were measured in the trials, and the choice of assessment tools was inconsistent for some of the GA domains. Incorporating GA into cancer clinical trials and improved consistency in its use may facilitate the generation of older adult-specific clinical evidence and inform clinical practice in older adults more effectively.
