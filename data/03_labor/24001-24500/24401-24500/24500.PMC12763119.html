Summary

This systematic review aimed to examine the magnitude and direction of the associations between prenatal sleep behaviours (i.e. nighttime sleep duration, sleep quality, night awakenings and daytime nap duration) and eating behaviours, physical activity and gestational weight gain. A systematic search was conducted using Medline/PubMed, PsychINFO, CINAHL Complete, ProQuest Dissertations and Thesis A&I, and Web of Science to identify studies with at least one sleep measure, and either eating behaviours, physical activity and/or gestational weight gain. In summary, 11 studies met the review criteria and generated 11 total effect size across 10,900 participants. The majority of the studies were conducted after 2010, which highlights the infancy of this research. Overall, the strengths of the effect size were small: sleep-gestational weight gain (effect size = 0.29), sleep-eating behaviours (effect size = 0.13) and sleep-physical activity (effect size = 0.13). The only effect size that emerged as significant was for the pooled sleep behaviours-physical activity association; good sleep behaviours were positively associated with higher levels of physical activity. These findings summarize and provide insight on how sleep behaviours are related to prenatal gestational weight gain, eating behaviours and physical activity by identifying the strength and direction of the associations that have been previously unknown. Results support the rationale for future longitudinal and randomized control trials to examine the effects of sleep behaviours on gestational weight gain, eating behaviours and physical activity over the course of pregnancy.

INTRODUCTION

1 |

Sleep is a multidimensional construct that is essential for human health (Hall, 2013). Sleep duration, a main component of sleep, is the total amount of sleep that is obtained either during the nocturnal sleep episode or across the 24-hr period (Kline, 2013a). Other aspects of sleep are sleep quality, or the perceived value of sleep one feels they have achieved and generally conceptualized as a global indicator of overall sleep, nighttime awakenings (e.g. waking up during the night due to interruptions), and daytime naps (e.g. short periods of sleep during the day; Kline, 2013b; Yamada et al., 2016). Nighttime awakening and daytime nap duration may decrease nighttime sleep duration and sleep quality as well as disturb the nocturnal sleep cycle (Ohayon et al., 2010; Yamada et al., 2016). In the general population, the National Sleep Foundation guidelines recommend that adults get 7–9 hr of sleep per night and limit night awakenings to one or fewer each night for good quality sleep, but there are no pregnancy-specific sleep guidelines and/or recommendations (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015).

Good sleep hygiene is necessary for overall health and wellness, and it is especially important during critical periods in the life cycle such as during pregnancy to promote healthy maternal and fetal outcomes (Chang et al., 2010). The lack of prenatal sleep guidelines and/or recommendations is problematic because pregnant women are at elevated risk for sleep disturbances, such as short or interrupted sleep duration, poor sleep quality, nighttime awakenings and long daytime naps (Hedman et al., 2002; Kennedy et al., 2017; Mindell & Jacobson, 2000; Schweiger, 1972). For example, Mindell et al. (2015) conducted a study to characterize sleep patterns and problems (e.g. sleep duration, sleep quality, night awakenings, daytime naps) in 2427 pregnant women, and found that 38% reported insufficient sleep (i.e. ≤ 6 hr per night). Furthermore, 76%–100% of the sample reported poor sleep quality, taking daytime naps and frequent night awakenings (Mindell et al., 2015). Prenatal sleep disturbances are especially concerning because they can elevate the risk for adverse pregnancy outcomes, particularly excessive gestational weight gain (GWG; Facco et al., 2010; Gay et al., 2017; O’Brien et al., 2012; 2013; Qiu et al., 2010; Okun et al., 2013; Sharma et al., 2016; Williams et al., 2010).

Excessive GWG (i.e. gaining weight in excess of recommended ranges; Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2009) is one adverse pregnancy outcome that is of special concern because many pregnant women exceed the GWG guidelines (i.e. > 15.9 kg [35 pounds] for women with normal weight, > 11.3 kg [25 pounds] for women with over-weight, > 9.1 kg [20 pounds] for women with obesity; Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2009). Excessive GWG may be related to sleep behaviours; however, the existing prenatal literature is limited and has generated inconsistent findings such that both insufficient and excessive GWG are associated with poor sleep behaviours (Abeysena & Jayawardana, 2011; Gay et al., 2017; Guinhouya et al., 2018; Merkx et al., 2015; Restall et al., 2014). These findings suggest that women with short sleep duration may be particularly vulnerable to weight gain because of increased nocturnal eating and increased fatigue, which in turn may lead to lower levels of physical activity (PA; Lyytikainen et al., 2011). These finding are important because they relate excessive GWG to two lifestyle behaviours that may also be affected by sleep disturbances – poor eating behaviours and low PA.

Prenatal guidelines recommend a gradual increase of energy intake and high-quality foods over the course of pregnancy (i.e. additional 340 kcals day−1 in the second trimester and 452 kcals 14;day−1 in the third trimester; Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2006; McGowan & McAuliffe, 2012; Wennberg et al., 2016). A consistent finding in the literature is that most pregnant women exceed the energy intake recommendations and make poor food quality choices (Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2006; Cohen & Koski, 2013). Although the literature examining the association between prenatal sleep behaviour and eating behaviour is scant, the existing published studies show that poor sleep behaviours may be associated with high energy intake and poor eating choices (Chang et al., 2015; Duke et al., 2017; van Lee et al., 2017).

Regarding PA, both non-pregnant and pregnant women are encouraged to engage in 150 min per week of moderate-intensity PA, but most pregnant women fail to meet this recommendation (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists [ACOG] Committee on Obstetric Practice, 2015; Borodulin et al., 2009; Jung & Choi, 2017; Schmid et al., 2009; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2018). The literature examining associations between prenatal sleep behaviours and PA is also limited. Only four studies were located for this review, and they found equivocal associations between sleep behaviours and PA (Baker et al., 2018; Borodulin et al., 2010; Duke et al., 2017; Rodríguez-Blanque et al., 2019). Conversely, there is an extensive body of literature (e.g. over 11,000 published studies) examining associations between sleep behaviours and PA in non-pregnant populations; however, the findings from these studies have also yielded equivocal findings, with short sleep duration associated with increased PA (Jung & Choi, 2017; Markwald et al., 2013) and decreased PA (Benedict et al., 2011; Schmid et al., 2009), and those suggesting a lack of association between sleep duration and PA (Al Khatib et al., 2017; Benedict et al., 2011; Calvin et al., 2013; St-Onge et al., 2011). One potential reason for these equivocal findings may be attributed to the inconsistency between studies measuring sleep behaviours and PA such that various methods and difficulties arose when measuring sleep and PA (i.e. self-report bias and no consistent measurement method).

Despite the numerous studies examining sleep behaviours and weight, eating behaviours and PA in non-pregnant individuals, to date there are no systematic reviews examining the interrelationships of these behaviours during pregnancy. This review of the prenatal literature is critical to generate new information about the magnitude and direction of the associations between sleep behaviours, GWG, eating behaviours and PA. Thus, the purpose of this study was to conduct a systematic review of the literature to examine the magnitude and direction of the associations between sleep behaviours (i.e. nighttime sleep duration, sleep quality, night awakenings and daytime nap duration) and GWG, eating behaviours and PA. Based on the existing literature in non-pregnant populations (Lyytikainen et al., 2011; St-Onge, 2013), it was hypothesized that: (1) the sleep duration-GWG and sleep duration-eating behaviours associations would be negative such that shorter sleep duration will be associated with excessive GWG and poor eating behaviours; (2) the sleep quality-GWG, sleep quality-eating behaviours, nighttime awakenings-GWG, nighttime awakening-eating behaviours, daytime nap duration-GWG and daytime nap duration-eating behaviours associations would be positive such that poorer sleep quality, higher number of nighttime awakenings and longer daytime nap duration would be associated with higher GWG and poorer eating behaviours; (3) the effect sizes (ES) for the sleep behaviour-GWG and eating behaviours associations would be medium. Given the equivocal literature examining the association between sleep behaviours and PA, no a priori hypotheses regarding the direction of associations or size of the effects were established.

METHODS

2 |

This review was prepared according to PRISMA statement guidelines (Moher et al., 2015). Methods for data analyses and assessing risk of bias and study quality were also identified, and results were reported systematically.

Search strategy

2.1 |

Electronic searches of Medline/PubMed, PsychINFO, CINAHL Complete, ProQuest, Dissertations and Thesis A&I, and Web of Science from 1 July 1968 (i.e. date of first study conducted examining sleep in pregnancy) to 25 October 2021 were conducted to identify relevant peer-reviewed articles and primary studies. Search strategies were based on the terms “sleep”, “pregnancy”, “energy intake”, “eating behaviours”, “physical activity” and “gestational weight gain”, and were limited to human participants. Search strategies for each database were reviewed and approved by a health science research librarian to ensure proper search terms and procedures were used (Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2018). Reference lists of included articles were hand-searched to identify additional articles and primary studies that were missed during electronic database searches. Duplicate search results from database searches were removed and were confirmed by two coders (AMP and CD).

Study selection

2.2 |

Article titles and abstracts were reviewed by two coders (AMP and CD; with high interrater reliability κ = 0.91) and, once excluded, a full text review of articles was conducted to determine eligibility for inclusion. Studies were included in this review if they: (1) were written in English; (2) were conducted with expectant mothers (i.e. women who are currently pregnant); (3) contained at least one measure of sleep; (4) contained at least one measure that assessed eating behaviours (fat intake [meals per week]), PA [MET-min] or GWG [below/within/above recommendations]; and (5) were cross-sectional, longitudinal or intervention studies. Articles were excluded if they: (1) included participants with a sleep disorder diagnosis (e.g. obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, narcolepsy) as those with sleep disorders are being treated for poor sleep/short sleep duration and treatment may influence the results; (2) were review articles; and (3) included duplicated samples. The Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal tool for Cross Sectional Studies and Intervention Studies were used for critical appraisal of bias for each individual study (Moola et al., 2007; Tufanaru et al., 2020). The Downs & Black checklist was used to examine the quality of studies examined (Downs & Black, 1998).

Data extraction

2.3 |

Full-text articles were double-coded by two coders (AMP and CD; with high interrater reliability κ = 0.88); both were trained in standard coding procedures (Higgins et al., 2019). Participant characteristics that were coded included: number of participants, maternal age (in years), socioeconomic status (i.e. the social class of an individual based on education, income and occupation, and calculated based on Kuppuswamy’s socioeconomic scale; Saleem, 2018), marital status, height, weight, body mass index (BMI) category, gestational age at measurement time-point (in weeks), and trimester at measurement time-point. Study characteristics that were coded included: author, year, publication format, journal, study type (cross-sectional, longitudinal, randomized control trial), mode of data collection (i.e. in-person, by mail, online), sample size, relevant data (i.e. means and standard deviations, ranges, p-values), measures of sleep and eating behaviours, PA, GWG outcomes, and response rate. As a result of the search strategy and study selection, 11 studies based on 10,900 participants were retained. The PRISMA flow chart (Moher et al., 2015) summarizing study selection is shown in Figure 1. Supplemental Table 1 highlights the search results from each search database.

Calculating ES

2.4 |

All data were analysed with SPSS v25 (Armonk, NY; IBM). Cohen’s d ES were calculated from means, standard deviations and samples sizes from intervention and control groups (Cohen, 1988). To obtain an unbiased estimate, ES were corrected using the following equation: [1 – (3/([4*n] – 3))] (Hedges, 2007). Weighted mean ES were then calculated using the equation weighted mean ES = (ES/variance)/(1/variance), where variance = ((1 – [ri*ri])*(1 – [ri*ri]))/(n – 1), where ri is the ith correlation (Hedges & Olkin, 1985). Weighting ES allows for those ES with smaller variances to receive a larger weight than those with larger variances. In several cases, it was also appropriate to average multiple weighted ES available within studies, such as studies reporting multiple outcomes of eating behaviours (e.g. caloric intake, intake frequency), PA behaviours (e.g. activity minutes, MET-min, MET/week), and meeting/not meeting GWG recommendations. These cases violated assumptions that ES are independent and over-weighted studies with multiple ES, threatening the validity of the results (Becker, 2000; Borenstein et al., 2009; Dong et al., 2008; Matt & Cook, 2009). Significance was determined by conducting a one-sample t-test to compare the pooled overall ES for sleep behaviours-eating behaviours, sleep behaviours-PA and sleep behaviours-GWG to zero. This was done due to the limited number of studies examining these associations, because mean ES could not be compared within categories (i.e. BMI status, age, treatment group, etc.).

RESULTS

3 |

Study selection

3.1 |

A total of 879 articles were collected; after removing duplicates, 864 (98%) articles remained. After title and abstract review for inclusion/exclusion criteria, 13 (2%) full-text articles were assessed and 11 (85%) were included in this review. The majority of articles were removed because they did not meet the inclusion criteria of targeting pregnant women (30%), or including a measure of sleep and a measure of GWG, eating behaviours or PA (70%).

Participant characteristics

3.2 |

All of the studies included pregnant women (N = 11, 100%); there were a total of N = 10,900 participants across the studies. Participant age was reported in 91.7% (n = 11) of the studies with a range of 26–32 years. Of the 11 studies, 9 (75%) reported the pregnancy trimester at enrolment, with the majority of participants in the third trimester. The majority of participants were married (78.6%), Caucasian (60%) and were normal weight (62.5%; BMI M = 25.4, SD = 2.2 kg m−2). Of the 11 studies included in the review, five (46%) examined sleep behaviour-GWG associations, two (18%) examined sleep behaviour-eating behaviour associations, three (27%) examined sleep behaviour-PA associations, and one study (9%) examined sleep behaviour associations with both eating behaviours and PA. None of the studies examined associations between sleep behaviours and all three target behaviours (i.e. GWG, eating behaviours and PA).

Study characteristics

3.3 |

An evidence table summarizing each study can be found in Supplemental Files. Only 27.3% of studies were conducted in 2010–2015, and the majority were conducted in 2015–2019 (72.7%). No studies were published prior to 2010 illustrating the limited depth of research in this area. The majority of the studies used a cross-sectional design (72.7%), followed by longitudinal design (18.2%), and randomized control trial design (9.1%). All of the studies were published in peer-reviewed journals (100%); most studies (67%) were supported by federal grants. Studies were conducted in the USA (46%), followed by Canada, Australia, Netherlands, Spain, Sri Lanka and Singapore (54%). All studies included were of either moderated or high quality.

Measures

3.4 |

All of the studies utilized self-report measures of sleep (100%), including: Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; 45%), study team member lead interviews (27%), Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System Sleep Questions (9%) and a self-administered sleep questionnaire (9%). Two studies (18%) also included wrist actigraphy in addition to the PSQI. GWG measures included self-report (18%), stadiometer (27%) and medical record data (9%). All studies did not report actual GWG but reported if women gained below/within/above the Institute of Medicine (IOM) GWG recommendations (Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2009). Over half of the studies (55%) measured more than one component of sleep (e.g. sleep duration and sleep quality), and 45% of studies measured one component of sleep (e.g. only sleep quality). Eating behaviour measures were all self-report, and included the 24-hr Rapid Food Screener (9%), Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System Fruit and Vegetable Consumption Questions (9%), 24-hr recall (9%), and the Healthy Eating Index for Pregnant Women in Singapore (9%). PA measures were all self-report, and included interviews to determine PA level/duration (18%), activity log (18%) and the Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System (9%); see Supplemental Files.

ES for the associations between sleep behaviours (sleep duration, sleep quality, nighttime awakenings and daytime nap duration) and eating behaviours, PA and GWG

3.5 |

Individual ES for each study can be found in Supplemental Files, and mean ES for associations between sleep behaviours and eating behaviours, PA and GWG are located in Tables 1, 2 and 3, respectively.

Sleep behaviours-GWG

3.5.1

GWG was assessed by electronic medical record pulls, self-weighing on scales and self-report, then classified if the woman gained below/within/above the IOM GWG recommendations (Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2009). Five studies examined the sleep behaviours-GWG associations with one pooled ES that was small and not significant (ES = 0.29; t = 0.04, p = 0.97; Abeysena & Jayawardana, 2011, Gay et al., 2017, Guinhouya et al., 2018, Merkx et al., 2015, Restall et al., 2014). Two studies examined the sleep quality (PSQI)-GWG association with one calculated ES of 0.51 (Gay et al., 2017; Guinhouya et al., 2018). One study examined the sleep duration (author-developed self-report questionnaire)-GWG association with one calculated ES of 0.26 (Abeysena & Jayawardana, 2011). One study examined the sleep duration (actigraphy)-GWG association with one calculated ES of 0.04 (Gay et al., 2017). One study examined the daytime nap duration (actigraphy)-GWG association with one calculated ES of 0.37 (Gay et al., 2017). There was high heterogeneity for the three studies examining the associations between sleep behaviours and eating behaviours as indicated by I2 = 90.9%.

Sleep behaviours-eating behaviours

3.6 |

The average pooled ES for the three studies examining the associations between sleep behaviours and eating behaviours was small (ES = 0.13) and not significant (t = 0.93, p = 0.36; Chang et al., 2015; Duke et al., 2017; van Lee et al., 2017). There was only one study and one calculated ES of 0.20 for the association between sleep duration (PSQI) and fruit/vegetable intake (24-item Rapid Food Screener; Chang et al., 2015). There was one study with one calculated ES of 0.66 for the association between sleep quality (PSQI) and nighttime fasting interval (hr; Healthy Eating Index for pregnant women in Singapore; van Lee et al., 2017). There was one study that examined the nighttime awakenings (PSQI) and fat intake (kcal; 24-item Rapid Food Screener) association with one calculated ES of 0.39 (Chang et al., 2015). There were no studies examining objective sleep behaviours and eating behaviours, thus no ES were calculated. There was high heterogeneity for the four studies examining the sleep behaviour-PA associations as indicated by I2 = 90.7%.

Sleep behaviours-PA

3.7 |

The average pooled ES for the four studies examining the sleep behaviours-PA associations was small (ES = 0.13); this ES was significant, (t = 4.86, p = 0.000; Baker et al., 2018; Borodulin et al., 2010; Duke et al., 2017; Rodríguez-Blanque et al., 2019). Three studies examined subjective measures of sleep behaviours and PA with one pooled ES of 0.09 (Baker et al., 2018; Borodulin et al., 2010; Duke et al., 2017). There was one study that examined the association between sleep duration (Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System) and PA (yes/no; Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System) with one calculated ES of 0.18 (Duke et al., 2017). There was one study that examined the sleep quality (PSQI)-aerobic and resistance training exercise in water with heart rate and ratings of perceived exertion measured with one calculated ES of 0.59 (Rodríguez-Blanque et al., 2019). There was one study that examined the association between sleep quality (PSQI) and PA (metabolic equivalent [MET]-min; author-developed self-report log with each reported activity assigned a MET value) with one calculated ES of 0.23 (Baker et al., 2018). Lastly, there was one study that examined the association between the overall sleep behaviours (actigraphy) and PA (author-developed self-report log) with one calculated ES of 0.19 (Baker et al., 2018). There was high heterogeneity for the five studies examining the sleep behaviour-GWG associations as indicated by I2 = 99.7%.

DISCUSSION

4 |

The goal of this review was to examine the strength and direction of the associations between prenatal sleep behaviours and eating behaviours, PA and GWG. Eleven studies met the review criteria and generated 11 total ES across 10,900 participants. The 11 studies that were selected either were of moderate to high quality; however, there was high heterogeneity across studies. These conflicting characteristics show the need for studies to utilize the same measurement and study methodologies in order to best understand the interrelationships between these behaviours. The qualities of the studies were moderate to high, which suggests that each study had sound procedures, methods and analyses, but due to the high heterogeneity across studies it may be difficult to develop sound recommendations for prenatal sleep. Because prenatal sleep research in relation to energy balance factors is in its infancy, these conflicting characteristics are not as surprising as one may have assumed.

Of the 11 studies, three examined the associations between sleep behaviours and eating behaviours, four studies examining associations between sleep behaviours and PA, and five studies examining associations between sleep behaviours and GWG (one study included multiple behaviours). The majority of the studies were conducted after 2010, which highlights the infancy of this area of research. Overall, the strength of the associations between sleep behaviours and components of energy balance were small; the only ES that emerged as significant was for the pooled sleep behaviours-PA association. The largest pooled ES from the studies that were reviewed was for the sleep behaviour-GWG association (ES = 0.29), such that negative sleep behaviours were associated with inadequate weight gain among pregnant women with normal weight. However, the one study that examined sleep and GWG in pregnant women with overweight/obesity found that negative sleep behaviours were associated with excessive GWG. The strongest individual association was between sleep quality (measured by the PSQI) and eating behaviour (measured as the nighttime fasting interval, or the time at night between eating in hr; ES = 0.66), such that poorer sleep quality was associated with a shorter nighttime fasting interval (e.g. frequent eating during the night). Despite the small averaged pooled effects, these findings are nevertheless important because they are among the first to show the magnitude and direction of associations between prenatal sleep behaviours and eating behaviours (ES range 0.002–0.66), PA (ES range 0.03–0.59) and GWG (ES range 0.03–0.42). It is also important to note this study highlights the significant association between sleep behaviours and PA, which has not been definitive in the prenatal literature. These findings can lay the groundwork for future studies aiming to better understand the influence of sleep behaviours in energy balance to regulate GWG. Further discussion of these findings is provided below.

First, while there were 11 studies that met the study inclusion criteria, none of the studies examined prenatal sleep behaviours with all three target behaviours (eating/PA behaviours and GWG); and only one study examined sleep behaviours with both eating and PA behaviours (Duke et al., 2017). This study found that nighttime sleep duration had a small and negative association with fruit and vegetable consumption (ES = 0.04), but had a small and positive association with PA such that pregnant women who reported to have exercised over a 30-day period reported 20 min of additional sleep per night compared with those who did not exercise (ES = 0.18; Duke et al., 2017). Thus, while prenatal nighttime sleep duration may not be strongly related to fruit/vegetable consumption, prenatal nighttime sleep duration is related to PA, though small. This finding is promising that in relation to energy balance, good sleep behaviours may be related to increases in PA, which could compensate for unhealthy eating behaviours. The remaining 10 studies examined associations between sleep behaviours and the individual target behaviours: eating behaviours (N = 2), PA (N = 3) and GWG (N = 5). In contrast to the prediction that the ES associations would be medium, the average strengths of the associations were small. While the sleep behaviours-eating behaviours and sleep behaviours-GWG ES were not significant, the pooled ES for sleep behaviours-PA did emerge as significant. That is, good prenatal sleep behaviours (i.e. 7–9 hr of nighttime sleep duration, good sleep quality) were associated with higher levels/duration of prenatal PA. This finding is particularly interesting given the literature in this area among pregnant and non-pregnant individuals has generated inconsistent findings. As such, a potential reason for the equivocal and small findings is due to inconsistent measurement methods across and within studies. Across studies, the sleep behaviour and PA measures varied, with the majority being all self-report. The PA measures were either a self-report interview or log, and the respective authors converted the data to a PA intensity value (MET). This could have led to issues with self-report bias and increased variability across studies. More consistent measurement methods should be used and actigraphy measures should be incorporated, especially when examining sleep and PA behaviours in relation to one another.

In partial support of the hypothesis, poor sleep behaviours were associated with both insufficient and excessive GWG. For example, one study found there was an association with increased sleep deprivation and gaining weight below or within the IOM guidelines such that with a 1 day per week increase in perceived sleep deprivation, women did not gain or met the recommended amount of GWG (Merkx et al., 2015), Also, weight gain below the IOM guidelines was seen more often in women who perceived they were more sleep deprived (Merkx et al., 2015). Moreover, the individual associations showed the daytime nap duration-GWG association was small but positive, suggesting that longer daytime nap duration was associated with higher GWG. Daytime napping is often reported in pregnancy, with some estimates showing that up to 78% of pregnant women take daytime naps (Mindell et al., 2015). While napping is encouraged during pregnancy, taking naps that are too long (> 90 min) and taking too many naps (> 4 naps) may be problematic for obtaining optimal nighttime sleep duration, sleep quality and regulating GWG (Ebert et al., 2015; Mindell et al., 2015; Ohayon et al., 2017). The findings of this current study – specifically, the associations between low nighttime awakenings (< 1) and short daytime nap duration (< 90 min per day) with sufficient GWG may provide the initial evidence to inform prenatal sleep recommendations to regulate GWG. It should also be noted that associations between poor sleep behaviours and excessive GWG were seen in pregnant women with overweight/obesity, which warrants future research because this group of women are at greater risk for both sleep disturbances and excessive GWG (Borodulin et al., 2009; Gay et al., 2017; Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2009, Nunnery et al., 2018; Rice et al., 2015; Sebire et al., 2001). The combined findings from this review may be useful to researchers and intervention specialists by suggesting there is relevance to understanding the associations between prenatal sleep behaviours and GWG. Future prenatal intervention studies may want to consider tailoring education on sleep behaviour content based on weight status as there were different findings for normal weight and pregnant women with overweight/obesity. For example, women with overweight/obesity are at a greater risk for snoring, restless legs syndrome and feeling less comfortable in bed compared with normal-weight women, and strategies should be developed to overcome these issues.

Also, in partial support of the hypotheses and past researchers’ conclusions (Al Khatib et al., 2017; Baron et al., 2011; Markwald et al., 2013; St-Onge, 2013), the sleep behaviours-eating behaviours associations were negative such that poor sleep behaviours (i.e. short nighttime sleep duration, poor sleep quality, high number of awakenings, long daytime nap duration) were associated with negative eating behaviours (i.e. shorter fasting periods, high energy intake, high intake of high fat/unhealthy foods). Moreover, one study observed that good sleep quality was associated with better diet quality and greater adherence to eating vegetables and fruits (van Lee et al., 2017). The strongest ES for the sleep behaviour-eating behaviour associations was between the PSQI global sleep quality and nighttime fasting interval (hr) such that poor sleep quality was associated with shorter time spans between eating episodes (van Lee et al., 2017). That is, when sleep quality is poor and a person spends more time awake, it appears to increase the opportunities to eat, when normally one would be sleeping. For example, Chaput (2014) conducted a review that summarized mechanisms by which insufficient sleep may increase caloric consumption in the general population. Their results suggested that short sleep duration, poor sleep quality and later bedtimes are all associated with increased food consumption and poor dietary habits such that those who sleep less are more likely to snack and consume unhealthy foods at any time during the day compared with those who have normal sleep duration (7–9 hr per night; Chaput, 2014). The findings of this current study may be used to inform the development of prenatal sleep recommendations such as meeting 7–9 hr per night and striving for good-quality sleep as these might promote healthy eating behaviours. These findings may also inform future study and intervention development as they illustrate the importance of educating pregnant women about how good sleep behaviours are crucial to promote healthy eating behaviours and to reduce the likelihood of snacking, eating unhealthy foods, and eating into the night instead of sleeping.

Furthermore, good prenatal sleep behaviours (i.e. 7–9 hr of nighttime sleep duration, good sleep quality) were positively associated with higher levels/time spent in PA, and this was the only significant association. This finding is important for developing prenatal sleep recommendations such that the 7–9 hr per night guideline among the general population may also be advantageous for pregnant women to promote healthy sleep and PA behaviours. For example, there was higher sleep continuity (i.e. amount and distribution of sleep versus wakefulness in a given sleep period) and lower wake after sleep onset in women who engaged in ≥ 500 MET-min per week compared with women who did not exercise at all (Baker et al., 2018; Hall et al., 2018). The majority of women expressed engaging in some PA (59%); and while not significant, those women had longer self-report (446.6 min versus 474.1 min and 460.4 min) and actigraphy (304.6 min versus 325.4 min and 317.5 min) measured total sleep time, and lower self-report sleep quality scores (6.4 versus 5.5 and 5.3), respectively, compared with those who were inactive (Baker et al., 2018). The strongest ES was between PSQI global sleep quality and water-based aerobic and resistance exercise such that women who engaged in the exercise had better sleep quality than those who did not. However, this mean ES was based on only two individual ES from a single study (Rodríguez-Blanque et al., 2019) and, thus, these results should be interpreted with some caution. It should also be noted that the measurement methods for both the sleep and PA behaviours varied across studies. None of these studies utilized actigraphy for PA and relied on self-report logs and questionnaires, which could have introduced error and misreporting. More consistent measurement tools are needed across and within studies in order to have the most accurate understanding of these behaviours. Moreover, in contrast to the non-pregnancy literature, the findings of this study are important in clarifying the inconsistencies such that good sleep behaviours are positively associated with higher PA behaviours. Future interventions may want to include education on how good sleep behaviours are crucial to promote PA behaviours and to reduce the likelihood of increasing sedentary behaviours and daytime fatigue.

Implications and future directions

4.1 |

This review highlights that poor sleep quality, shorter sleep duration, increased nighttime disturbances and increased daytime nap duration were associated with poor prenatal eating behaviours, low PA and excessive GWG, but further research is needed to replicate and confirm these study findings. The majority of the studies in this review were cross-sectional, which also limits the ability to make assumptions about causal relationships between sleep behaviours and GWG, eating behaviours and PA. Future randomized trials and experimental studies examining the effect of sleep behaviours on energy balance are warranted in this area. In addition, only two of the studies included objective assessments of sleep behaviours (18%); however, no study included objective assessments of GWG, eating behaviours and PA. This is important because pregnant women often over- and under-report their behaviours on self- report measures, and there has been a recent increase in studies using actigraphy to measure sleep behaviours (Bei et al., 2010; Herring et al., 2013). More research is needed to determine if actigraphy can be used in replacement for self-report measures during pregnancy. The studies also focused on a wide variety of eating behaviours (e.g. nighttime fasting interval, fruit, vegetable and fat intake); however, no study specifically examined the relationship between sleep behaviours and energy intake (i.e. calorie intake), which is a major gap in the research. Prenatal recommendations are based on caloric intake, but the study findings do assume that sleep behaviours are associated with higher caloric intake (i.e. increased fat intake). However, only one study examined the association between sleep behaviours and overall caloric intake; additional research examining this association is needed to inform how sleep behaviours may influence the change of caloric intake over pregnancy. This in turn may lead to women exceeding their recommended caloric intake and gaining excessive GWG.

Furthermore, there was a lack of heterogeneity with the study and participant characteristics. A few sources of heterogeneity include: differences in sample sizes across studies, and variability of study measures within and across studies. For example, the majority of the studies included pregnant women with normal weight (63% of studies). However, pregnant women with overweight/obesity are at a higher risk for sleep disturbances, low levels of PA and high GWG compared with normal weight pregnant women (Borodulin et al., 2009; Gay et al., 2017; Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2009; Nunnery et al., 2018; Rice et al., 2015; Sebire et al., 2001). Thus, future research is needed to understand the impact of weight status on the associations between sleep behaviours and these outcomes. In addition, the majority of the studies (63%) included pregnant women in their third trimester, which did not provide insight about how sleep, GWG and eating/PA behaviours are associated over the course of pregnancy. This research is needed because prior studies have found that sleep, eating and PA behaviours worsen over the course of pregnancy, which could elevate the risk for excessive GWG (Borodulin et al., 2009; Cohen & Koski, 2013; Hedman et al., 2002; Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2006, 2009; Jung & Choi, 2017; Kennedy et al., 2017; Mindell & Jacobson, 2000; Schmid et al., 2009; Schweiger, 1972). Other related covariates that warrant attention include: (1) meeting/not meeting national recommendations for energy intake, PA and sleep recommendations; (2) socioeconomic status; (3) education level; (4) marital status; and (5) race/ethnicity. Past research has shown that pregnant women often do not meet energy intake and PA recommendations. Further, failure to meet the sleep recommendations for the general population has been associated with increased energy intake, decreased PA and increased GWG (Borodulin et al., 2010; Duke et al., 2017; Gay et al., 2017). Determining if these recommendations influence one another is important to understand at what levels energy intake, PA and sleep behaviours may become harmful. For example, if a woman is only achieving 6 hr of sleep each night, this may influence their energy intake to increase above their recommended amount and they may only achieve 90 min of PA each day. These interrelationships may increase the risks for adverse pregnancy outcomes. Also, past researchers have shown that pregnant women with low socioeconomic status and education level, who are single, and within racial/ethnic minority groups are at increased risk for short/long sleep duration and sleep disturbances, unhealthy energy intake levels, decreased PA and excessive GWG, and more research is warranted to examine how these factors influence these interrelationships (Feinstein et al., 2020; Gamba et al., 2019; Gay et al., 2004; Liu et al., 2014; Okun et al., 2014; Xu et al., 2017). Lastly, given the high heterogeneity across the studies, random effect models were used, which suggest the variability between the ES is due to sampling error and unique differences across the studies. This may suggest that each study is estimating different population ES, which calls for more homogenous samples and measurement tools and protocols.

CONCLUSION

5 |

To the author’s knowledge, this is one of the first systematic reviews to examine associations between prenatal sleep behaviours and components of energy balance. In summary, the magnitude of the ES for associations between sleep behaviours and GWG, eating behaviours and PA were small, and the findings illustrated that poor sleep quality, shorter sleep duration, increased nighttime disturbances and increased daytime nap duration were associated with insufficient and excessive GWG, poor prenatal eating behaviours and PA. Given the infancy of this field of research and because nearly all of the studies included in this review were cross-sectional, there is a need for future randomized control and experimental trials to establish causal relationships between prenatal sleep behaviours and components of energy balance. The limited diversity across the studies with respect to the participant characteristics (e.g. married, normal weight, Caucasian and in their third trimester) and measures (e.g. PSQI) suggests there is a need for more diverse samples and measures to better understand these interrelationships and the influences of other factors (i.e. marital status, weight status, trimester, measurement method) on associations between sleep behaviours and components of energy balance. Overall, these findings can provide the basis for future research and intervention development that includes education on improving sleep behaviours and examining the effects of normal sleep duration of 7–9 hr per night, reducing the number of awakenings to 1 per night, and limiting daytime nap duration to 90 min or less on effectively managing GWG, reducing risks for unhealthy snacking and increasing PA over the course of pregnancy.
