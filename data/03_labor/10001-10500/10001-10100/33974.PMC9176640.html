The Effect of Transfer Status on Trauma Outcomes in Low- and Middle-Income Countries: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.

Avital Yohann[1]

Brittney Williams[1]

Anthony Charles[1][2]

[1] Department of Surgery, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, USA

[2] Kamuzu Central Hospital, Lilongwe, Malawi

Abstract

Introduction:

Injuries are a leading cause of disability and death worldwide, and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are disproportionately burdened by trauma. Prior studies have shown that transfer status (direct transfer from injury scene to a referral hospital versus indirect transfer from another facility to a referral hospital) may affect patient outcomes. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the relationship between transfer status and trauma patient outcomes in LMICs by conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Methods:

We performed a systematic search to identify studies from LMICs that evaluated the relationship between transfer status and trauma patient outcomes. We extracted data on study country, design, patient characteristics, and outcomes. We report results in the form of a narrative summary stratified by type of outcome. We also performed a meta-analysis of studies that reported mortality by transfer status. We calculated a pooled odds ratio of mortality among indirectly transferred (IT) versus directly transferred (DT) patients using random-effects modeling.

Results:

We included 17 observational studies from 9 LMICs in this systematic review. Outcomes assessed were time from injury to arrival at a referral hospital, post-trauma functional status, hospital length of stay, and mortality. IT patients took between 0.6 and 37.9 hours longer to arrive at referral hospitals than DT patients. Hospital length of stay was up to 6 days longer for IT patients than DT patients. The pooled odds ratio of mortality among IT patients compared to DT patients was 1.55 (95% CI 1.12 – 2.15; p=0.009).

Conclusion:

Trauma patients in LMICs who are indirectly transferred to referral hospitals have significantly higher mortality rates than patients who present directly to referral hospitals. These results conflict with findings from HICs and reflect the relative immaturity of trauma systems in LMICs. Strategies to narrow the mortality gap between IT and DT patients include improving prehospital and primary hospital care and developing more efficient transfer protocols.

Introduction

Injuries are responsible for an estimated 7.5% of all deaths worldwide.1 Road traffic injury alone is the leading cause of death among adolescents and young adults globally.2 Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are disproportionately burdened by trauma, and 90% of injury-related deaths occur in LMICs.2 It is estimated that nearly 2 million lives could be saved annually in LMICs in the presence of functional trauma systems.3

Trauma systems, the coordinated processes for providing care to injured patients, have been shown to reduce trauma mortality by up to 25%.4 Many LMICs lack some or all of the components of formal trauma systems, including prehospital emergency medical services, provider education, and patient triage, transfer, and management protocols.5 An essential aspect of trauma systems is the transfer of more severely injured patients to higher levels of care. Transfer can happen in two ways: a patient can be transported from the injury scene directly to a trauma center (direct transfer), or a patient can be taken to another health care facility and then transferred to a trauma center (indirect transfer).

It is unclear whether and under what circumstances direct or indirect transfer is preferable. While direct transfer reduces time to definitive management, indirect transfer may result in early resuscitation and stabilization at a facility closer to the injury scene. Two systematic reviews and meta-analyses have evaluated the relationship between transfer status (direct versus indirect) and trauma patient outcomes.6,7 Neither found a significant association between transfer status and mortality, but most studies in these reviews were from high-income countries (HICs).

The differences in trauma system structure and maturity in LMICs compared to HICs likely impact the relationship between transfer status and patient outcomes. An understanding of this relationship is critical for trauma system design and protocol development in LMICs. We therefore performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effect of transfer status on trauma patient outcomes in LMICs.

Methods

Search Strategy

We queried PubMed, Scopus, and Global Health databases from January 1, 2001, to March 18, 2021. With the assistance of a health science librarian, we developed the search strategy to identify studies related to the following four topics: trauma, patient transfer, outcomes, and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). MESH terms and text words for these topics included “wounds and injuries,” “trauma centers,” “accidents,” “patient transfer,” “interfacility transfer,” “treatment outcome,” “hospital mortality,” “developing country,” “low-income country,” “middle-income country,” and others. The search also included the name of each country classified as low- or middle-income by the World Bank.8 The complete search strategy is available in Appendix A. In addition, we assessed references of all included studies and related reviews for potential eligibility, and we also searched clinicaltrials.gov to identify potentially relevant unpublished data.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they compared outcomes of indirectly transferred (IT) trauma patients with directly transferred (DT) trauma patients. Eligible outcomes included the following: time from injury to care, need for ICU admission, hospital length of stay, ICU length of stay, condition at discharge, discharge disposition, and mortality. Studies that reported outcomes stratified by transfer status were eligible for inclusion even if transfer status was not the primary exposure. Studies that included burn or pediatric patients were eligible for inclusion, but we excluded studies of exclusively burn or pediatric patients. Study designs eligible for inclusion were randomized trials and observational cohort or cross-sectional studies. We provide a complete list of inclusion and exclusion criteria in Appendix B.

Study Selection, Data Extraction, and Quality Assessment

We imported search results to Covidence (www.covidence.org, Melbourne, Australia). We utilized the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for study selection. Two reviewers (AY, IK) screened the abstracts of each study. We again reviewed full-text articles of studies marked as potentially eligible using the same criteria. Conflicts were resolved by discussion and by a third reviewer (AC).

The following information was extracted from each included study: year of publication, country of study, study design, characteristics of included patients, total number of included patients, number of direct and indirect transfers, measures of injury severity, outcomes measured, type of mortality assessed, and outcomes by transfer status. We attempted to contact study authors for clarification in cases where outcomes were not clearly stratified by transfer status. If we could not reach the authors, we excluded the study. Two reviewers (AY, BW) performed data extraction.

Two reviewers (AY, IK) assessed individual study quality using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies.9 Each study was given a rating of good, fair, or poor by each reviewer. Using 14 key concepts as a guide, the rating is based on the judgment of the overall study quality rather than on the number of criteria met. We resolved differences in ratings through discussion. Studies were not excluded based on quality.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

Due to significant heterogeneity in setting, population, study design, and outcomes assessed, data were primarily synthesized in a narrative format. We categorized studies based on the types of outcomes reported and present a descriptive summary. For each outcome, we summarized effect measures reported by the individual studies. In cases where data was provided in individual proportions or percentages without effect measures, we calculated risk ratios. We reported results of statistical significance testing (confidence intervals and p-values) if they were available. We extracted and summarized the following data: study year, country, design, size, patient population, measures of injury severity, and outcomes. Mortality data collected included type of mortality measured, mortality rates of direct and indirect transfers, unadjusted point estimates, and, if available, adjusted point estimates.

We performed a meta-analysis including only those studies that provided mortality outcomes to estimate the effect of transfer status on patient mortality. Adjusted odds ratios of mortality were used in the meta-analysis when they were available. Otherwise, we calculated odds ratios using the data provided in the study results. We performed the analysis using a random-effects model and assessed heterogeneity using the I2 statistic with greater than 50% indicating substantial statistical heterogeneity. To explain statistical heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses by region and by country income level. We performed all data analysis using STATA (release 16, College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

The search yielded 1491 unique studies, and we excluded 1448 studies during abstract screening. Of the 43 that underwent full-text review, 17 met all eligibility criteria and were included (Table 1).10–26 No additional relevant studies were identified by hand-searching references or from clinicaltrials.gov. A flow chart of the study selection process is shown in Figure 1.

Included studies enrolled participants from India (k=4),12,17,19,22 Nigeria (k=4),10,14,18,20 South Africa (k=2),13,16 and one study each from Pakistan,21 Egypt,24 Malaysia,25 Myanmar,15 Ethiopia,26 and Malawi.11 In addition, one multicenter study included data from 18 LMICs.23 The number of patients in the included studies ranged from 68 to 50,059. Most studies were prospective cohort (10 studies), and the remainder were retrospective cohort (3 studies)11,13,16 and cross-sectional (4 studies).14,20,24,26 Outcomes measured by transfer status included time from injury to arrival at a referral hospital, post-trauma functional status, hospital length of stay, and mortality.

Time from Injury to Arrival

Eight studies reported the time from injury to arrival at a referral hospital.11,12,16–18,23–25 In each of these studies, patients in the IT group took longer to arrive at a referral hospital than patients in the DT group. Seven studies reported mean or median hours from injury to presentation, and IT patients took between 0.56 and 37.91 hours longer to present than DT patients. The difference in time to presentation was statistically significant in 6 of these 7 studies (The 7th study did not report results of statistical testing).12

The 8th study assessed the proportion of patients who experienced delayed care after sustaining fractures rather than the mean time to presentation.23 The authors defined delayed care as longer than 2 hours from injury to arrival at a treating hospital for open fractures and longer than 24 hours for closed fractures. After adjusting for region, age, employment, urban living, health insurance, mode of transport, number of fractures, mechanism of injury, and fracture location, IT compared to DT was associated with an OR of delay of 7.89 for open fractures and 3.66 for closed fracture (p<0.0001 for both). No other studies performed adjusted analyses for the time from injury to arrival at a referral hospital.

Functional Status at or after Discharge

Functional status after trauma was reported by 3 studies. Of these, 2 studies utilized the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) but reported outcomes at different time points.18,22 The GOS categorizes outcomes after traumatic brain injury (TBI) using a 5-point scale. GOS of 4 or higher (baseline functioning status or minor impairment) is considered favorable, while GOS of less than 4 (significant impairment, vegetative state, or death) is unfavorable. One study reported that 64.7% of DT patients had a favorable outcome at discharge compared to 71% of IT patients.22 Statistical testing was not reported in this study. The second study measured GOS 3 months post-injury and found that 89.1% of DT patients and 93.3% of IT patients had a favorable outcome (p=0.40).18 Although the results were not statistically significant, both studies found a higher proportion of favorable outcomes among IT patients.

The 3rd study reported condition at discharge among patients with cervical spinal cord injury using the Spinal Cord Independence Measure (SCIM).14 The SCIM is a 100-point scale that measures activities of daily living, coordination, eating, functional mobility, and incontinence after spinal cord injury.27 Higher scores indicate better functional status, and a 10-point difference is associated with substantial improvement in functional status.27 In this study, 61.1% of DT patients and 26% of IT patients had a SCIM score of over 50 at discharge, and 16.67% of DT and 62% of IT patients had a score of 25 or lower (p=0.003).

Length of Stay

Five studies compared hospital length of stay among DT versus IT patients.11,16–18,25 Within these studies, length of stay was between 0 and 6 days longer in IT patients compared to DT patients. Three of the 5 studies found a significantly shorter length of stay in the DT group.11,17,25 In the 4th study, the median length of stay was 3 days longer in the IT group compared to the DT group, but statistical testing was not performed.18 In the 5th study, the median length of stay was 5 days for both groups.16

Mortality

Thirteen studies reported patient mortality by transfer status and were included in the meta-analysis (Table 2).10,11,13,15–22,25,26 These studies included a total of 15,424 patients. We contacted the authors of 2 studies to clarify mortality rates in the IT and DT cohorts.10,20 We used adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals in the 3 studies that performed regression analyses.10,11,19 For the remainder of the studies, we calculated odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals from study data.

Two of the included studies reported 2 different mortality estimates (either using different subsets of patients within their study or excluding early deaths).13,17 To account for the 2 mortality estimates in these studies, we ran the meta-analysis twice. First, to obtain the more conservative pooled estimate, results favoring the IT group in each study were used (Figure 2A). Next, results that favored the DT group in each study were used (Figure 2B). Data from the other 11 studies remained unchanged in these 2 analyses. Using results that favored the IT group, the pooled odds ratio of mortality in IT patients versus DT patients was 1.55 (95% CI 1.12 – 2.15; p=0.009). Using results that favored the DT group, the pooled odds ratio was 1.70 (95% CI 1.29 – 2.24; p<0.001). There was significant heterogeneity in both iterations (I2 = 72.72% and 57.44%, respectively).

We performed all subsequent analyses using the more conservative data used for the first pooled estimate above. To explain heterogeneity, we performed a subgroup analysis by WHO region (Figure 3).28 Among studies conducted in South East Asia (k=4), I2 decreased to 53.63%. Among studies performed in Africa (k=7), however, I2 increased to 85.27%. Other regions only included one study. Next, we performed a subgroup analysis by county income level (low, lower-middle, or upper-middle according to the World Bank).8 I2 decreased to 0% in the 2 studies from low-income countries but remained the same or increased in studies from lower-middle or upper-middle-income countries (Appendix C).

Quality of included studies

Using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies, each study was assigned a good, fair, or poor rating by each reviewer. After reconciling differences through discussion, 3 studies were rated as good, 13 as fair, and one as poor quality (Appendix D). All studies were observational. A majority of studies did not adjust estimates for potential confounders. In some cases, this was because the study’s primary objective was something other than assessing the relationship between transfer status and outcomes. Regardless, studies that did not adjust for confounders received a maximum rating of fair. Another common issue was the use of unreliable measurements, such as asking patients or caregivers to recall the time from injury to arrival at the health care facility. Studies that included multiple of the following characteristics were more likely to be rated as poor quality: cross-sectional design, concern for measurement bias, unclear source population or eligibility criteria, and no adjustment for potential confounders.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study of its kind to evaluate the relationship between transfer status and trauma patient outcomes in LMICs. Pooled odds of mortality were 1.6 times higher among IT patients than DT patients. IT patients also took longer to arrive at referral hospitals and generally had more prolonged hospitalizations than DT patients.

Our mortality results conflict with the two previous systematic reviews on this topic. Williams et al. found a reduced risk of mortality in the IT group compared to the DT group, though the results were not statistically significant (pooled OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.77 – 1.08).7 Hill et al. found that IT patients had significantly longer times to definitive care and longer hospital and ICU length of stay. Still, mortality rates did not significantly differ from DT patients (pooled OR 1.06, 95% CI 0.9 – 1.25).6 However, both of these reviews included almost exclusively studies performed in HICs.

The results of our review likely reflect the immature trauma systems of the countries from which studies were included. In particular, the higher mortality rates in the IT group may be attributable to inadequate care at hospitals before transfer and unsafe and uncoordinated transfer to referral hospitals. For example, Radjou et al. reported that lack of provider education at non-trauma centers in India results in providers prioritizing treatments such as wound suturing and dressing rather than resuscitation, management of life-threatening injuries, or rapid transfer.12 Similarly, Dharap et al. found that 35% of IT patients required intubation, and 13% required chest tube placement within one hour of arrival at a referral center.19 This is at least partially attributable to inadequate management before transfer. Perhaps not surprisingly, IT patients in their cohort had nearly twice the odds of in-hospital mortality compared to DT patients.19

Deficiencies in the transfer process itself may also negatively impact patient outcomes. For example, Radjou et al. found that 30% of IT patients were evaluated at more than one facility before reaching a referral center.12 Moreover, the referral center only had prior knowledge of the patients before transfer in 0.8% of cases.12 In their study of TBI patients in Ethiopia, Laeke et al. cautioned against equating ambulance transport with medical care.26 In their setting, ambulances are not equipped with trained providers, monitoring instruments, or life support equipment and are therefore solely transport vehicles.26 Mezue et al. commented on inadequate spine stabilization during the interhospital transfer of patients with cervical spinal cord injuries in Nigeria.14 In their study, over half of IT patients transported in ambulances were transported without neck support.14 This may partially explain the significantly worse functional status at discharge in the IT group than the DT group.

Addressing the discrepancy in outcomes between IT and DT patients by solely focusing on increasing the proportion of trauma patients directly transferred to referral hospitals is neither feasible nor advisable. First, prehospital care is nonexistent or in the early stages of development in many LMICs.5 In the absence of prehospital care, the first facility where care is sought is usually the nearest to the injury scene and is often ill-equipped to manage trauma patients.11,12 Until emergency medical transportation becomes more reliable, patients will likely continue going to the nearest hospital. Interestingly, there is also evidence suggesting that in some settings, most transferred patients may be overtriaged. A study from Malawi found that over 50% of patients transferred from a district hospital to KCH for trauma care were discharged from the ED or the wards within 48 hours without undergoing a procedure.29 Nearly 90% of these overtriaged patients were discharged from the ED.29 Additional unnecessary transfers will likely overburden busy referral hospitals and lead to further delays to care and worse outcomes for many patients.

Instead, we must address the underlying factors that lead to higher mortality rates in IT patients. Strengthening prehospital systems and providing trauma education to providers at non-referral hospitals have both been shown to improve trauma care and outcomes and should continue to be encouraged.30,31 Improving trauma care coordination through government agencies, centralized dispatches, or other entities is recommended to facilitate direct and indirect transfers.23,32 In particular, strategies should prioritize minimizing the number of patients transferred more than once in areas where this is an issue.12 Lastly, protocols for triage and transfer are needed.23,32 These protocols should be tailored to the specific settings in which they are to be used and should consider factors such as resources available at referral and non-referral hospitals, prehospital systems, and patient and injury characteristics.

To inform the development of trauma transfer protocols, however, we must also understand the characteristics and outcomes of non-transferred patients. Apart from direct and indirect transfers, non-transferred patients comprise a 3rd cohort of trauma patients who are never transferred to referral hospitals because they are either treated successfully or die before transfer. Patients who die before transfer have been termed “potential transfers,” as they likely would have been transferred had they survived long enough. Not including these patients in direct and indirect transfers comparisons may introduce a survivor bias in favor of indirect transfers. Several of the studies included in this review and one of the previous systematic reviews on this topic emphasized the need for further information about the non-transferred cohort.6,11,17

In Ontario, Canada, Haas et al. studied deaths in directly transferred, indirectly transferred, and non-transferred trauma patients. To eliminate possible survivor bias of indirect transfers, they included potential transfers in the IT group. Adjusted odds of mortality in this group were 1.24 times higher than in the DT group.33 In our review, Cheddie et al. also attempted to eliminate survivor bias. However, as they did not have access to data on potential transfers, they instead excluded early deaths from the IT and DT groups. Doing so resulted in significantly higher mortality in the IT group.13 If more studies had included potential transfers, results likely would have provided even more robust evidence for a survival advantage among DT patients. Still, we need additional research on this topic. Ultimately, the non-transferred cohort should be included in future studies both to eliminate potential survivor bias in IT patients and to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of current transfer practices and their effects on patient outcomes.

This study has several limitations. The meta-analysis had substantial statistical heterogeneity even after performing subgroup analyses by region and country income level. Heterogeneity is likely due to a combination of factors including differences in patient populations, study design, outcomes assessed, and individual trauma systems (prehospital care, primary hospital management, and transfer practices). Specifically, these differences may have contributed to varying degrees of survivor bias among the IT cohort. In addition, all studies were observational and therefore inherently subject to bias. Only three of the included studies adjusted for potential confounders when comparing mortality rates in the IT and DT groups. The remaining studies were therefore at higher risk of bias due to confounding. Lastly, either due to the search strategy or the selection process, all possible relevant studies may not have been included in the review.

Conclusion

In LMICs, direct transfer to a referral hospital is associated with a survival benefit compared to indirect transfer from another facility. Unlike in HICs, trauma patients in LMICs often do not receive prehospital care. Those who initially present to primary or district hospitals are unlikely to receive appropriate resuscitation or stabilization. The absence of protocols and infrastructure may also lead to inefficient transfer. These factors jointly contribute to higher mortality rates among indirectly transferred patients, and improving outcomes for these patients will best be achieved by strengthening the trauma system as a whole. Future research should focus on including both transferred and non-transferred patients to fully understand the state of trauma care in LMICs and to make informed recommendations for developing transfer protocols.

Highlights

  • In LMICs, indirect transfer to a trauma center is associated with a longer time to treatment and longer hospital length of stay.

  • The odds of trauma mortality are 1.6 times higher among indirectly transferred compared to directly transferred patients.

  • Higher mortality among indirectly transferred patients is likely a result of immature trauma systems in LMICs.
