Abstract

Background:

Up to 1 in 5 children are exposed to maternal depressive symptoms. Children exposed to maternal depressive symptoms have poorer language skills than children not exposed to maternal depressive symptoms. Due to the crucial role of children’s language skills in school readiness and academic achievement, it is imperative to understand the factors that underlie the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills. Previous reviews have examined the mechanistic role of social-pragmatic features of mother-child interactions. However, the literature on the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and data-providing aspects of mother-child interactions, including child-directed speech, has yet to be consolidated. In this systematic review, we present maternal child-directed speech as a potential pathway through which maternal depressive symptoms influence children’s language skills.

Methods:

Following PRISMA guidelines, three database searches produced 546 articles related to maternal depressive symptoms, child-directed speech, and children’s language skills, ten of which examined the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and child-directed speech.

Results:

Findings revealed that increases in maternal depressive symptoms may be associated with decreases in amount of child-directed speech but not necessarily with decreases in the complexity of child-directed speech.

Limitations:

The studies in this review varied in sample size, the inclusion of important sociodemographic factors, and the operationalization of depression and child-directed speech, thereby limiting conclusions, especially about whether maternal depressive symptoms are associated with the complexity of child-directed speech.

Conclusions:

This review has implications for prevention and intervention efforts aimed at optimizing children’s language skills; child-directed speech is modifiable, and mothers experiencing depressive symptoms may benefit from resources encouraging rich child-directed speech.

Introduction

Children’s language skills are powerful predictors of important life outcomes, like academic achievement (Pace et al., 2019). Thus, understanding the factors in children’s lives that predict these individual differences in children’s language skills is imperative. Researchers often study the role of parent-child interactions in the development of language skills, and among various aspects of parent-child interactions, child-directed speech is especially essential for children’s language (Rowe & Snow, 2019). However, while the relation between child-directed speech and children’s language skills is well-characterized, less is known about sources of variation in child-directed speech. In this systematic review, we highlight maternal depression as one factor that might undermine child-directed speech.

Up to 1 in 5 children are exposed to maternal depressive symptoms (Gavin et al., 2005), and maternal depressive symptoms have been shown to undermine various parenting practices that are linked to favorable child outcomes. Many prior reviews have consolidated the literature on relations between maternal depressive symptoms and various aspects of parenting (e.g., warmth, sensitivity, attachment). However, the literature on the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and the aspects of parenting most paramount to children’s language skills—that is, child-directed speech—has yet to be consolidated. Here, our goal is to synthesize the existing literature related to the impact of maternal depression on the characteristics of child-directed speech that have been shown to influence children’s language skills. Consolidating the literature on this relation will provide insight into appropriate prevention and intervention recommendations for children exposed to maternal depressive symptoms, as both child-directed speech and maternal depressive symptoms are modifiable (Forman et al., 2007; Leech et al., 2018). It will also illuminate gaps in the literature that should be addressed by future studies.

Children’s Language Skills and Child-Directed Speech

Children’s language skills are powerful predictors of important outcomes, including school readiness, academic achievement, socioemotional outcomes, and behavioral outcomes (Bleses et al., 2016; Demir-Lira et al., 2018; Girard et al., 2016; Kuhn et al., 2016; Marchman & Fernald, 2008; Pace et al., 2019; Peyre et al., 2016; Rose, Weinert, et al., 2018; Salmon et al., 2016; Suggate et al., 2018; Uccelli et al., 2019). In a recent study that included over 1,300 children in the United States, Pace et al. (2019) demonstrated that language skills at kindergarten entry consistently predicted academically-relevant skills in later grades, both within domains (e.g. language skills) and across domains (e.g. mathematics, reading, social skills). Early language skills also predict other important aspects of school readiness, like executive function (Kuhn et al., 2016) and socioemotional development (Girard et al., 2016; Rose, Lehrl, et al., 2018; Rose, Weinert, et al., 2018).

Child-directed speech has repeatedly been shown to predict children’s language skills (Demir-Lira et al., 2018; Fernald et al., 2013; Hurtado et al., 2008; Huttenlocher et al., 1991; Rowe, 2012). Several metrics have been used to examine how children learn language, with some researchers examining data-providing aspects of child-directed speech, and some researchers examining social-pragmatic aspects of child-directed speech (Hoff & Naigles, 2002; Rowe, 2008). Data-providing aspects of child-directed speech refer to those aspects from which children can extract meaningful information from the lexical and structural frequencies of the speech itself (Hoff & Naigles, 2002). Such aspects include features like total number of word tokens, total number of utterances, total number of word types, mean length of utterance, and use of sophisticated vocabulary. This type of information provided by child-directed speech allows children to learn language through a “data-crunching process” (Hoff & Naigles, 2002). On the other hand, social-pragmatic, or interactional, aspects of child-directed speech refer to those aspects from which children can make inferences about their conversation partner’s “communicative intent” (Hoff & Naigles, 2002). Such aspects include joint attention, mutual engagement, and responsivity. Previous research has supported the role of both data-providing (Hart & Risley, 1995; Huttenlocher et al., 1991; Rowe, 2008, 2012; Weisleder & Fernald, 2013; Weizman & Snow, 2001) and social-pragmatic aspects of parent-child interactions (Barnes, 2011; Hart & Risley, 1995; Madigan et al., 2019) in developing children’s language skills. Previous research has also demonstrated that parents who use more data-providing aspects of child-directed speech also tend to use more social-pragmatic ones (Jessee et al., 2016; Wade et al., 2018). However, data-providing aspects of child-directed speech may play a larger role in children’s language skills than social-pragmatic ones, especially as children age (Hoff & Naigles, 2002).

Indeed, previous work has repeatedly supported the relation between data-providing aspects of child-directed speech and children’s language skills. For example, in a seminal study on children’s language environments, Hart and Risley (1995) discovered that, by age four, children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds had heard 30 million fewer words than their peers from higher socioeconomic backgrounds. This difference in the amount of language exposure was not only associated with children’s vocabulary but also with the rate of their vocabulary growth. Several other studies have corroborated the relation between data-providing aspects of child-directed speech and children’s language skills, such that increases in data-providing aspects of child-directed speech are associated with increases in children’s language skills (Hurtado et al., 2008; Huttenlocher et al., 1991; Huttenlocher et al., 2002; Huttenlocher et al., 2010; Rowe, 2012; Weisleder & Fernald, 2013). These differences in child-directed speech may not only predict differences in children’s language skills, but they may also mediate the relationship between children’s language skills and demographic characteristics, like socioeconomic status (Hoff, 2003). It is possible, then, that differences in child-directed speech may mediate the relation between other family characteristics, like maternal depression, and children’s language skills.

Maternal Depression and Children’s Language Skills

Research has shown that children exposed to maternal depressive symptoms may have poorer developmental outcomes, including language outcomes, than children not exposed to such symptoms (Chapin & Altenhofen, 2010; Downey & Coyne, 1990; Murray et al., 1999; Quevedo et al., 2012; Slomian et al., 2019; Taiwo, 2020). Children exposed to more maternal depressive symptoms perform more poorly on measures of language skills and academic outcomes than children exposed to fewer depressive symptoms, with discrepancies emerging long before children enter formal schooling (Aoyagi et al., 2019; Breznitz & Sherman, 1997; Murray et al., 2010; Quevedo et al., 2012). For example, Pan et al. (2005) showed that children whose mothers had higher levels of maternal depression had slower vocabulary growth between 14 and 36 months of age than children whose mothers had lower levels. Breznitz and Sherman (1997) demonstrated that 30- to 42-month-old children whose mothers had higher levels of maternal depression spoke less than children whose mothers had lower levels. Aoyagi et al. (2019) demonstrated that children exposed to higher levels of maternal depression between 5 and 12 weeks postpartum had poorer expressive language skills at 18 months of age than children exposed to lower levels. Due to the central role of language skills for later child outcomes, the effect of maternal depressive symptoms on children’s language skills warrants specific attention. However, the exact pathways through which maternal depressive symptoms exert their effects on children’s language skills remain unclear.

One possible pathway is child-directed speech. It is possible that the symptoms of depression—dysphoria, anhedonia, appetite changes, sleep disturbances, psychomotor agitation/retardation, fatigue, feelings of worthlessness, difficulty concentrating, and thoughts of death—may interfere with the data-providing aspects of child-directed speech that are so important for children’s language skills. Researchers examining other aspects of the mother-child interactions, including social-pragmatic ones, have concluded that maternal depression does indeed undermine aspects of mother-child interactions linked to favorable children’s outcomes (Bernard et al., 2018; Goodman & Gotlib, 1999; Justice et al., 2019; Lovejoy et al., 2000). For example, a large body of literature, including recent meta-analyses (Bernard et al., 2018), on the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and social-pragmatic aspects of mother-child interactions suggests that, compared to mothers with lower levels of depression, mothers with higher levels of depression are more withdrawn, less sensitive, less engaged, less warm, and less responsive towards their children (Cummings & Davies, 1994; Gelfand & Teti, 1990; Lovejoy et al., 2000; Sohr-Preston & Scaramella, 2006; Tronick & Reck, 2009). Given this literature, it is plausible that maternal depressive symptoms could undermine data-providing aspects of child-directed speech as well.

Rationale and Scope of this Review

Taken together, maternal depressive symptoms are highly prevalent and are strong predictors of child outcomes, including children’s language skills. However, the pathways that underlie the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills are unclear. Child-directed speech might be one pathway through which maternal depressive symptoms influences children’s language skills. As a first step to filling this gap in the literature, this systematic review will examine the association between maternal depressive symptoms and data-providing aspects of child-directed speech. To our knowledge, it will be the first review paper to do so.

We will focus on two data-providing aspects of child-directed speech that have been shown to most strongly predict children’s language skills: amount of child-directed speech and complexity of child-directed speech (Hurtado et al., 2008; Huttenlocher et al., 1991; Huttenlocher et al., 2002; Huttenlocher et al., 2010; Pace et al., 2017; Rowe, 2012; Weisleder & Fernald, 2013). We specifically ask whether increases in maternal depressive symptoms are associated with 1) reductions in amount of child-directed speech and 2) reductions in the complexity of child-directed speech. Additionally, we examine whether demographic, contextual, and study characteristics act as potential moderators of the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and child-directed speech. We focus on studies that included children under the age of 5, due to demonstrated discrepancies in children’s language skills before children enter formal schooling (Fernald et al., 2013) and due to the high prevalence of maternal depressive symptoms early in children’s lives (Gavin et al., 2005). Moreover, the amount of time that children spend with their caregivers is usually highest early in childhood, prior to entering formal schooling, thus familial factors may play a larger role in children’s development earlier in children’s lives (Goodman et al., 2011). Finally, there may be sensitive periods early in children’s lives, before the age of 5, for various aspects of language acquisition (Friedmann & Rusou, 2015). And language exposure prior to age 5 may be especially beneficial for certain language skills (Newport, 2006; Newport et al., 2001). Findings from this review could help illuminate very specific aspects of mother-child interactions that should be analyzed as mediators of the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills. This could subsequently lead to future research questions and to the identification of targeted areas of the mother-child relationship that are appropriate for intervention.

Methods

Data Sources

Following PRISMA guidelines (see PRISMA checklist in Table 1), three databases were searched for articles related to the present study: CINAHL, PsycInfo, and PubMed. Searches were developed by a university librarian and the primary author, using an iterative process of gathering and evaluating terms. Three concepts were used to guide the search terms for each database: 1) mothers, 2) depression, and 3) language. Comprehensive strategies, including both index and keyword methods, were devised for these three concepts. Searches were finalized in March 2020. To maximize sensitivity, no pre-established database filters were used. Additionally, several search terms were expanded to include any terms that may be related to these three concepts. The full CINAHL search strategy, as detailed in Figure 1, was adapted for use with the other electronic databases. Complete search strategies for the other databases are available upon request.

Study Selection

Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) the study was empirical, 2) peer-reviewed, 3) in the English language, 4) included typically-developing children five years of age and/or younger, 5) included spontaneous maternal speech samples, and 6) included an analysis on the association between maternal depressive symptoms and child-directed speech. Studies were excluded if maternal depressive symptoms were too broadly operationalized (e.g. maternal distress), if the study focused on aspects of the parent-child interaction beyond features of child-directed speech (e.g. touch, affect, gaze), if the analyzed speech sample was not spontaneous, and/ or if the study included atypically-developing children.

The database searches produced 3,882 unique articles. The first author and an undergraduate research assistant each independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of 500 articles from the initial search to establish inclusion/ exclusion reliability. This was a binary decision (include or exclude). Out of these 500 articles, the two reviewers attained 94% agreement; Kappa was .70, indicating substantial agreement. For titles/ abstracts on which there was disagreement, full-text articles were retrieved, reviewed, and discussed until a consensus was reached. The first author subsequently screened the remaining 3,382 articles. The screening process is described below, and a flow chart for the process is presented in Figure 2.

During the first iteration of the screening process, a conservative approach was taken to include any articles related to the association between maternal depressive symptoms and any aspects of child-directed speech and/or children’s language skills. After this iteration, 546 articles were retained. The second iteration of the screening process involved examining the methods sections of these 546 articles and excluding any articles that did not include analyses specifically related to maternal child-directed speech. For example, several studies focused on the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills but did not include a variable related to maternal child-directed speech (e.g., Aoyagi et al., 2019; Tse et al., 2010). This resulted in 64 articles, and these 64 articles included analyses related to several features of child-directed speech, including the quantity of speech, the complexity of speech, speech acts, speech content, and speech acoustics. We chose to focus on the data-providing aspects of child-directed speech—specifically the amount and complexity of child-directed speech—rather than the social-pragmatic ones, both because research on child-directed speech highlights the contribution of data-providing aspects for child-directed speech over and above the social-pragmatic ones (Hoff & Naigles, 2002) and because there are existing reviews on relations between maternal depressive symptoms and the social-pragmatic ones (Bernard et al., 2018; Lovejoy et al., 2000). Thus, the third and final iteration of the screening process involved excluding articles that did not include analyses related to the aforementioned features of maternal child-directed speech (Figure 2). This final iteration resulted in ten articles for the current review.

Results

This search produced ten studies (Table 2) that represent the state of the current literature regarding maternal depressive symptoms and the data-providing aspects of maternal child-directed speech, specifically the amount and complexity of maternal child-directed speech. Studies varied widely in measures of maternal depressive symptoms, measures of child-directed speech, context, sample size, child age, and relevant sociodemographic information. Below, we discuss: 1) relations between maternal depressive symptoms and amount of maternal child-directed speech, 2) relations between maternal depressive symptoms and complexity of maternal child-directed speech, and 3) moderators that potentially influenced findings.

Amount of Child-Directed Speech

Six studies included analyses related to the amount of maternal child-directed speech (Table 2). Metrics used to measure the amount of maternal child-directed speech included total utterances, total word tokens, and sum of vocalizations. Four out of these six studies demonstrated a statistically significant negative association between maternal depressive symptoms and amount of maternal child-directed speech. Two studies, by the same authors, used the metric “sum of vocalization” to demonstrate that mothers who were depressed tended to speak less to their children than did mothers who were not depressed (Breznitz & Sherman, 1987, 1997). In these studies, “sum of vocalization” referred to the total time the mother spent vocalizing to the child. In one of these studies, the researchers found a 118 second difference in total time spent vocalizing during a five-minute mother-child interaction (Breznitz & Sherman, 1997). That is, mothers who were depressed spent up to two minutes less, per five minutes, vocalizing with their infants.

In other studies, researchers measured maternal child-directed speech with a total count of utterances or words. Jessee et al. (2016) reported a statistically significant negative association between depressive symptoms and total maternal utterances, suggesting that total utterances decreased as depressive symptoms increased. Rowe et al. (2005) used the metric “word tokens,” referring to the number of words produced, to demonstrate that mothers with more depressive symptoms spoke less to their children than mothers with fewer depressive symptoms. In their study, every time a mother’s depression score doubled, the number of words she produced decreased by 53 tokens. In contrast, Bettes (1988) and Fink et al. (2019) did not find a statistically significantly different amount of utterances between mothers who were depressed and mothers who were not.

Taken together, four out of six of these studies reported statistically significant results, suggesting that increases in maternal depressive symptoms are associated with decreases in amount of child-directed speech. Amount of child-directed speech, therefore, may be one factor that influences the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills.

Complexity of Child-Directed Speech

Six studies from this search examined the relation between maternal depression and the complexity of maternal child-directed speech (Table 2). Metrics used to measure the complexity of maternal child-directed speech included mean temporal duration of utterance, mean structural length of utterance, and word types.

When measuring the temporal duration of utterances, Bettes (1988) reported that the mean duration of the utterances of mothers who were depressed was a third of a second longer than those of mothers who were not, when responding to infant vocalizations, a difference that was statistically significant. Mothers who were depressed and mothers who were not did not, however, differ in mean duration of utterance when not responding to their infants’ vocalizations. In other words, for those utterances that were not responses to infant vocalizations but part of the continuous speech stream, mothers who were depressed and mothers who were not did not differ in mean duration of utterance. Reissland et al. (2003) also examined mean duration of utterances; they found that the mean duration of utterances did not significantly differ between mothers who were depressed and mothers who were not.

When measuring the structural complexity of the utterance, Gueron-Sela et al. (2018) reported a statistically significant negative correlation between depressive symptoms when children were 24 months old and mean length of utterance when children were both 24 months and 36 months old. However, this finding did not hold after controlling for covariates, including family income and maternal education. Additionally, neither Hwa-Froelich et al. (2008) nor Murray et al. (1993) found statistically significant differences in the mean length of utterances between mothers who had more depressive symptoms and mothers who had fewer depressive symptoms.

In addition to mean duration of utterance, Reissland et al. (2003) examined mean length of utterance. They did not find a main effect of depression status on mean length of utterance, but they did find a significant effect of the interaction between maternal depression (depressed vs. nondepressed), child age (average of 5–6 months old vs. average of 9–10 months old), and context of utterance (textual vs. extra-textual). In their study, mothers read a picture book to their infants. Maternal child-directed speech was divided into textual utterances (utterances that mothers read from the book) and extra-textual utterances (utterances about the book that did not involve reading the book’s text). Mothers who were not depressed used longer textual utterances with older children than with younger children, indicating that they read more of the book with older children; however, mothers who were depressed did not differ in the amount of the book read based on child age.

Rowe et al. (2005) used a different proxy of language complexity—word types— which is a measure of the number of different words a mother uses. They did not find a significant effect of maternal depression on word types.

Taken together, in contrast from the studies on the amount of child-directed speech, the literature surrounding the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and complexity of child-directed speech suggests that maternal depressive symptoms likely do not affect language complexity; maternal depressive symptoms may, however, affect language complexity as a function of certain demographic or contextual characteristics.

Potential Moderators and Methodological Considerations

Despite fairly convergent evidence that maternal depressive symptoms may affect the amount of child-directed speech but likely does not affect the complexity of child-directed speech, studies varied in their methodology, and findings should be interpreted with caution. Studies varied in the operationalization of both maternal depressive symptoms and child-directed speech, sample size, relevant demographic characteristics, and relevant contextual characteristics.

First, studies varied in the instrument used to measure maternal depressive symptoms (Table 2). Although there were no conspicuous patterns suggesting that a certain measure or type of measure increased the likelihood of producing statistically significant results, measures varied in several important ways. Measures varied in the components of depression on which they focused. This is noteworthy because it is possible that certain components of depression may differentially relate to different aspects of parenting, including speech. For example, in their review, Lovejoy et al. (2000) explain that different symptoms of depression may influence different aspects of parenting; they explain that, among several depressive symptoms, rumination may be most related to responsivity, and irritability may be most related to negative affect. Further, measures vary in their assessment of symptoms commonly associated with pregnancy and/or having a new baby (e.g. change in appetite, change in sleep, cognitive impairment, anxiety) (Beck & Gable, 2001). Among the studies in this review, Murray et al. (1993) and Reissland et al. (2003) were the only studies that included a motherhood-specific measure (the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale). And although there was no conspicuous pattern of moderation associated with using this measure—Reissland et al. (2003) produced statistically significant findings, but Murray et al. (1993) did not—the symptoms assessed should be considered when interpreting findings and extending them to practical applications.

Furthermore, considering whether researchers used a categorical variable or a continuous variable for maternal depression is important. Some studies characterized maternal depression categorically (depressed vs. not depressed), either by using a diagnostic interview or by using a cut-off score, (Bettes, 1988; Breznitz & Sherman, 1987, 1997; Hwa-Froelich et al., 2008; Murray et al., 1993; Reissland et al., 2003), while other studies characterized maternal depression continuously (Fink et al., 2019; Gueron-Sela et al., 2018; Jessee et al., 2016; Rowe et al., 2005). Relatedly, some studies used self-report measures, while some studies used diagnostic interviews, a methodological consideration that has been shown to moderate relations between maternal depression and child outcomes (e.g., behavioral outcomes, socioemotional outcomes). In one review, studies that used diagnostic measures rather than symptom rating scales produced larger effect sizes (Goodman et al., 2011). Together, some studies used self-report categorical variables (depressed vs. not depressed, using a cut-off score), some studies used diagnostic categorical variables (met criteria for depression vs. did not meet criteria for depression), and some studies used self-report continuous variables. In this review, there was no conspicuous pattern of moderation related to how studies characterized maternal depression, but these factors are important to consider when interpreting and extending findings for several reasons. Creating categories for maternal depression may be effective when the goal is to screen for a likely clinical diagnosis; however, creating such categories might result in the loss of important data points when the goal is to examine the predictive power of depressive symptoms on outcomes like child-directed speech. Moreover, many mothers experience depressive symptoms without meeting full criteria for a clinical diagnosis (Gavin et al., 2005), so it is still important to consider the role of subclinical levels of depression. As there was no conspicuous pattern of moderation, future studies should examine whether there is a threshold past which maternal depressive symptoms influence child-directed speech.

Measures also differed on the time period about which the items ask. For example, both the BDI and the CES-D ask about depressive symptoms during the prior two weeks, whereas the SADS-L asks about depressive symptoms during the lifetime. These variations in the time period captured are important to consider, especially due to research suggesting that both the timing and chronicity of depressive symptoms may differentially impact children’s outcomes (Brennan et al., 2000). For example, Aoyagi et al. (2019) found a negative association between expressive language scores at 18 and 24 months of age and late-onset postpartum depression (elevated symptoms at 5–12 weeks postpartum) but not early-onset postpartum depression (elevated symptoms within 4 weeks postpartum). Pan et al. (2005) discovered that gaps in child word types between children exposed to high levels of maternal depression and children exposed to low levels of maternal depression widened as children aged. At 24 months of age, children exposed to more maternal depressive symptoms produced an average of 5 fewer word types per 10 minutes than children exposed to fewer maternal depressive symptoms. By 36 months of age, this difference had increased to 20 word types per 10 minutes. It is possible that the lack of statistical significance in the Fink et al. (2019) study, for example, was related to the timing of depressive symptoms; in their study, depressive symptoms were measured three months earlier than child-directed speech, whereas in most other studies depressive symptoms and child-directed speech were measured concurrently.

Moreover, chronic maternal depression—that is, elevated depressive symptoms at more than one time point—may also have an effect on children’s language outcomes (Brennan et al., 2000; Sohr-Preston & Scaramella, 2006). In their study, the NICHD Early Child Care Research Network (1999) discovered that children whose mothers were chronically depressed had lower expressive language scores than children whose mothers were sometimes or never depressed, even after accounting for maternal education.

Second, studies varied in the operationalization of amount and complexity of maternal child-directed speech. Researchers from the six studies related to amount of child-directed speech included the variable “how much maternal child-directed speech” in their analyses; however, how “how much” was measured varied across studies (Table 2). The two studies that used total utterances to represent the quantity of maternal speech were similar in that researchers used a count of utterances; however, they varied in terms of what researchers considered an utterance. In the Bettes (1988) study, utterances were instances of “vocalizations bounded by pauses of at least 300 milliseconds.” In the Jessee et al. (2016) study, utterances were defined as either complete sentences or sentence fragments, thus approaching utterances as proxies for turns taken in a dialogue. In the studies that used total number of words (Fink et al., 2019; Rowe et al., 2005), researchers also used a count, in this case a count of words mothers produced. However, in the two studies that used sum of vocalizations (Breznitz & Sherman, 1987, 1997), amount of child-directed speech was measured temporally, rather than as a count variable. Sum of vocalizations referred to total speaking time and was used as a measure of how much speech mothers contributed to the interaction. Such a measure could, however, be impacted by both the mean duration of an utterance and the mean length of utterance. It is possible, for example, that one mother might take longer to say a word than another mother or that one mother might use a longer word than another mother. Two mothers, therefore, could produce the same number of words, however in different amounts of time.

Researchers from the six studies related to the complexity of child-directed speech also varied in terms of how they measured complexity. In both the Gueron-Sela et al. (2018) study and the Hwa-Froelich et al. (2008) study, mean length of utterance was considered the average number of morphemes (the smallest units of meaning in a word) per utterance. In the Murray et al. (1993) study, utterances were considered sentences and phrases bounded by pauses. However, the authors did not specifically delineate the equation used to calculate mean length of utterance. Finally, in the Reissland et al. (2003) study, the only study that found statistically significant differences in mean length of utterance, speech was divided into textual speech and extra-textual speech that occurred during a book-reading task. The mean length of utterance for textual speech was considered to be the proportion of words read from the text. The mean length of utterance for extra-textual speech was measured by “counting the words the mother uttered per page of the picture-book.” In this study, therefore, mean length of utterance reflected how much of the book was read, whereas in the aforementioned studies mean length of utterance reflected the structural complexity of sentences, phrases, and turns taken in a conversation. Moreover, Rowe et al. (2005) measured lexical diversity rather than syntactical complexity, and Bettes (1988) and Reissland et al. (2003) measured mean duration of utterance in seconds. A metric like mean duration of utterance could be affected by the length of words a mother uses or by the rate of a mother’s speech. Such variations in the operationalization of maternal language complexity require caution while comparing results across studies.

Third, studies varied in sample sizes; sample sizes ranged from 23 mother-child dyads to 1,114 mother-child dyads. Such variations in sample size may influence the statistical power necessary to detect important findings and may influence the likelihood of finding falsely significant results. Again, there was no conspicuous pattern in terms of statistically significant findings as a function of sample size. However, having smaller sample sizes may have prevented some researchers in this search from including important covariates, like family socioeconomic status, which has previously been shown to influence both maternal depression (Belle, 1990; Ertel et al., 2011; Mora et al., 2009; Rich-Edwards et al., 2006) and child-directed speech (Fernald et al., 2013; Hart & Risley, 1995). Gueron-Sela et al. (2018), for example, were able to include a large sample size and discovered that an initial correlation between maternal depressive symptoms and mean length of utterance disappeared after controlling for sociodemographic characteristics, like family income and maternal education.

Fourth, studies varied in child age. Child age ranged from 2 to 42 months old across studies; this is noteworthy because child-directed speech changes as children age (Rowe & Snow, 2019). The lack of statistical significance in the Bettes (1988) and the Fink et al. (2019) studies, compared to the other studies that examined amount of child-directed speech, may have been related to child age. In their studies, children were younger and ranged from 3 to 7 months old, whereas in the other studies children were older and ranged from 14 to 42 months old. Mothers in general tend to use less speech when their children are younger, compared to when their children are older (Rowe et al., 2005), and the extent to which maternal child-directed speech affects children’s language skills may be differentially salient as a function of child age (Pan et al., 2005). Thus, perhaps at a time when mothers are using comparatively less speech, these differences in quantity based on depressive symptoms are not statistically detectable.

Fifth, studies varied in context characteristics related to utterances. Interactions ranged from 2 to 30 minutes. Some interactions involved no stimuli, whereas other interactions involved stuffed animals, picture books, and dolls. The two studies that addressed context characteristics in their analyses found an effect of context characteristics on the association between maternal depressive symptoms and child-directed speech. Bettes (1988) found that mothers who were depressed differed from mothers who were not depressed in the mean duration of utterances that were responses to infant vocalization but not utterances that were part of the continuous speech stream. Reissland et al. (2003) found significant differences in the mean length of textual but not extra-textual utterances. Moreover, after examining differences between mothers who were depressed and mothers who were not, regarding the sum of vocalization during lunchtime activities, Breznitz and Sherman (1987) found that mothers who were depressed increased their rate of speech, and mothers who were not depressed decreased their rate of speech when in a more stressful situation (waiting for the doctor), compared to a less stressful situation (lunchtime). In this way, it appears that the context of utterances—whether an utterance-related context (e.g., response vs. non-response, textual vs. extra-textual) or an activity-related context (waiting for the doctor vs. lunchtime)—may interact with depression to influence the strength of findings. This interaction is important to consider due to evidence that amount and complexity of child-directed speech vary as a function of context. For example, Hoff-Ginsberg (1991) reported that the highest average number of utterances per minute may occur during book-reading, as compared to eating, dressing, and playing. And Weizman and Snow (2001) discovered that, in their sample, the average number of sophisticated word tokens was highest during mealtimes, as compared with other contexts of the day.

Finally, socioeconomic status (SES) should be considered when examining the association between maternal depressive symptoms and child-directed speech. Overall, women from low-SES backgrounds demonstrate higher rates of depressive symptoms than women from high-SES backgrounds (Ertel et al., 2011; Mora et al., 2009; Vericker et al., 2010), and children from low-SES backgrounds demonstrate poorer language skills than children from high-SES backgrounds (Fernald et al., 2013; Pace et al., 2017). Some studies in this review included only low-SES families; some studies included a mixed-SES sample; and some studies included only mid- to high-SES families. There was no conspicuous pattern in this review regarding statistically significant findings as a function of each sample’s SES, but it should be noted that only Gueron-Sela et al. (2018) and Rowe et al. (2005) included SES in their analyses. This is noteworthy because studies that have combined maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills suggest that affluence may even act as a buffer against the negative consequences of maternal depressive symptoms on children’s outcomes (Kurstjens & Wolke, 2001; Petterson & Albers, 2001). Thus, it is important to disentangle how much variation in child-directed speech is accounted for by the actual symptoms of depression and how much variation in child-directed speech is accounted for by more distal family characteristics that may covary with maternal depressive symptoms. Justice et al. (2019) reported a developmental pathway whereby financial hardship may increase parental distress, which adversely impacts parent-child interactions and contributes to reductions in children’s language skills. These complex relations may have been reflected in the Gueron-Sela et al. (2018), in which controlling for SES removed the significant effect of maternal depressive symptoms on child-directed speech.

Discussion and Future Directions

Although maternal depressive symptoms are prevalent and have strong effects on child outcomes, the pathways that underlie the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills remain unclear. The goal of this systematic review was to examine child-directed speech as a potential influencing factor in the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills. And although this review did not examine whether child-directed speech acts as a mediator in the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills, consolidating the literature on the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and child-directed speech was a necessary first step for future work wishing to explore the mechanistic role of child-directed speech. This review highlighted several important results as well as several gaps in the literature and corresponding opportunities for future studies.

Potential Pathways through which Maternal Depressive Symptoms Influence Children’s Language Skills

We specifically focused on data-providing aspects of child-directed speech that have been shown to most strongly predict children’s language skills. First, this review suggests that maternal depressive symptoms are likely more strongly related to differences in amount of child-directed speech than they are to complexity of child-directed speech. In other words, the amount of child-directed speech might be more sensitive than the complexity of child-directed speech to fluctuations in depressive symptoms. It is possible that the disengagement, withdrawal, and lethargy associated with depression might make talking feel more effortful than it would for speakers without such symptoms. Research on patients with depression has shown that they require more time to complete and are less likely to engage with tasks that demand effort; this has been demonstrated in cognitive tasks (Cohen et al., 2001; Grahek et al., 2019; Hammar, 2003; Thomas et al., 1999), physical activity (Padin et al., 2019; Roshanaei-Moghaddam et al., 2009; Schuch et al., 2017), and social activity (Holtfreter et al., 2017; Schelde, 1998). Although Slomian et al. (2019) did not explicitly comment on depression’s association with engaging in effortful tasks, they noted that mothers who are depressed, compared to mothers who are not depressed, are less likely to engage in activities that require more effort (e.g., tell stories, play games) and are more likely to engage in activities that require less effort (e.g., watch television) with their children. This desire to expend less effort may also interact with the attentional difficulties commonly associated with depressive symptoms (Cohen et al., 2001); many individuals with depressive symptoms find it difficult to redirect their attention away from mood-congruent content, like negative information or ruminative thoughts (Lang, 2013; Teachman et al., 2012). Thus, it is possible that mothers with depressive symptoms talk less to their children as a result of wanting to both expend less effort and as a result of finding it difficult to combat the attentional demands of depression in order to engage with the present moment.

In contrast to the consensus on differences in amount of child-directed speech, the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and the complexity of child-directed speech revealed a more complicated picture. Specifically, maternal depressive symptoms were not related to structural complexity (mean length of utterances) or lexical diversity (use of different words) of child-directed speech (Gueron-Sela et al., 2018; Hwa-Froelich et al., 2008; Murray et al., 1993; Rowe et al., 2005). Following the proposed relation between maternal depressive symptoms and effort, one could argue that maternal depressive symptoms should be associated with shorter utterances, which may require less effort and thus be one way to preserve energy. However, metrics used in the existing studies did not appear sensitive to maternal depressive symptoms. In two of the studies reviewed (Gueron-Sela et al., 2018; Hwa-Froelich et al., 2008), mean length of utterance was measured by the number of morphemes per utterance. It is possible that, especially at an age when caregivers use comparatively shorter utterances with their children and there is overall less variability in speech, differences in utterance length are less pronounced between those with more symptoms and those with fewer symptoms. In another study reviewed, complexity was represented by the diversity of words (Rowe et al., 2005). In this case, utterances with the same number of total words could still differ in total word types. If maternal depressive symptoms influence speech through desire to expend less effort, it follows that word types wouldn’t necessarily be affected. Moreover, once engaged in activity, it follows that attentional difficulties would affect the amount of speech but not necessarily the complexity of speech.

Maternal depressive symptoms do not appear to influence structural complexity or lexical diversity, but they could still influence the mean duration of utterances. Although using longer words and more complex sentences could influence the mean duration of utterances, the rate of speech could too. This is noteworthy because psychomotor retardation is one of the nine criterion used for a diagnosis of depression, and it affects speech among other motoric processes (Buyukdura et al., 2011). The findings from the Bettes (1988) study—that the utterances of mothers who were depressed were temporally longer than the utterances of mothers who were not depressed—may reflect psychomotor retardation. The Reissland et al. (2003) study did not find differences in mean duration of utterances. However, some of their findings were in the expected direction, with mothers who were depressed having a higher mean duration of utterance than mothers who were not. It should be noted that psychomotor retardation may be unique to severe cases of depression (Buyukdura et al., 2011). Neither of these studies necessarily included mothers with clinical diagnoses of depression, thus future studies should explore whether psychomotor retardation plays a role in differences in child-directed speech between mothers with varying levels of depressive symptoms.

The finding that complexity of child-directed speech might be a more stable feature of speech than amount of child-directed speech is partially supported by work focusing on other predictors of child-directed speech. For example, Ellwood-Lowe et al. (2020) examined the role of financial scarcity, a family characteristic thought to fluctuate throughout the month, in child-directed speech. They demonstrated that parents who reflected on recent financial scarcity, as compared to parents who reflected on other types of scarcity (e.g., not enough time), used fewer word tokens, but not word types, with their children than parents not reminded of financial scarcity. They also found that parents talked less at the end of the month, when financial scarcity is more salient, than at other times during the month. It is possible that fluctuations in depressive symptoms and fluctuations in other family characteristics, like scarcity, similarly predict the amount of child-directed speech but not the complexity of child-directed speech. Why mothers vary in the complexity of their child-directed speech remains an open question. It is possible that the complexity of child-directed speech is more sensitive to more stable, less fluctuating characteristics, like maternal education (Huttenlocher et al., 2007; Rowe, 2012). In some situations, it may also be the case that maternal depressive symptoms covaries with and is actually a reflection of these other, more stable family characteristics. This was seen in the Gueron-Sela et al. (2018) study, in which there was an initial correlation between maternal depressive symptoms and mean length of utterance, but the significance of this correlation disappeared when controlling for family income and maternal education.

Second, this review highlighted a feature of child-directed speech that has not been emphasized in prior work—adjustment of child-directed speech over time. Reissland et al. (2003) found that, for textual utterances, mothers who were not depressed and had older infants used qualitatively different speech from mothers who were not depressed and had younger infants, whereas mothers who were depressed and had older infants and mothers who were depressed and had younger infants did not differ in their speech. This finding highlights the possibility that mothers who are depressed have more difficulty adjusting their speech as children age than do mothers who are not. When examining other aspects of child-directed speech, such as speech acts, previous longitudinal studies have similarly demonstrated that mothers who are depressed may adjust their speech differently from mothers who are not (Kochanska et al., 1989).

The question of adjustment is important because child-directed speech changes over time (Huttenlocher et al., 2007; Pan et al., 2005; Rowe et al., 2005), and different aspects of child-directed speech may be more or less influential for children’s language skills based on the child’s developmental stage (Landry et al., 2000; Rowe, 2012; Rowe & Snow, 2019). Indeed, several researchers have hypothesized that tailoring child-directed speech according to the child’s developmental stage is a crucial aspect of language development (Denby & Yurovsky, 2019; Rowe & Snow, 2019; Snow, 1972). In their review, Rowe and Snow (2019) suggest that infants benefit most from lexical repetition and short utterances, while toddlers benefit most from increasing lexical diversity and grammatical complexity. Other studies have corroborated these findings, suggesting that different aspects of child-directed speech may be most salient at different developmental periods (Landry et al., 2000; Pan et al., 2005). Focusing on child-directed speech at a single point in time, therefore, may reveal a limited view. This review points to the importance of considering the dynamic and fluctuating nature of both mother-child interactions and depressive symptoms when exploring child-directed speech as a potential factor linking maternal depressive symptoms and children’s language skills.

Taken together, findings from this review revealed that maternal depressive symptoms may influence the amount of child-directed speech, the duration of utterances, and the adjustment of child-directed speech across time but probably not via the structural complexity or lexical diversity of child-directed speech. If children in part learn language through a “data-crunching process,” of detecting lexical and syntactic frequencies (Hoff & Naigles, 2002; Saffran et al., 1996; Smith & Yu, 2008), then reductions in amount of child-directed speech and speaking more slowly would provide fewer opportunities for children to learn language and pick up on those frequencies. Moreover, although the complexity of child-directed speech may predict children’s language skills above and beyond the amount of child-directed speech (Rowe, 2012), amount and complexity of child-directed speech are strongly correlated (Hoff & Naigles, 2002). Further, it does not seem to be the case that complexity of child-directed speech compensates for amount of child-directed speech; in cases of reduced amount of child-directed speech, the speech is not necessarily of higher complexity (Cristia, 2020). Therefore, reductions in the amount of child-directed speech could still reduce the number of opportunities a child has to hear complex language.

Future Directions and Limitations

This review identified several gaps in the literature. In addition to accounting for the potential moderators and methodological considerations listed above, future studies would benefit from several other opportunities. First, to better understand the mechanisms that link maternal depressive symptoms to children’s language skills, future studies should include maternal depressive symptoms, child-directed speech, and children’s language skills in the same model. Mediation models should be used to test the hypothesis that the features of maternal child-directed speech identified in this systematic review act as mechanisms through which maternal depressive symptoms affect children’s language skills. Analyzing maternal child-directed speech as a mediator is an important next step in identifying targets for interventions designed to improve children’s language skills in children at risk for falling behind their peers.

Second, future studies should examine several aspects of child-directed speech together, rather than examining each aspect individually. Data-providing aspects of child-directed speech interact with social-pragmatic aspects of child-directed speech to influence children’s language skills (Hoff & Naigles, 2002; Rowe & Snow, 2019). Furthermore, recent research has highlighted a third dimension—conceptual aspects of child-directed speech—that may interact with both the data-providing aspects of child-directed speech and the social-pragmatic aspects of child-directed speech to predict children’s language skills (Rowe & Snow, 2019). The conceptual dimension refers to topics of conversation (e.g., abstract topics). Rowe and Snow (2019), in their review on qualitative features of child-directed speech, argue that these three dimensions must be considered simultaneously and that minimizing any one of these dimensions would minimize language learning. Together, it appears that children best develop language skills through a dynamic system of processing input into meaningful information, interpreting social cues, and engaging with conceptually challenging material. Previous research has already established that mothers with depressive symptoms have difficulty with social-pragmatic aspects of dyadic interactions. Thus, an important next step is to move beyond the use of an isolated approach to examine these different aspects of mother-child interactions by examining how data-providing aspects of child-directed speech fit into social-pragmatic difficulties and vice versa.

Third, future studies should incorporate a developmental approach that accounts for the dynamic ways in which mother-child interactions change across time. Not only should future studies examine the features of child-directed speech that are most developmentally relevant to the participating children but also examine the adjustment of child-directed speech across time. Relatedly, future studies should use a developmental approach to examine the timing and chronicity of maternal depressive symptoms, as maternal depressive symptoms may differentially affect children’s outcomes as a function of timing and chronicity.

Finally, the findings from this review highlight the importance of analyzing the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and maternal child-directed speech in a way that further parses the components of maternal depressive symptoms to illuminate why mothers who are depressed differ from mothers who are not depressed on certain aspects of child-directed speech. Future studies should utilize measures that enable not only the examination of different components of maternal depressive symptoms but also the examination of commonly comorbid symptoms (e.g., anxiety symptoms) (Brennan et al., 2008; Farr et al., 2014; Reck et al., 2008; Stuart et al., 1998). The expanded version of the Inventory of Depression and Anxiety Symptoms (IDAS-II; Watson et al., 2012), for example, includes several subscales, including lassitude. Using more specific subscales would move us closer to understanding the aspects of maternal depression that might drive variations in maternal behavior. Understanding the different components of depression is important for creating effective interventions as well. Several studies have demonstrated that treatment of maternal depression is not sufficient to improve certain aspects of mother-child interactions, including social-pragmatic ones, like responsivity (Forman et al., 2007; Goodman et al., 2015; Logsdon et al., 2009). Understanding the facets of depression that drive reductions in certain aspects of child-directed speech could help fine-tune the content of mother-child interventions.

Conclusion

In sum, it is well-established that mothers vary in several aspects of child-directed speech, but less is known about the cause of the variability itself. Several interventions aim to improve children’s language skills; however, mothers and their children do not interact in a vacuum. Understanding the broader interactional context in which mothers are embedded is crucial for developing effective interventions. Here, we discuss one aspect of that interactional context—maternal mental health. We contribute to the growing literature examining parental factors that predict variability in child-directed speech by systematically reviewing ten studies related to the relation between maternal depressive symptoms and data-providing aspects of child-directed speech. Amount of child-directed speech, duration of utterances, and adjustment of child-directed speech across time may be plausible mechanisms through which maternal depressive symptoms exert their effects on children’s language skills. These potential mechanisms require further study, as part of important next steps in conceptualizing prevention and intervention efforts aimed at optimizing children’s language outcomes.

  • Maternal depression is likely not associated with lexical diversity.

  • Maternal depression is likely not associated with structural complexity of speech.
