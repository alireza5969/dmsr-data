Abstract

Background:

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of death among adults. Over 35% of women worldwide report lifetime exposure to sexual violence. While psychosocial factors broadly have been linked to CVD risk, it is unclear if a history of sexual violence is associated with increased risk for CVD. This study employed quantitative meta-analysis to investigate the association between sexual violence and CVD risk.

Methods:

PubMed and PsycINFO databases were searched through March 1, 2021. Included articles had a measure of sexual violence and at least one cardiovascular outcome (i.e., clinical CVD, subclinical CVD, select CVD risk factors) in women and men aged 18 years or older. Data were expressed as odds ratios (OR) or hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) extracted from fully-adjusted models. OR and HR effects were pooled separately, given the inability to statistically harmonize these effects and differences in interpretation, using random effects meta-analysis. Heterogeneity of effects was tested using Cochran’s Q test.

Results:

Overall, 45 articles based on 830,579 adults (77.1% women) were included (113 individual effects expressed as OR and 9 individual effects expressed as HR). Results indicated that sexual violence was related to adult CVD risk (OR [95%CI] = 1.25 [1.11–1.40]; HR [95%CI] = 1.17 [1.05–1.31]). Results varied by outcome type and measurement, and timing of violence.

Conclusions:

Adults with a history of sexual violence demonstrate greater CVD risk relative to those without this history. The results highlight the importance of addressing sexual violence in CVD risk reduction efforts.

Introduction

1.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are one of the leading causes of global morbidity and mortality, with over 422 million cases and 17 million CVD deaths in 2015 alone [1]. Psychosocial factors, including childhood abuse and trauma in adulthood (e.g., accidents, natural disasters), are increasingly understood to be important to CVD risk [2–7]. Sexual violence is a prevalent trauma and is relevant to consider in relation to CVD risk.

Globally, over 35% of women report non-partner sexual assault or physical and/or sexual assault by an intimate partner, with more limited data on prevalence in men [8]. Sexual violence includes both contact violence (e.g., rape, sexual coercion, unwanted sexual contact [9]) and/or non-contact forms of violence (e.g., a person exposing themselves in front of a victim, verbal harassment [9]). Prior research has reported associations between sexual violence and poor mental and physical health, including depression, suicidality, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), substance abuse, chronic pain, and gastrointestinal disorders [8–15].

A more limited literature has considered violence in relation to CVD risk. Prior reviews and meta-analyses have reported that childhood maltreatment and adversity are broadly related to obesity, inflammation, diabetes, and clinical CVD in adulthood [4,6,16–18]. Additionally, several recent reviews have reported mixed results for the association of violence/abuse in adulthood with CVD risk factors or clinical CVD [4,15, 19–21]. However, these reviews were focused on composite measures of abuse/violence, rather than sexual violence specifically, with two exceptions [17,19]. In a meta-analysis of four studies, Huang et al [17] found that childhood sexual abuse history was associated with 39% greater odds of type II diabetes mellitus in adulthood (OR [95% confidence interval] = 1.39 [1.28–1.52]). In a systematic review of nine studies, Peer et al [19] reported mixed results for the relationship between adulthood sexual abuse and select CVD risk outcomes (e.g., adiposity, hypertension, diabetes) in adult women. Given the prevalence of sexual violence and its intimate nature, there is a need to measure its impact on a broader range of adult cardiovascular health outcomes.

The primary aim of this investigation was to meta-analyze the literature on the relationship between sexual violence and cardiovascular health in adults. We consider sexual violence across both childhood and adulthood and across contexts (including sexual harassment, military sexual trauma, and sexual intimate partner violence) in relation to clinical CVD, subclinical CVD, and select CVD risk factors (e.g., blood pressure, diabetes/insulin resistance, lipids, metabolic syndrome), henceforth termed “CVD risk” for the purposes of this review. The outcomes identified for this review are established CVD risk factors or outcomes that have robust relationships with cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. High blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes mellitus are independent risk factors for CVD, including coronary disease and stroke [22], while indices of subclinical CVD (e.g., carotid intima media thickness [IMT] and carotid plaque) are well-established to be associated with later CVD events [23].

We hypothesized that persons with a history of sexual violence, compared to persons without this history, would have increased CVD risk. Secondary aims were to examine potential moderators of this relationship, including characteristics of participants (e.g., sex), sexual violence (e.g., type, timing, measurement), analyses (e.g., adjustment for sociodemographics; psychological factors; other CVD risk factors, such as smoking or diet) and CVD risk outcomes (e.g., type; self-report vs. direct measurement/medical record).

Materials and methods

2.

Guidelines and pre-registration

2.1.

This review was performed in accordance with the Meta-analyses of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines [24] (Supplemental Table 1) and the 24-step guide for study conduct [25]. The protocol was pre-registered with PROSPERO (CRD42021225204).

Search strategy

2.2.

PubMed and PsycINFO databases were searched for all English articles through March 1, 2021; additional hand searches were performed on references lists of included studies and related reviews and meta-analyses. Both MeSH terms and free-text were searched to return as many articles as possible (see Supplement for search strategy). Three trained reviewers (KPJ, VM, NS) screened titles and abstracts of all returned articles. Abstracts unrelated to sexual violence or cardiovascular outcomes were removed. All articles with original quantitative data for human subjects were included (see Fig. 1). Effort was made to contact authors for additional data and/or for clarification regarding results.

Eligibility criteria

2.3.

Eligible articles included a measure of past exposure to sexual violence and at least one indicator of clinical CVD, subclinical CVD, or CVD risk factors ever in women or men aged 18 years or older. Sexual violence included the following: sexual abuse or assault (in childhood and/or adulthood), sexual harassment, sexual intimate partner violence or dating violence, or military sexual trauma. Indicators of CVD risk included the following: (1) Clinical CVD: CVD, coronary heart disease (CHD), ischemic heart disease (IHD), myocardial infarction (MI), stroke or cerebrovascular disease, or combinations of the aforementioned endpoints; (2) Subclinical CVD: carotid IMT, coronary artery calcification, carotid atherosclerosis or plaque, pulse wave velocity, endothelial dysfunction, flow mediated dilation; and (3) CVD risk factors: blood pressure, hypertension, diabetes, homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), insulin, glucose, lipids, and metabolic syndrome (presence of three or more: elevated waist circumference, triglycerides, blood pressure, fasting glucose; reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol; [e.g., 26]). Cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional study designs were eligible.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) not written in English; (2) samples of children (<18 years old) or pregnant women; (3) studies that examined only composite measures of trauma (e.g., cumulative adversity, combinations of physical, sexual, and/or psychological abuse); or (4) studies that examined cardiovascular reactivity, composite CVD risk factor scores (other than metabolic syndrome, e.g., Framingham score), other cardiovascular problems (e.g., angina, chest pain, arrhythmias), or obesity as an individual outcome (as opposed to part of the classification of the metabolic syndrome), as it has been examined in several prior reviews/meta-analyses on sexual violence in childhood [e.g., 16,27–29] and adulthood [19]. Qualitative studies, case studies, editorials, reviews, meta-analyses, and unpublished theses were excluded.

Data extraction

2.4.

Article screening, data extraction, and quality assessment was divided among three authors (KPJ, VM, NS), who each screened an independent group of articles and performed data extraction and quality evaluation following predetermined forms. In addition, the first author (KPJ) reviewed all full-text articles, extracted data, and article quality assessments and provided consensus for disagreements.

The following information was extracted from each article: (1) sample characteristics (sample size, age, sex, race/ethnicity, country, psychological illness [e.g., depression, anxiety, PTSD]); (2) study design (cohort, cross-sectional, case-control); (3) analytic approach (cross-sectional vs. longitudinal analysis of the relationship between sexual violence and CVD risk); (4) sexual violence characteristics (i.e., type [sexual abuse/assault, military sexual trauma, sexual harassment, and sexual intimate partner violence or dating violence], timing [childhood; adulthood; or lifetime, which refers to studies that did not specify timing], assessment [prospective vs. retrospective; instrument; questionnaire, interview, or government record]; and severity); (5) cardiovascular outcome characteristics (i.e., type [clinical CVD, subclinical CVD, CVD risk factors], measurement [direct measurement of outcomes in a laboratory/clinic or extracted from medical records vs. self-report of a diagnosis made by a healthcare provider]); and (6) covariates included in analytic models (sociodemographics: e.g., age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, childhood/adulthood socioeconomic status; psychological: mental health symptoms or diagnoses; other CVD risk factors: e.g., body mass index (BMI), diet, physical activity, sleep, smoking, alcohol use, family history of CVD or diabetes, CVD-related medications, e.g., blood pressure-lowering, lipid-lowering, or diabetes medications), and additional types of abuse (e.g., physical or psychological abuse).

The reported effect size was extracted for each outcome from minimally-adjusted and fully-adjusted models, when both were available; for one article [30], fully-adjusted models were provided but effect sizes were extracted from unadjusted/descriptive data as fully-adjusted data could not be included in the meta-analysis without obtaining and grouping individual level data from the study. Extracted effects included regression weights, prevalence ratios, odds ratios (OR), and hazard ratios (HR). Regression weights and prevalence ratios were pooled with OR effects. We followed the guideline that prevalence ratios may be treated as OR if the disease prevalence is <10% [31]; of the two studies reporting prevalence ratios [32,33], application of this guideline resulted in exclusion of two out of five effects from one article [32] and exclusion of all effects from the other [33]. Consistent with other meta-analyses [6,34,35], HR effects were pooled separately from OR, given differences in interpretation and the inability to statistically harmonize these effects.

All relevant outcomes were extracted from all eligible articles. Different articles based on the same study were included in the meta-analysis only when the articles reported on different CVD risk outcomes. The majority of articles (56.8%) contributed multiple unique effects toward the meta-analysis for one or more of the following reasons: reporting results for several different CVD risk outcomes [30,32, 36–53]; providing sex-stratified results for sexual violence-CVD risk relationship(s) [41,42,45,46,52,54–56]; and/or reporting CVD risk results for two different measures of sexual violence (e.g., childhood sexual abuse and adulthood sexual violence) [48,50,57–59]. These effects were non-overlapping and, thus, were treated as independent effects in the meta-analysis. If multiple levels of severity of a single sexual violence predictor were tested with a CVD risk outcome, effects for the most severe form of violence were retained; this applied to four OR articles (k = 7 effects [37,54,56,60]) and four HR articles (k = 4 effects [61–64]). For articles that provided continuous values and clinical cutoffs for the same outcome [56,59,65], the effect for clinical cutoff was retained. When multiple measures of fasting plasma glucose, fasting plasma insulin, or insulin resistance were available from the same study [47,66], we included only HOMA-IR, as it provides a global indicator of insulin resistance [67].

Five OR effects from three articles were not included in the meta-analysis due to cell sizes being too small to compute OR [41,46,52]. Three articles used the same dataset to test the direct relationship between childhood sexual abuse and diabetes [68] as well as moderators [69] and mediators [70] of that relationship; the article that reported the direct relationship was retained for analysis. One article [71] was removed due to redundancies with outcomes reported in an earlier article based on the same study [38]; the earlier article was retained for analysis [38]. One article [68] with three eligible effects (MI, CHD, diabetes) was excluded, as analyses were replicated in a separate article [44] that used an overlapping but larger version of the same dataset (with MI/CHD combined into a single CVD outcome). Finally, seven OR effects across three articles [36,72,73] were not included as data were in a format that did not permit inclusion in the meta-analysis and attempts to obtain data from authors were unsuccessful.

Risk of bias and study quality assessment

2.5.

A modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) [74] was used to assess study quality (see Supplemental Tables 4 and 5 for criteria and quality ratings, respectively). Three authors (KPJ, VM, NS) each independently rated one-third of articles. Disagreement about study quality ratings was resolved by the first author (KPJ).

Statistical analysis

2.6.

Random effects meta-analysis of associations between history of sexual violence (versus no history of sexual violence) and adult CVD risk (combined across outcomes of clinical CVD, subclinical CVD, and CVD risk factors) was conducted with Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software (Version 3, Biostat Inc, Englewood, NJ). Results are presented in two ways: (a) a more conservative approach, which aggregated effects across outcomes within articles (OR studies: K = 38; HR studies: K = 8), and (b) a less conservative approach, which treated each sexual violence-CVD risk effect as independent (OR studies: k = 113; HR studies: k = 9). Hereafter, “K” denotes number of articles and “k” denotes effects (i.e., relation of sexual violence to one outcome).

Moderation/subgroup analyses were conducted to quantify differences in the effect of sexual violence on combined CVD risk by sample, sexual violence, and outcome characteristics, as well as by type of covariates included in fully-adjusted models. Heterogeneity was tested using Cochran’s Q test. If a significant Q-value coefficient emerged, subgroups were examined. The I2 statistic was used to quantify the amount of variation in results across articles due to heterogeneity. To ensure adequate power, moderation analyses treated each sexual violence-CVD risk effect as independent and moderators were tested only when each subgroup was composed of at least 20% of available effects. Moderators were chosen a priori.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we removed articles that studied samples with reported psychological illness (i.e., depression, anxiety, PTSD), as these disorders have been linked with increased CVD risk [75,76]. Second, we conducted a one-study-removed analysis on OR and HR effects, respectively, to assess the impact of each independent effect on the estimated overall OR and HR effect sizes. Third, we tested the association of sexual violence on CVD risk across unique studies (i.e., aggregating different effects drawn from the same study but published in separate articles). Fourth, we repeated subgroup analyses in women-only samples (using only OR effects to ensure adequate power). Fifth, given heterogeneity of outcomes included in the CVD risk factors category, we tested the association of sexual violence on CVD risk factor subtypes (e.g., blood pressure/hypertension, cholesterol/lipids), using OR effects to ensure sufficient power for each subtype. Finally, given evidence of a relationship between sexual violence and obesity from prior reports [17,19], we tested the effect of sexual violence on CVD risk for articles that did vs. did not adjust for obesity (i.e., BMI, waist circumference).

Finally, additional analyses were conducted to examine risk of publication bias, including examination of (1) forest plots of individual effects; (2) funnel plots [77], (3) Kendall’s tau [78], and (4) Egger’s regression [79].

Results

3.

Article selection

3.1.

The search produced 3,802 articles after excluding duplicates and 240 full-text articles were assessed in detail for eligibility (Fig. 1). Ultimately, 45 articles were included: 38 OR articles (113 individual effects) and 8 HR articles (9 individual effects); one article [80] was included in both sets of pooled effects because it investigated two separate outcomes relevant to OR and HR analyses. A description of OR and HR articles is provided in Supplemental Tables 2 and 3, respectively.

Characteristics of included articles

3.2.

Table 1 provides a summary of participant characteristics across all 45 articles and further stratified by articles reporting ORs versus HRs. Overall, the 45 articles reflect 830,579 participants (77.1% women, 73.0% white). Participants were largely drawn from midlife samples; however, 16 articles (35.6%) did not provide sufficient data to calculate a mean sample age. Two articles (4.4%) reported on samples with known anxiety, depression, and/or PTSD [42,66] while remaining articles involved otherwise healthy samples. Thirty articles (66.7%) included women-only samples or provided stratified results for women, eight articles (17.8%) provided stratified results for men, and 16 articles (35.6%) conducted analyses in a combined sample of women and men and did not provide sex-stratified results. Thirty-four articles (75.6%) involved samples from the United States, followed by three articles on samples from the United Kingdom; two articles on multi-country studies across the Americas, Europe, and Asia; and one article each on samples from South Africa, Mexico, Ireland, Brazil, New Zealand, and Canada, respectively. There were 33 unique studies in our analysis. 19 articles were derived from seven studies. The studies that contributed the most articles were the Nurses’ Health Study II (five articles; [61–64,81]) and the MsHeart study (four articles; [49,50,58,59]). Five additional studies contributed two articles each [44,48,53,65,68,80,82,83,110], and the remaining 26 studies contributed one article each.

The prevalence of sexual violence across all forms ranged from 0.5% to 48.9%, with higher prevalence in women compared to men for all types (see Supplemental Tables 2–3). Twenty-eight articles (62.2%) involved childhood sexual abuse [30,36,41,43,44,47–49,51,53–57,60, 62–66,68,80,82–86,110], followed by nine articles (20.0%) on lifetime sexual violence [32,37,40,42,45,46,52,87,88], five articles (11.1%) on adulthood sexual violence [48,50,57–59], four articles (8.9%) on sexual harassment [50,58,59,89], two articles (4.4%) on military sexual trauma [38,39], and two articles (4.4%) on sexual intimate partner violence [61,81]. The majority of articles (66.7%) measured sexual violence via retrospective self-report questionnaires, followed by interviews (31.1%) [32,37,42,44,45,53,55,57,60,68,82,89,110], and government records (2.2%) [51].

Considering outcomes, 34 articles (75.6%) studied CVD risk factors [30,32,36–41,43–47,51–54,56,57,59–61,63–66,80–83,85,89,110], followed by 18 articles (40.0%) with clinical CVD outcomes [32,38–46,52, 53,55,62,68,84,86,87] and seven articles (15.6%) with subclinical CVD outcomes [30,36,48–50,58,88]. Outcomes were largely assessed via self-report (25 articles; 55.6%) [32,37,38,40–45,52,53,55,57,60–64,68, 81,82,84–87], with a substantial minority of articles implementing direct measurement or medical record documentation (20 articles; 44.4%) [30,36,39,46–51,54,56,58,59,65,66,80,83,88,89,110]. Twenty-one articles (46.7%) involved cohort study designs and 24 articles (53.3%) were cross-sectional designs. However, 36 articles – including 11 articles from cohort studies [30,46,48,56,57,65,83,84,86,88,110] – conducted only cross-sectional analyses of the association between sexual violence and CVD risk. Most articles (86.7%) adjusted for sociodemographic factors in their analyses, while considerably fewer articles conducted adjustments for other CVD risk factors (53.3%) or psychological variables (40.0%). Study characteristics for OR articles and HR articles were similar to those reported across all 45 articles (see Table 1).

Quality assessment

3.2.

The majority of articles were rated as fair quality (29 articles; 64.4%), followed by 10 articles (22.2%) rated as good quality, and six articles (13.3%) rated as poor quality (Supplemental Table 4).

Association of sexual violence and cardiometabolic outcomes

3.3.

When considering OR effects drawn from minimally-adjusted models, sexual violence was associated with 18% or 25% greater odds of CVD risk, based on whether effects were treated as independent (k = 113; OR [95% CI] = 1.18 [1.12–1.24], Q-value = 688.47, p-value for heterogeneity <.001, I2 = 84%) or aggregated within articles (K = 38; OR [95% CI] = 1.25 [1.12–1.41], Q-value = 301.54, p-value for heterogeneity <.001, I2 = 88%); see Table 2. Considering effects drawn from fully-adjusted models, results were slightly reduced but still significant, such that sexual violence was related to 18% or 25% greater odds of CVD risk when effects were treated as independent or aggregated within articles, respectively (Table 2). Fig. 2 provides a forest plot of OR effects aggregated within articles (see also Supplemental Fig. 1 for a forest plot of independent OR effects).

When considering HR effects drawn from minimally-adjusted models, sexual violence was associated with 46% and 43% greater CVD risk, based on whether effects were treated as independent (k = 9; HR [95% CI] = 1.46 [1.18–1.82]; Q-value = 93.26, p-value for heterogeneity <.001, I2 = 91%) or aggregated within articles (K = 8; HR [95% CI] = 1.43 [1.16–1.76]; Q-value = 75.41, p-value for heterogeneity <.001, I2 = 91%); see Table 3. Considering effects from fully-adjusted models, results were reduced but still significant, such that sexual violence was related to 22% and 17% greater CVD risk, when effects were treated as independent or aggregated within articles, respectively (Table 3). Fig. 3 provides a forest plot of HR effects aggregated within articles (see also Supplemental Fig. 2 for a forest plot of independent HR effects).

Considering outcome types, results from OR effects drawn from fully-adjusted models indicated that sexual violence was related to 32% greater odds of clinical CVD (OR [95% CI] = 1.32 [1.22–1.42]). While sexual violence was also related to 13% greater odds of CVD risk factors (OR [95% CI] = 1.13 [1.05–1.21]), the 95% CI overlapped with subclinical CVD (Table 4), suggesting the effects for CVD risk factors and subclinical CVD were similar. Results from HR effects drawn from fully-adjusted models suggested increased risk of CVD risk factors (HR [95% CI] = 1.13 [1.01–1.25]), but not of clinical CVD (HR [95% CI] = 1.66 [0.82–3.38]); there were no HR effects for subclinical CVD (Table 5). However, in the case of HR effects, given the small numbers of effects and the overlap of 95% confidence intervals, HR results for outcome types should be interpreted with caution.

Given evidence of high heterogeneity across OR effects (I2 = 84–88%) and HR effects (I2 = 64–91%; see Tables 2,3), moderators were tested to evaluate differences in associations between sexual violence and CVD risk outcomes by prespecified subgroups.

Moderation analyses

3.4.

Moderation analyses for OR effects revealed significant between-group differences for four of the ten moderators tested. Larger effects for associations of sexual violence to cardiovascular health were observed for: (1) sexual violence occurring in childhood or over the life course (versus adulthood only), (2) clinical CVD outcomes (versus subclinical CVD or CVD risk factors), (3) self-reported health outcomes (versus direct measurement/medical record); and (4) cross-sectional (versus longitudinal) analyses; see Table 4. Moderation analyses for HR effects revealed significant between-group differences for three of the six moderators tested. Larger cumulative effects were observed for: (1) childhood sexual abuse (compared to sexual violence in adulthood), (2) effects that did not adjust for psychological factors (compared to those that did), and (3) effects that did not adjust for other types of abuse (e.g., physical abuse) compared to those that did; see Table 5.

However, given the large number of moderators tested, the high degree of heterogeneity within subgroups, the overlap of 95% confidence intervals across subgroups of several moderators (sexual violence timing and analytic approach for OR effects; adjustment for psychological factors and other abuse types for HR effects), and for HR studies, the small number of individual effects (k =9) and preponderance of data from a single study (Nurses’ Health Study II), caution is warranted in interpreting these results.

Sensitivity analyses

3.5.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we considered associations between sexual violence and CVD risk when removing the seven OR effects drawn from samples with diagnosed mental health (e.g., depression, anxiety; [42,66]) conditions, finding that the association between sexual violence and odds of CVD risk was largely unchanged (OR [95% CI] = 1.17 [1.11–1.23]). Second, we conducted a one-study-removed analysis on OR effects (k = 113) and HR effects (k = 9) drawn from fully-adjusted models, respectively; we did not find evidence that any one OR/HR effect had a notable impact on the estimated overall OR or HR effect sizes. Third, given that multiple articles used data from the same study, we tested the sexual violence-CVD risk relationship in fully-adjusted models aggregated across unique studies. The estimated effects of sexual violence on CVD risk for the 31 unique OR studies (OR [95% CI] = 1.26 [1.11–1.43]) and for the three unique HR studies (HR [95% CI] = 1.25 [1.01–1.54]) were similar to results obtained when aggregating across OR or HR articles, respectively. Fourth, we repeated subgroup analyses among women-only samples, and results (Supplemental Table 6) were consistent with subgroup analyses conducted among both women and men (Table 5). Fifth, we found that sexual violence was related to increased odds of high cholesterol/lipids (OR [95% CI] = 1.22 [1.08–1.38]), but no associations emerged with other individual CVD risk factor subtypes (Supplemental Table 7). Finally, while 36.8% and 62.5% of OR and HR articles, respectively, adjusted for a measure of BMI/obesity, moderation analyses suggested that overall estimated OR and HR effects of sexual violence on CVD risk did not differ significantly based on whether BMI/obesity was included as a covariate (data not shown).

Publication bias

3.6.

We considered publication bias in several ways. Funnel plots for individual effects suggested evidence of bias (Supplemental Figs. 3-4). When considering statistics, results for Kendall’s Tau did not suggest significant publication bias for OR effects (Tau = −.06, p = .38) or HR effects (Tau = .14, p = .60). Results for Egger’s regression did suggest bias for OR effects (Intercept = 1.28, SE = .30, p<.05), but not for HR effects (Intercept = 1.81, SE = 1.20, p = .17). Thus, Duval and Tweedie’s (2000) trim-and-fill procedure was used to provide a bias-corrected estimate of the cumulative effect size. Notably, statistically correcting for this bias does not eliminate associations; the estimated effect size was slightly reduced for OR effects (OR [95%CI] = 1.09 [1.04–1.15]) but was unchanged for HR effects (HR [95%CI] = 1.22 [1.05–1.42]).

Discussion

4.

The present study provides the first quantitative meta-analysis of the relationship of sexual violence across the life course with adult CVD risk. Based on 491,736 unique participants across 113 OR effects and 338,843 unique participants across 9 HR effects, results suggest that the overall estimated effect of sexual violence on CVD risk is 1.18 for OR studies and 1.22 for HR studies. The overall estimate for OR effects was reduced to 1.09, but there was no change in the overall estimate for HR effects, after correcting for publication bias. Critically, nearly 90% of articles adjusted for sociodemographic, psychological, and/or other CVD risk factors, some of which are putative mediators of the association between sexual violence and CVD risk outcomes (e.g., smoking, obesity). Thus, these estimates may be conservative. Further, results suggested notable heterogeneity across effects, a reflection of the diverse samples, sexual violence measures, and cardiovascular outcomes considered here. Indeed, findings of links between sexual violence and adult CVD risk are robust when viewed in light of these multiple conceptual and analytic considerations.

This meta-analysis examined whether the strength of the association between sexual violence and CVD risk varied across subgroups of sexual violence, outcome, and participant characteristics. First, for OR effects and HR effects, there was a larger cumulative effect of childhood sexual abuse, compared to adulthood sexual violence, on CVD risk; for OR effects, there was also a larger cumulative effect for lifetime versus adulthood sexual violence, which can include experiences of childhood sexual abuse. Thus, early experiences of sexual abuse may have particularly potent impacts on health, often decades after the abuse has ended. Second, results from OR effects suggested a larger cumulative effect of sexual violence on clinical CVD outcomes; however, the effect of sexual violence on CVD risk factors was also significant, which is broadly consistent with previous reports [6,20]. While sexual violence was not related to subclinical CVD, this may be due to a smaller number of effects [30,36,48–50,58,88] principally drawn from four cohorts of midlife women. Third, results from OR effects suggested a larger cumulative effect of sexual violence on self-reported health outcomes compared to direct measurement/medical record, consistent with a previous report [6]. Fourth, for HR effects, the cumulative effect of sexual violence on CVD risk outcomes was smaller when psychological covariates were considered (versus not considered) in analyses, although there was overlap in 95% CI among these subgroups. Given the high prevalence of mental health disorders among individuals with sexual violence histories [10,12,13], and the relationship between depression and post-traumatic stress disorder and CVD risk [75,76,90], it is understandable that adjustment for variables likely on the pathway between sexual violence and CVD may diminish the overall estimated effect. Notably, sex did not emerge as a moderator, and results from subgroup analyses conducted among women only mirrored results of subgroup analyses conducted among women and men. Ultimately, caution is warranted in interpreting these results, given the large number of moderators tested, the overlap in 95% confidence intervals across some subgroups, and the small number of HR effects primarily drawn from a single cohort.

The present meta-analysis is the most comprehensive review to date regarding the relationship between sexual violence and CVD risk. Two prior reviews were substantially more narrow in their scope: Huang et al [17] conducted a meta-analysis on four studies of childhood sexual abuse and diabetes while Peer et al [19] conducted a systematic review on nine studies of adulthood sexual violence and select clinical CVD and CVD risk factor outcomes. Our meta-analysis included 45 articles of sexual violence in childhood and adulthood and examined both clinical and subclinical CVD outcomes, in addition to a broader range of CVD risk factors (e.g., HOMA-IR, glucose, metabolic syndrome). We did find an association of sexual violence to CVD risk factors, but not specifically to diabetes-related outcomes, which stands in contrast to the findings of Huang et al [43]. Similar to Peer et al [74], we did not observe a relationship between adulthood sexual violence or military sexual trauma and CVD risk; however, the studies on these topics were few and thus findings must be observed with caution. This limited literature underscores the importance of future studies examining adult sexual violence or military sexual trauma in relation to CVD risk.

A number of mechanisms may link sexual violence and CVD risk. Prior work suggests that obesity may link childhood sexual abuse and adult CVD-related outcomes [91]. We tested whether the effect of sexual violence on CVD risk varied by adjustment for obesity and found that effect sizes were similar and statistically significant with or without this adjustment; future work should further investigate obesity as part of the pathway between sexual violence and CVD. Sexual violence survivors (compared to those without a history of trauma) demonstrate increased substance use, worse mental health, and worse sleep [32,59], which are themselves related to CVD risk [22,92]. Future work is also needed to study biological mechanisms, including dysregulation in inflammatory [21] and stress-related pathways, such as the sympathetic nervous system or hypothalamic pituitary adrenal axis, which have been related CVD risk [93,94].

Limitations

4.1.

This meta-analysis has limitations, many of which reflect the studies included in this analysis. Most of the articles relied on retrospective reports of sexual violence, which may result in under-reporting of exposure [95]. Sexual violence questions varied widely across studies. Some articles used validated multi-item measures of abuse, such as the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire [96] [e.g., 47, 49, 56, 66, 80], while others queried exposures using single-item measures [e.g., 46, 85]. Single-item measures largely indicate whether or not abuse has occurred, and are useful for that purpose, however, they do not capture other important aspects of this violence, such as timing, frequency, or duration. Relatively few articles considered non-contact forms of sexual violence [39,50,57,58,88,89] such as verbal sexual harassment or stalking, thus, it is important for future studies to also examine relationships between non-contact sexual violence and CVD risk. While analyses compared persons with vs. without a history of sexual violence, it is likely that some persons with a history of sexual violence were survivors of other forms of violence/abuse, as these exposures tend to co-occur [97], or, that persons without a history of sexual violence had a history of other types of abuse. The majority of studies collected data on CVD risk concurrently with participant reports of sexual violence, which precludes the ability to make clear claims about the causality or temporality of associations. While it is likely that childhood sexual abuse preceded the development of clinical CVD, many adverse indicators for CVD risk factors and subclinical CVD indices emerge quite early in life (e.g., adolescence; [98]), and therefore the temporality of associations is less clear. A slight majority of articles (56.8%) utilized self-reported cardiovascular outcomes, which may result in misclassification bias. Articles varied considerably with regard to adjustments for sociodemographic, psychological, and/or other CVD risk factors, with some studies reporting only unadjusted or age-adjusted results [e.g., 38, 46, 57, 71, 85] and others upwards of 15 covariates [48,49,55,58,61,62, 83,89]. Some articles had limited statistical power due to small sample sizes [47,66] or small number of participants reporting exposure to sexual violence [43,55,60,83]. The majority of articles were from white, female participants from the United States, thus limiting generalizability. The majority of OR articles included broad age ranges (e.g., 18 and older), which limits their ability to identify cardiovascular conditions (e.g., clinical CVD, hypertension, diabetes) unlikely to be present at younger ages [22]. Further, as clinical CVD events typically do not manifest in women until the sixth decade of life [99], studies that did not have adequate representation of older women are limited in their ability to consider clinical CVD in women. Additionally, three authors conducted abstract screening, full-text review, and data extraction on an independent set of articles, which may have led to biases in screening, data extraction, and study quality evaluation; however, the first author reviewed all full-text articles and extracted data and provided consensus for disagreements. Finally, it is possible that some studies were missed, despite using two major electronic databases for behavioral medicine research and performing hand searches.

Recommendations and Clinical Implications

4.2.

This study highlights the need for further rigorously-designed research assessing relationships between sexual violence and CVD risk. First, the field would benefit from using multidimensional scales of sexual violence, including contact and non-contact forms of violence, and considering timing, chronicity, severity, and perpetrators, features that are likely relevant to health [14,61]. Second, while data suggest that sexual intimate partner violence and workplace sexual harassment are prevalent experiences, especially among women in the United States (18% and 41%, respectively [100,101]), few studies have examined these types of violence in relation to CVD risk, particularly sexual harassment outside the work setting. Third, more research on relations between sexual violence and subclinical measures of CVD is needed. These indices can be useful for understanding CVD risk before clinical disease is present, particularly among midlife women [23]. Self-reported CVD is subject to biases, including contact with the health care system and health literacy [22,102]. Fourth, studies must include persons from diverse racial/ethnic and geographic backgrounds, as well as gender and sexual minorities, who disproportionately experience sexual violence [9, 103]. Fifth, when possible, studies should incorporate repeated measurements of both violence and CVD risk factors to understand the temporal nature of these relationships as well as to understand the relationship between sexual violence and progression of CVD risk over time, which would be informative for future intervention efforts. For example, identifying and intervening on traumatic stress, particularly in childhood, may reduce adverse psychological and health sequelae that increase risk for poorer lifelong health [104]. Treatment of PTSD, particularly using cognitive-behavioral approaches, may improve some cardiovascular outcomes in adults [105], while emerging data suggests that mindfulness-based interventions may improve mental and physical health, and reduce stress reactivity and some cardiovascular outcomes, among both child and adult survivors of trauma [106–109]. Finally, future work should investigate the pathways that link sexual violence across the lifespan with later CVD disease risk, which may inform prevention efforts. Clinically, this study underscores the high prevalence of sexual trauma history among midlife individuals and the importance of identifying sexual violence exposure as a CVD risk factor.

Conclusion

4.3.

This study provides the first quantitative meta-analysis of sexual violence and CVD risk in adults. Results suggest that exposure to sexual violence, particularly in childhood, is related to CVD risk in adults. These findings emphasize the need for health care providers to understand the sexual violence histories of their patients and for targeted interventions to improve the health of persons exposed to sexual violence and reduce CVD risk in midlife and aging adults.
