Post-immigration factors affecting retention in HIV care and viral suppression in Latin American and Caribbean immigrant populations in the United States: a systematic review

Abstract

Objectives:

To reduce disparities in HIV care outcomes among Latin American and Caribbean (LAC) immigrants living with HIV in the U.S., it is necessary to identify factors influencing HIV care in this population. A systematic review that provides a comprehensive understanding of factors influencing retention in HIV care and viral suppression among LAC immigrants living with HIV in the U.S. is lacking. This systematic review used the Immigrant Health Services Utilization theoretical framework to provide an understanding of these factors.

Design:

We searched for peer-reviewed publications in MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and ASSIA, from January 1996 to June 2020.

Results:

A total of 17 qualitative (n=10) and quantitative (n=7) studies were included in the review. The most commonly reported general and immigrant-specific factors appearing in studies were undocumented immigration status, HIV stigma, homophobia, cultural norms, values and beliefs, family and social support, language barriers, structure, complexity and quality of the U.S. healthcare delivery system, and patient-provider relationship.

Conclusion:

These findings highlight the importance of considering immigrant-specific factors along with general factors to improve the provision of HIV care services and HIV care outcomes among LAC immigrant populations.

Introduction

People born outside of the United States (U.S.) comprised an estimated 13.6% of the U.S. population in 2017 (the highest percentage since 1970), with 50.9% originating from Latin American and Caribbean (LAC) countries (Radford 2019). The immigrant population in the U.S. is disproportionately affected by human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (Prosser, Tang, and Hall 2012). An estimated 16.2% of people living with HIV (PLWH) in the U.S. self-identify as foreign-born, and a high proportion of these PLWH are diagnosed with HIV after entry into the U.S. (Prosser, Tang, and Hall 2012; Myers, Lin, and Skarbinski 2016; Valverde et al. 2017). The majority of immigrants diagnosed with HIV in the U.S. originate from the LAC regions of Central America and Mexico (41.0%), the Caribbean (21.5%), and South America (7.6%) (Prosser, Tang, and Hall 2012). Although immigrants are not considered a priority group in the U.S. National HIV/AIDS Strategy, many are part of high-priority ethnic/racial and sexual minority groups including black, Latinx, gay, bisexual, and men who have sex with men (MSM) (Office of National AIDS Policy (ONAP) 2020). Therefore, addressing HIV-related health disparities in risk of HIV exposure, HIV testing, and HIV care outcomes among immigrants is crucial to end the HIV epidemic in the U.S.

Immigrant populations face disproportionately more challenges accessing and utilizing healthcare services relative to other groups due to unique and complex individual and structural barriers, which may impact their engagement along the HIV care continuum (Sangaramoorthy and Guevara 2017; Luque et al. 2018; Rhodes et al. 2015; Ross, Cunningham, and Hanna 2018). Immigrant PLWH in the U.S. experience disparate HIV care outcomes when compared to U.S.-born PLWH; although data are limited and inconsistent (Ross, Cunningham, and Hanna 2018). Studies show that immigrants are more likely than U.S.-born to be diagnosed with HIV late (Sheehan et al. 2015; Sheehan, Trepka, et al. 2017; Demeke et al. 2018), and to enter HIV care with lower CD4 counts (Levison, Regan, et al. 2017; Ross et al. 2017). Three studies conducted in Florida found that immigrants, particularly LAC immigrants, were less likely to be retained in care (RIC) and be virally suppressed (VS) as compared to U.S.-born persons (Sheehan, Fennie, et al. 2017; Sheehan, Mauck, et al. 2017; Cyrus et al. 2017). Conversely, some studies have found that immigrants have similar rates of antiretroviral therapy (ART) prescription and higher rates of ART adherence, linkage to care, RIC and VS as compared to U.S. born individuals (Levison, Regan, et al. 2017; Myers, Lin, and Skarbinski 2016; Demeke et al. 2019; Demeke et al. 2018).

The documented disparities among immigrants in HIV incidence and disparities in HIV care outcomes among some immigrant groups such as LAC immigrants, and the individual and public health benefits of achieving RIC and sustained VS (reduced morbidity and mortality, and decreased risk of HIV transmission) (Tripathi et al. 2011; Mugavero et al. 2009; Attia et al. 2009), support the need to identify factors affecting RIC and VS in this population. Therefore, our objective was to descriptively synthesize available qualitative and quantitative research on factors affecting RIC and VS among LAC immigrants using the Yang and Hwang Immigrant Health Services Utilization theoretical framework (Yang and Hwang 2016), to provide a comprehensive multilevel understanding, which is lacking in current research on LAC immigrants living with HIV in the U.S.

Theoretical Framework

Yang and Hwang’s theoretical framework is an adaptation of the commonly used Andersen’s Behavioral Health Model of Health Care Utilization, that considers factors pertinent and specific to immigrants and provides a fuller understanding of immigrants’ heath service utilization (Yang and Hwang 2016). It includes four categories of determinants at general and immigrant-specific levels: (a) macro-sociostructural or contextual conditions, (b) predisposing factors, (c) resources or enabling factors, and (d) need for health care. Macro-sociostructural or contextual factors are described as sociostructural conditions at the larger societal level that are beyond individual control. General contextual factors include government policy, healthcare system, social, economic and political conditions, and immigrant-specific factors include those related to the context of emigration and reception, and experience with health service utilization in the homeland prior to immigration. Predisposing factors are individual-level factors that indicate an inclination towards health service utilization. These include demographic factors, socioeconomic status, health beliefs, and genetic factors as general; and immigration status, assimilation, and immigrant ethnic culture as immigrant-specific factors. Resources or enabling factors are described as means that enable people to access and/or receive health services. General types of resources include financial, social, and access to health care, and immigrant-specific resources include homeland-based financial and social resources and transnational access to healthcare. Lastly, need for healthcare or health conditions are motivations to access and/or receive health services, and include general and immigrant-specific needs such as self-reported or professionally evaluated health status.

Methods

Study Protocol Registration

This systematic review was conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al. 2009). The study protocol was drafted in accordance with the PRISMA Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines (Moher et al. 2015), and registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD42019131843). The Cochrane database was searched to ensure that there was no replication of the study.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

We performed a systematic, all-language literature search for all original, peer-reviewed studies that addressed RIC and/or VS in LAC immigrant PLWH in the U.S. We searched five online electronic databases from January 1st, 1996 to June 26th, 2020: MEDLINE (ProQuest), EMBASE (excluding MEDLINE), CINAHL (Ebsco), PsycINFO (ProQuest), and ASSIA (ProQuest). The year 1996 was selected because it was the year that ART was made more available to PLWH in the U.S. The first search was conducted on April 10th, 2019 and was later updated on June 26th, 2020. We supplemented this search by reviewing the bibliographies of selected papers.

Our search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords in four areas: (a) HIV/AIDS, (b) engagement in HIV care, (c) immigrants, and (d) the country/geographic region under study (LAC) (Appendix 1). Terms related to engagement in HIV care were included to broaden the search of studies in RIC and VS. Terms related to the country/geographic region were taken from the World Bank list of countries for LAC (The World Bank 2020).

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: a) original quantitative or qualitative research published in a peer-reviewed journal, b) the study assessed post-immigration factors associated with RIC and/or VS, c) conducted in the U.S. or U.S. territory, and d) participants were people diagnosed with HIV who are post-immigrant populations from LAC countries. Abstracts/articles from conferences, unpublished studies, case studies, review articles or non-research studies were excluded.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Screening was done in four stages. Two reviewers conducted the database searches for the identification of studies (MP and DRO). After removing duplicates, two reviewers (MP and JS; DRO and AA) independently screened remaining titles and abstracts for eligibility. References considered relevant were retrieved in full text. The two reviewers then conducted a full text screening of remaining references. At all stages of the screening, any discordance was resolved by consensus and a third reviewer (MJT). Inclusion and exclusion of studies was further discussed in depth with authors (MJT and DMS). The Mendeley reference management system was used to manage citations. The Covidence systematic review software was used to removed duplicates and manage article screening and selection.

Three reviewers (MP, JS, and DRO) independently extracted data from all included studies. Variables extracted were author(s), publication year, study design, source of study data, period in which the study was conducted, location(s) where study was conducted, study population, sample size, participants characteristics (race/ethnicity, country/region of origin, years living in the U.S., immigration status, age group, sex, and length of time since HIV diagnosis), outcome definition, and factors associated with RIC and VS. All factors from quantitative and qualitative studies were linked to the established categories of the Yang and Hwang’s theoretical framework. Those factors that didn’t fall into any of the established categories of the framework were classified as “other”.

Quality Assessment

The Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) Qualitative Checklist (responses: “yes”, “no” or “can’t tell”) and the Joanna Briggs Institute’s (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist (responses: “yes”, “no”, “unclear”, or “not applicable”) were used to assess the quality of qualitative and quantitative studies, respectively (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme 2020; The Joanna Briggs Institute 2017). Two reviewers (DRO and AA) assessed the quality of each included study independently, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Results

The search yielded 6,819 references (Figure 1). No additional records were found in the manual search of bibliographies of selected papers. After removing duplicates (n=295), 6,524 unique references remained, and after screening titles and abstracts of these 6,524 references, 88 references met criteria for full-text review. The full-text assessment excluded an additional 71 references, mainly due to irrelevancy, publication type (e.g., abstract, non-research study), population, and lack of separate analysis for LAC immigrants. Thus, 17 references (7 quantitative and 10 qualitative studies) were included in the final synthesis of the systematic review.

Study Characteristics and Participants

Except for four studies that did not specify when the study took place (Barrington et al. 2019; Barsky and Albertini 2006; Enriquez et al. 2010; Pivnick et al. 2010), included studies were conducted between 1994 and 2018. All studies were published in English. Of the quantitative studies, 3 were retrospective cohort (Cyrus et al. 2017; Sheehan, Fennie, et al. 2017; Sheehan, Mauck, et al. 2017), 3 were cross-sectional (Demeke et al. 2019; Dennis et al. 2013; Myers, Lin, and Skarbinski 2016), and 1 was a multicenter prospective cohort (Adedimeji et al. 2020) (Table 1). Data sources included national/state surveillance systems, medical records, patient interviews, and laboratory testing. Of the qualitative studies, 7 used in-depth and semi-structured interviews (Barrington et al. 2019; Barsky and Albertini 2006; Bowden et al. 2006; Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012; Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Maiorana, Zamudio-Haas, et al. 2020), 1 used narrative description (Enriquez et al. 2010), 1 used focus groups (Shedlin and Shulman 2004), and 1 used kinchart-sociogram, ethnographic interviews, focus groups and progress notes (Pivnick et al. 2010) (Table 2). Seven studies were conducted in the Southern region of the U.S., 4 studies in the Northeast, 1 study in the Midwest, and 1 study in the Southwest. The remaining 4 studies were conducted across several U.S. regions.

All studies were conducted among populations aged ≥13 years. Immigrant population groups sampled included undocumented people, men, women, men who have sex with men (MSM), and transgender people. The most represented LAC Spanish-speaking countries sampled were Mexico, El Salvador, Honduras, Guatemala, Colombia, Dominican Republic, and Cuba, and non-Spanish speaking countries included Haiti, Jamaica, Bahamas, Trinidad and Tobago, and Guyana. Study participants had been living in the U.S. for less than 1 year to over 30 years and had been diagnosed with HIV for less than a year to 19 years. Of all 17 studies, 4 studies reported on number of LAC immigrants not RIC (Cyrus et al. 2017; Demeke et al. 2019; Sheehan, Mauck, et al. 2017; Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017), and 6 studies reported on non-VS (Cyrus et al. 2017; Adedimeji et al. 2020; Demeke et al. 2019; Myers, Lin, and Skarbinski 2016; Sheehan, Mauck, et al. 2017; Dennis et al. 2013). Among these studies, the percentage of LAC immigrants who were not RIC varied from 12.7% (Demeke et al. 2019) to 43.0% (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017), and the percentage of those who were non-VS based on the most recent viral load test varied from 19.9% (Demeke et al. 2019) to 39.3% (Cyrus et al. 2017).

Quality of Studies

The quality assessment showed that all included qualitative and quantitative studies met most (75% or more) of their respective criteria for quality (Appendix 2).

Measurement of Retention in HIV Care and Viral Suppression

Measures of RIC and VS varied among studies. Most quantitative studies used evidence of clinical visits, laboratory tests or receipt of ART prescription at least 3 months apart in a 12-month period to measure RIC, and an undetectable or <200 copies/mL viral load in the most recent test or sustained VS in a 12-month period to measure VS (Adedimeji et al. 2020; Cyrus et al. 2017; Demeke et al. 2019; Myers, Lin, and Skarbinski 2016; Sheehan, Fennie, et al. 2017; Sheehan, Mauck, et al. 2017). Moreover, qualitative studies did not objectively measure and report RIC and instead focused on exploration of factors that affect HIV care utilization. Only 1 qualitative study defined non-RIC as inconsistent HIV clinic attendance in a 12-month period (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017). No qualitative studies focused on VS.

Factors Affecting Retention in HIV Care and Viral Suppression

Results of factors affecting RIC and VS among LAC immigrants are reported below (Tables 1 [quantitative studies], 2 [qualitative studies] and 3 [summary and classification of factors]).

Macrostructural/Contextual Factors

General

i.

Healthcare system:

Cultural and linguistic discordance between healthcare/service providers and patients (Barrington et al. 2019; Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Shedlin and Shulman 2004) and lack of trust and support (Barrington et al. 2019; Barsky and Albertini 2006; Enriquez et al. 2010; Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Taylor et al. 2014) from providers were frequently cited as a barrier to consistent HIV care. Further, a negative perception of healthcare providers (i.e., being insensitive and inpatient, not listening or explaining well, or not involving patients in decisions about their care) and bias and discrimination in treatment were barriers to HIV care among Latino immigrants (Shedlin and Shulman 2004); whereas attentive, compassionate, and professional treatment from providers was found to be a facilitator (Shedlin and Shulman 2004). Providers within the same ethnic group as the patient were reported to act as a facilitator to HIV care (Barsky and Albertini 2006). Barsky et al., 2006 indicated that, for Haitian immigrants, caseworkers from the same ethnic group facilitated access to HIV/AIDS care services as they were linguistically (spoke Haitian Creole) and culturally competent (Barsky and Albertini 2006). Additionally, service providers confusion regarding the use of two last names for check-in among Latino immigrants resulted in missed HIV care appointments (Bowden et al. 2006).

Moreover, Barrington et al., 2019 and Bowden et al., 2006 found that lack of understanding of the complex and disjointed U.S. healthcare system delivery and policies presented challenges to HIV care (e.g., identifying HIV care and support services) among Latino immigrants, which was exacerbated by low English language proficiency (Barrington et al. 2019; Bowden et al. 2006). Also, the existence of co-located specific and non-specific HIV/AIDS care services was an important facilitator to care among Haitian immigrants as this ensured that they were able to attend HIV care services without auto-disclosing their HIV status (Barsky and Albertini 2006).

Social Conditions:

Stigma against HIV (Barrington et al. 2019; Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Barsky and Albertini 2006; Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012; Pivnick et al. 2010; Taylor et al. 2014; Maiorana, Zamudio-Haas, et al. 2020) and homophobia (Barrington et al. 2019; Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Pivnick et al. 2010; Shedlin and Shulman 2004) were frequently reported barriers that affected HIV care due to HIV-related misconceptions and health beliefs (e.g., HIV is a fatal illness, HIV is the result of sexual impropriety, homosexuality or drug use, HIV can be transmitted through casual contact, AIDS inevitably leads to death, medication use is reserved only for severe illness) (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Barsky and Albertini 2006; Pivnick et al. 2010). HIV- and homosexuality-related stigma deterred HIV care through negative emotions (e.g., denial, shame, fear) (Shedlin and Shulman 2004), reactions such as social and family rejection, discrimination, physical and emotional threats and abuse, and unfair treatment (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Barrington et al. 2019; Barsky and Albertini 2006; Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012; Pivnick et al. 2010), and avoidance of HIV disclosure (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Barsky and Albertini 2006; Pivnick et al. 2010; Shedlin and Shulman 2004).

Other:

Another general contextual barrier to HIV care was lack of transportation. Haitian immigrants (specifically living in Broward County, Florida) cited difficulty getting to HIV care services because of lack of adequate transportation or no bus stops near HIV clinics (Barsky and Albertini 2006).

Immigrant-specific

i.

Health service utilization in the homeland:

Negative experiences with the healthcare system or providers in country of origin was cited as a barrier to HIV care. Shedlin & Shulman, 2004 found that limited or no access to health services in country of origin was a barrier to utilization of HIV care services in the U.S. among Latino immigrants (Shedlin and Shulman 2004). Also, Barrington et al., 2019 found that previous experience with a unified healthcare system and easy access to HIV services in country of origin among Latino immigrants, made navigating the U.S. healthcare system challenging, hindering HIV care (Barrington et al. 2019). Moreover, previous experience in country of origin with untrustworthy healthcare providers and confidentiality issues were barriers to HIV care in the U.S. for Haitians immigrants (Barsky and Albertini 2006).

Predisposing Factors

General

i.

Demographic factors:

Several demographic factors including foreign-born status, country/region of birth, sex at birth, rural/urban status, year of HIV diagnosis, age at HIV diagnosis, AIDS diagnosis, and HIV transmission mode affected RIC and VS among LAC immigrants living with HIV. In a study that included both foreign-born and U.S. born Latinos, being foreign-born was a facilitator to RIC and recent and sustained VS (Demeke et al. 2019). However, among studies that included both foreign-born and U.S. born Latinos, one study found that foreign-born status did not significantly affect RIC (Sheehan, Fennie, et al. 2017), and two studies found that foreign-born status did not significantly affect VS (Sheehan, Fennie, et al. 2017; Dennis et al. 2013). For Latino immigrants, having been born in Mexico, Guatemala and Honduras was a barrier to RIC and VS (Sheehan, Mauck, et al. 2017). However, one study found that having been born in Mexico was a facilitator for VS (Myers, Lin, and Skarbinski 2016). Moreover, being a Caribbean-born Black immigrant from a non-Spanish speaking country, particularly from the Bahamas, Haiti, and Trinidad and Tobago, was a barrier to RIC and VS (Cyrus et al. 2017). One study found that LAC region of birth did not significantly affect VS (Adedimeji et al. 2020).

Specifically, for Caribbean-born Black immigrants from non-Spanish speaking countries, having been diagnosed with HIV between 2000 to 2009, being a male (for Haitians and Jamaicans), having been infected with HIV through MSM behavior (for Haitians) or other/unknown mode of exposure (for Haitians, Bahamians and Jamaicans), and not having had an AIDS diagnosis by the year 2015 (for Haitians and Bahamians) were barriers to RIC; whereas, older age (for Haitians and Jamaicans), and living in a rural area (for Haitians) were facilitators to RIC (Cyrus et al. 2017). Additionally, for Caribbean-born Black immigrants, having been diagnosed with HIV between 2000 to 2009, being a male (for Haitians and Jamaicans), having been infected with HIV through other/unknown mode of exposure (for Haitians and Bahamians), and not having had an AIDS by the year 2015 were barriers to VS; whereas, older age (for Haitians and Jamaicans), having been infected with HIV through MSM behavior (for Haitians) and living in a rural area (for Haitians) were facilitators to VS (Cyrus et al. 2017). Neighborhood socioeconomic status did not significantly affect RIC or VS among Caribbean-born Black immigrants from non-Spanish speaking countries (Cyrus et al. 2017).

Socioeconomic factors:

Higher levels of education and socioeconomic status were facilitators to HIV care; whereas, lower levels were barriers (Barsky and Albertini 2006). A study among Haitian immigrants found that higher levels of education facilitated HIV care through better understanding of HIV and greater acceptance of HIV care (Barsky and Albertini 2006), and higher socioeconomic status provided better access to HIV care services (Barsky and Albertini 2006). Conversely, lower levels of education led to fear of the healthcare system and misinformation about HIV/AIDS, and lower socioeconomic status limited access to HIV care services due to lack of financial resources among Haitian immigrants (Barsky and Albertini 2006). Education levels of family members also affected HIV care among Haitian immigrants, as no education on HIV/AIDS limited the emotional and instrumental support provided by family to accessing HIV care services (Barsky and Albertini 2006). Also, low HIV literacy was a barrier to consistent HIV care among Latino immigrants (Maiorana, Zamudio-Haas, et al. 2020).

Other:

Levison et al. (2017) and Barrington et al., (2019) found that mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, and substance use were common barriers that interrupted RIC among Latino immigrants (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Barrington et al. 2019; Maiorana, Zamudio-Haas, et al. 2020). Mental health conditions manifested around dealing with HIV diagnosis and stigma, intimate partner violence, sexual violence during migration, social isolation, financial insecurity, and physical and verbal assaults, thereby disrupting people’s ability to attend HIV care (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Barrington et al. 2019). Substance use interrupted HIV care because the financial, physical, and emotional demands of substance use inhibited the importance of attending HIV care among Latinos immigrants (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017). Moreover, domestic violence made it difficult to attend HIV care among Caribbean immigrants from non-Spanish speaking countries, particularly in circumstances in which abusive partners monitored or prevented attendance of medical appointments; which was exacerbated by undocumented status (Pivnick et al. 2010).

Other general predisposing factors acting as barriers to HIV care included family and work responsibilities. Bowden et al., 2006 found that among Latino immigrants, work and family responsibilities were considered a higher priority over HIV care (Bowden et al. 2006).

Immigrant-specific

ii.

Immigration status:

Undocumented immigration status was a major barrier to HIV care (Bowden et al. 2006; Barsky and Albertini 2006; Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012; Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Pivnick et al. 2010; Shedlin and Shulman 2004; Maiorana, Zamudio-Haas, et al. 2020). Studies indicated that LAC immigrants were concerned about the risk of deportation for having HIV or accessing HIV services, which in turn discouraged them from receiving HIV care services (Bowden et al. 2006; Barsky and Albertini 2006; Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012; Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Pivnick et al. 2010; Shedlin and Shulman 2004; Maiorana, Zamudio-Haas, et al. 2020). Undocumented status also caused fear and confusion regarding losing access to care due to potential changes in healthcare and immigration policies in the U.S. among Latino immigrants, hindering HIV care (Barrington et al. 2019). Other barriers to HIV care related to undocumented status included unsteady or no employment (Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012; Barrington et al. 2019; Pivnick et al. 2010), work restrictions due to lack of employee benefits (e.g., paid leave) (Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012), and difficulties meeting paperwork requirements to receive services (Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012).

Immigrant ethnic culture:

Cultural norms, values and health beliefs were also salient factors that affected HIV care. Familism or a strong attachment to and protection of the family was a factor that both facilitated (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017) and hindered (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Shedlin and Shulman 2004; Barsky and Albertini 2006) HIV care attendance. While family pride, obligation to be well for others and close relationships served as motivations to attending HIV care (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017), protection of family and fear of family disapproval or conflict discourage HIV care attendance (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Shedlin and Shulman 2004; Barsky and Albertini 2006). Strict cultural conceptions of masculinity (machismo) led to avoidance of HIV status disclosure for fear of association with homosexuality, and in turn, affected HIV care attendance among Latino immigrants (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Shedlin and Shulman 2004). Other cultural values that acted as a barrier to HIV care among LAC immigrants included strong sense of pride and self-reliance (Barsky and Albertini 2006; Shedlin and Shulman 2004), high value for personal privacy (Barsky and Albertini 2006), and predilection for avoiding stress (Barsky and Albertini 2006).

Fatalistic beliefs (fatalism) such as dying of AIDS being a natural part of life or God’s plan were a barrier to HIV care among Haitian immigrants (Barsky and Albertini 2006). Also, cultural beliefs regarding the source (i.e., AIDS is caused by supernatural sources or by government officials) (Barsky and Albertini 2006) and cure (i.e. AIDS can be cured through traditional remedies and healers (curanderos), and faith in God)) (Barsky and Albertini 2006; Bowden et al. 2006) of HIV were barriers to HIV care. Conversely, religion provided a sense of hope, optimism, and resilience through faith in a higher power and encouraged commitment to attending HIV care among Latino immigrants (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017). Moreover, respect towards healthcare providers as authority figures (respeto), trusting relationships with healthcare providers (confianza), and satisfaction with clinical interactions when treated with courtesy (cortesía) also facilitated attendance of HIV care among Latino immigrants (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017).

Assimilation:

Acculturation or cultural assimilation to the U.S. culture facilitated HIV care (Shedlin and Shulman 2004; Barsky and Albertini 2006). For instance, one study among Haitian immigrants found that more acculturated people had more positive attitudes and less stigma towards receiving HIV care (Barsky and Albertini 2006). Also, more acculturated people were familiar with the U.S. healthcare system and available services which facilitated their HIV care (Barsky and Albertini 2006; Shedlin and Shulman 2004).

Conversely, no or limited English language proficiency was a major barrier to HIV care among Latino immigrants in several studies, as it limited effective and direct communication with health care providers (Barrington et al. 2019; Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Shedlin and Shulman 2004; Enriquez et al. 2010), compromised the quality of healthcare services provision (Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012; Enriquez et al. 2010), and made navigating HIV care services confusing and difficult (Bowden et al. 2006; Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012; Enriquez et al. 2010).

Resources/Enabling Factors

General

ii.

Social resources:

Family and social support, particularly emotional and instrumental, were found to facilitate attendance of HIV care appointments among Latino immigrants as a result of disclosure of HIV status (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Maiorana, Zamudio-Haas, et al. 2020). Emotional support came from motivation and encouragement to attend HIV care (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Maiorana, Zamudio-Haas, et al. 2020), and instrumental support from monetary and transportation assistance and appointment and treatment reminders from family and friends (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017). Conversely, lack of social support due to geographic separation, rejection from family and friends, or preference to maintain no or few social contacts left LAC immigrants vulnerable to social isolation hindering their HIV care (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Pivnick et al. 2010; Barrington et al. 2019; Barsky and Albertini 2006).

Financial Resources:

Lack of money or health insurance to attend clinic visits and economic and time loss (e.g., hourly wages lost from work, transportation cost) from attending clinic visits were also cited as barriers to HIV care among LAC immigrants (Bowden et al. 2006; Shedlin and Shulman 2004). Moreover, studies found that basic unmet financial, food, clothing, housing and transportation needs were barriers to HIV care as LAC immigrants living with HIV prioritize these needs and did not consider HIV care as urgent (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017; Bowden et al. 2006; Pivnick et al. 2010).

Access to health services:

Having access to HIV care services in the U.S. was a facilitator to HIV care for LAC immigrants. For geographically unstable Latino immigrants (those moving from place to place depending on employment availability), learning their HIV status and having successfully accessed HIV care services in the U.S. promoted geographic stability, which in turn facilitated HIV care (Bowden et al. 2006). Also, having no knowledge of available HIV care services and resources in the U.S. was a barrier to HIV care among Latino immigrants (Maiorana, Zamudio-Haas, et al. 2020; Shedlin and Shulman 2004). Moreover, limited access to trained language interpreters was a barrier to HIV care among Latino immigrants (Bowden et al. 2006; Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012; Enriquez et al. 2010). Overall, studies found that not having a language interpreter through the entire HIV care visit (Enriquez et al. 2010) or having an underqualified language interpreter hinders HIV care (Dang, Giordano, and Kim 2012). One study among Latino immigrants found that having access to Spanish-speaking interpreters was critical to adequate communication with non-Spanish speaking healthcare providers, although the fidelity of interpretation and interpreter’s discretion with personal information was a concern (Levison, Bogart, et al. 2017). Some studies also found that family members or partners served as language interpreters when this service was not available, which facilitated attendance of HIV care (Barrington et al. 2019; Enriquez et al. 2010).

Immigrant-specific

iii.

Transnational access to health care:

Geographic mobility (e.g., transnational and frequent long-stay travel and vacation) to country of origin was a barrier to consistent attendance of HIV care due to unwillingness to use HIV care services in country of origin (Taylor et al. 2014). Taylor et al., 2014 found that fear of HIV stigma/prejudice from people and physicians, mistrust of physicians, and low perceived quality of care led to HIV care interruptions while traveling to country of origin among Dominican immigrants (Taylor et al. 2014).

Need for Healthcare

No studies explored the influence of general or immigrant-specific health needs or conditions on RIC or VS among LAC immigrants living with HIV.

Discussion

In this systematic review we have identified a spectrum of general and immigrant-specific barrier and facilitating factors at the macrostructural/contextual, predisposing, and resources/enabling levels affecting RIC and VS among LAC immigrants. Most studies focused on factors affecting RIC (14/17), and fewer (7/17) focused on VS. The most common factors identified in studies were undocumented immigration status, HIV stigma, homophobia, cultural norms, values and beliefs, family and social support, limited English language proficiency, access to language interpreters, structure, complexity and quality of the U.S. healthcare delivery system and patient-provider relationship.

At the macrostructural/contextual level, navigating the structure and policies of the U.S. healthcare system was confusing and challenging and hindered RIC. This was mainly due to lack of understanding of the fragmented U.S. healthcare delivery system, differences between the country of origin and U.S. healthcare systems and policies, and lack of provision of healthcare resources and services in native language. Providing education on how to navigate HIV care services and how the U.S. healthcare system and policies work in the U.S. may be an effective strategy to overcome these challenges. Peer-navigators who are culturally and linguistically concordant, as found in previous studies (Maiorana, Sevelius, et al. 2020; Zamudio-Haas et al. 2019), may also facilitate navigation of the U.S. healthcare system. Furthermore, the co-location of specific and non-specific HIV/AIDS care services facilitated RIC as it allowed for LAC immigrants to attend HIV care without being recognized as having HIV. Having non-HIV/AIDS specific services in facilities offering HIV care would be particularly important to diminish the stigma associated with HIV and the fear of being seen receiving HIV care services. Some studies have found that HIV care co-located with non-HIV services (e.g., non-HIV specific primary care, drug abuse treatment) may improve RIC (Mizuno et al. 2019). Moreover, as found in other studies (Flickinger et al. 2013; Berger et al. 2016; Yehia, Mody, et al. 2015), the quality of patient-provider relationships and encounters was a major factor affecting RIC. Most studies reported that trust and support, cultural competency, good communication, and professional care and treatment were crucial to cultivate positive relationships with providers and to ultimately encourage RIC. For some LAC immigrants, patient-provider relationships and encounters were complicated by negative experiences with providers and confidentiality issues in country of origin. Therefore, enhancing providers’ skills in building trusting and supportive relationships and good communication with patients, and their understanding of the influence that previous experiences with providers in country of origin may have on building these relationships may improve RIC (Whetten et al. 2006; Flickinger et al. 2013). Predominantly, HIV stigma and homophobia were major barriers to HIV care due to many misconceptions related to HIV. While modifying embedded stigmatizing and homophobic beliefs may be considerably challenging, a more feasible alternative is providing psychological counseling services and education to help to equip LAC immigrants with coping skills to manage stigma and homophobia, and foster/build positive beliefs about maintaining consistent HIV care (Brown, Macintyre, and Trujillo 2003).

At the predisposing level, similar to other findings (Yehia, Stewart, et al. 2015; Rooks-Peck et al. 2018; Schafer et al. 2012), medical needs such as mental health conditions and substance use, and domestic violence hindered RIC as they compromised LAC immigrants’ ability to attend HIV care and diminished the importance of HIV care. Thus, ensuring the use of ancillary services among LAC immigrants, such as mental health and substance use treatment, may help to address multiple needs and diagnoses and improve RIC (Rooks-Peck et al. 2018). Further, similar to studies among other immigrant groups, undocumented status was a major barrier to RIC (Foley 2005). Undocumented status restricted the ability of LAC immigrants to consistently attend HIV care (e.g., fear and confusion related to risk of deportation and loss of access to HIV care). Although some of these consequences are difficult to address, reassuring people of their access to HIV care regardless of immigration status through programs like the Ryan White Program, and protections of personal information may ease these uncertainties. Moreover, this review identified myriad cultural norms (e.g., machismo) and values (e.g., familism, fatalism), and health (e.g., use of traditional remedies and healers) and religious beliefs that affected RIC among LAC immigrants. These findings are consistent with other studies showing that cultural and religious beliefs and practices influence HIV care outcomes (Burns et al. 2007; Szaflarski 2013) and are central to immigrants’ healthcare utilization (Lor et al. 2017; Omenka, Watson, and Hendrie 2020; Sanchez-Birkhead et al. 2011). Thus, it is critically important that healthcare providers be aware of how cultural and religious factors influence decisions to attend HIV care, that some patients may use traditional medicine alongside conventional healthcare for their HIV care, and how important it is to work collaboratively respecting cultural beliefs and practices to promote RIC. This can be achieved by increasing awareness and training healthcare providers (e.g., cultural competency training (Saha et al. 2013) to address retention issues in a culturally appropriate manner. Furthermore, as found in other studies of HIV services utilization (Kinsler et al. 2009; Rajabiun et al. 2008), acculturation to the host country facilitated RIC as patients had less stigma towards HIV care and were more acquainted with HIV care services in the U.S. Lastly, limited English proficiency was a salient barrier to RIC among LAC immigrants in this review; a finding that is consistent with previous studies (Foley 2005; Russ et al. 2012). Limited English proficiency is a deterrent to direct communication and trust between providers and patients and compromises the quality of care. Thus, providing peer navigators and language interpreters to patients who are less acculturated and have language and literacy issues may help to promote consistent engagement in HIV care.

At the resources/enabling level, family and social support was a major factor affecting RIC. For many LAC immigrants, the perceived and actual HIV stigma in their social networks discouraged disclosure of HIV status and made social relationships complex, which in turn prevented them from seeking and/or receiving emotional and instrumental support needed to consistently attend HIV care. In addition, geographic separation and preference to maintain no or few social contacts disrupted social connections and limited the support received. These findings are consistent with other research showing that family and social support play an important role in determining engagement in HIV care (George et al. 2009; Yehia, Stewart, et al. 2015), and reinforce the importance of interventions to facilitate HIV disclosure (e.g., counseling (Breger et al. 2017)) and social support (e.g., peer support (Chang et al. 2015)) for LAC immigrants. Moreover, lack of financial resources (e.g., health insurance, money) and unmet needs (e.g., housing, food) are barriers to RIC as these limit the ability to attend HIV care visits and become urgent priorities over HIV care. Additionally, having successfully accessed HIV services in the U.S. promoted HIV care as this served as a motivation to be geographically stable and attend HIV care. Thus, the resolution of financial and other unmet needs, and access to HIV services through case management programs is important to ease these issues and prioritize consistent HIV care (Katz et al. 2001; Ko et al. 2013). Furthermore, an important resource that facilitated RIC among non-English speaking LAC immigrants was language interpreters. Having access to well-qualified and trusted language interpreters through the entire HIV care appointment was crucial for receiving quality of care and having adequate communication with providers. However, mistrust of interpreters and concerns about confidentiality and fidelity of interpretation had negative consequences on consistent HIV care attendance. Thus, ensuring LAC immigrants’ access to well-qualified and trained language interpreters through in-person or remote services is imperative. Lastly, even when transnational access to HIV care in country of origin was available, LAC immigrants were unwilling to use HIV care when traveling to their home country due to mistrust, fear of stigma, and low perceived quality of care, which interrupted their HIV care. This finding is consistent with other studies (Tanser et al. 2015) and shows the importance of healthcare providers’ assistance in forming travel plans that reduce interruptions in HIV care and the potential for technological interventions (e.g., mobile phone applications) to deliver remote HIV care (Muessig et al. 2013).

Utilizing the Yang and Hwang’s theoretical framework to integrate the findings of this systematic review revealed the intersectionality of general and immigrant-specific factors influencing RIC and VS among LAC immigrants. The intersection of being an immigrant, especially undocumented immigrant, in the U.S. and living with a stigmatized disease causes myriad complex and unique experiences and circumstances (e.g., social isolation, fear of deportation) that affect LAC immigrants’ utilization and quality of healthcare and HIV care outcomes. It also clearly demonstrates how transnationalism shapes HIV care among some LAC immigrants in the U.S. Therefore, future research and public health programs and policies on HIV care among LAC immigrants should take into consideration the multiple intersecting and transnational factors that mutually influence HIV care outcomes in this under-research population that is disproportionately burdened with HIV.

There remains a need to research some potentially important factors in the Yang and Hwang’s theoretical framework that were rarely or not assessed in the studies included in this review, including general factors such as governmental policy, economic and political conditions, and immigrant-specific factors such as context of emigration and reception, transnational access to healthcare, homeland-based resources, and health needs and conditions. Stratified analyses (e.g., by gender, sexual orientation) among LAC immigrants should be conducted to determine which factors are associated with HIV care outcomes for specific sub-groups. This knowledge is important to fully understand factors influencing HIV care outcomes in LAC immigrants and sub-groups, determine which factors should be prioritized, and support the development and implementation of targeted interventions for this population. Moreover, it is a priority for future studies to consistently operationalize and measure general and immigrant-specific factors as well as some important demographic factors pertinent to immigrant populations such as years in the U.S., immigration status, country of origin, and language proficiency. More studies on VS and among LAC immigrants from non-Spanish speaking countries and those from groups such as youth and transgender people are needed.

Lastly, this systematic review confirmed the validity of the Yang and Hwang’s theoretical framework to study HIV care among LAC immigrants and provided knowledge about other factors that should be considered. Future studies on HIV care among immigrants should apply this theoretical framework to consider immigrant-specific factors and develop theory-driven hypotheses to fully understand how these factors are link to HIV care outcomes.

Limitations

Our systematic review has several limitations to consider. First, our review is mainly based on qualitative studies and due to the limited number of quantitative studies included and their heterogeneity in methods and outcome measures, we were unable to compare the strength of associations across studies. Second, a broad search strategy was used recognizing that few studies specifically focus on factors affecting RIC and VS among LAC immigrants. Much of the data extracted was from qualitative studies that did not specifically focus on retention (rather on utilization of HIV care) or included samples with some U.S. born people and migrants from U.S. territories. Third, some potentially relevant literature may have been missed as we did not search gray literature. However, since we searched across five databases and manually searched publications, it is unlikely that any major relevant literature was missed.

Conclusions

Given the growing LAC population in the U.S. and their disproportionate representation in the U.S. HIV epidemic, addressing HIV care needs and outcomes in this population needs to be prioritized. Our review has identified important factors influencing LAC immigrants’ HIV care that can be used to better tailor the provision of HIV care services to their needs. Our findings also underscore the need for multilevel and culturally appropriate programs and policies to address the multiple general and immigrant-specific factors affecting HIV care to promote and improve RIC and VS among LAC immigrants.
