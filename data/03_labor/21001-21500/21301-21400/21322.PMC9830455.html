Abstract

Hemivertebra is a congenital vertebral malformation caused by unilateral failure of formation during embryogenesis that may be associated with additional abnormalities. A systematic review was conducted to investigate genetic etiologies of non-isolated hemivertebra identified in the fetal, neonatal, and infant periods using PubMed, Cochrane database, Ovid Medline, and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception through May 2022 (PROSPERO ID CRD42021229576). The Human Phenotype Ontology database was accessed May 2022. Studies were deemed eligible for inclusion if they addressed non-isolated hemivertebra or genetic causes of non-isolated hemivertebra identified in the fetal, neonatal, or infant periods. Cases diagnosed clinically without molecular confirmation were included. Systematic review identified 23 cases of non-isolated hemivertebra with karyotypic abnormalities, 2 cases due to microdeletions, 59 cases attributed to single gene disorders, 18 syndromic cases without known genetic etiology, and 14 cases without a known syndromic association. The Human Phenotype Ontology search identified 49 genes associated with hemivertebra. Non-isolated hemivertebra is associated with a diverse spectrum of cytogenetic abnormalities and single gene disorders. Genetic syndromes were notably common. Frequently affected organ systems include musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, central nervous system, genitourinary, gastrointestinal, and facial dysmorphisms. When non-isolated hemivertebra is identified on prenatal ultrasound, the fetus must be assessed for associated anomalies and genetic counseling is recommended.

INTRODUCTION

1 |

Hemivertebra is a congenital vertebral malformation found either in isolation or associated with multiple other anomalies with an incidence of 0.3 in 1000 births.1,2 Hemivertebra develops due to unilateral failure of vertebral formation during embryogenesis, which may result in congenital scoliosis. In the case of lateral formation failure, half of a vertebral body, a single pedicle, and a hemilamina will be present.3,4 There does not appear to be a clear male or female predominance associated with the development of hemivertebra, though some studies have found a predominance in either males or females.1,3,5,6 The majority of patients with hemivertebra appear to have other associated anomalies.1,2,7,8

During embryogenesis, the vertebrae begin development during the sixth week of gestation via two lateral chondrification centers per segment. The chondrification centers fuse by seven to eight weeks, forming the primary ossification centers. It has been postulated that if there is a disruption in the process of somitogenesis during membranous vertebral body formation, chondrification and ossification will follow the anomalous membranous vertebral body scaffolding.4 This will result in an anomalous vertebral formation, such as a hemivertebra. Another hypothesis postulates that hemivertebra may arise from anomalous distribution of intersegmental arteries of the vertebral column.9

Hemivertebra can be detected prenatally with ultrasound, but are not always detected in utero. The congenital malformation may not be diagnosed until childhood after scoliosis has already developed. Prenatal detection of fetal hemivertebra most commonly occurs between 20 and 28 weeks’ gestation but can be diagnosed earlier.4,10 Chen et al reports prenatal sonographic visualization as early as 13 weeks.11 Sonographic findings include distortion in the shape of the spine.12 This is best assessed on sagittal or coronal views (Figures 1 and 2). 3D-ultrasound also assists in fetal diagnosis. The hemivertebra itself is observed as a triangular bony structure, smaller than the rest of the vertebrae.12 It will appear to be wedged between normal vertebral bodies.

This study was conducted to investigate the genetic etiologies of non-isolated hemivertebra identified in the fetal, neonatal, and infant periods. Secondary objectives were to describe associated prenatal and postnatal findings as well as associated syndromes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

2 |

Search strategy

2.1 |

This systematic review was conducted using PubMed, Cochrane database, Ovid Medline, and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception through May 2022, identifying relevant articles describing the genetics of hemivertebra (PROSPERO ID CRD42021229576). We used the following search terms: (hemivertebra or hemivertebrae) and ((fetus or fetal), OR (infant, newborn [MeSH] or neonate or newborn or newborns or neonates), OR (genetic phenomena [MeSH] or genetic variation [MeSH] or genetic structures [MeSH] or genes [MeSH] or gene or genes or genetic). The search was limited to the English language. See Appendix for full search strategies. We further manually searched the references of identified studies to capture articles that were not found in the initial search and were able to find additional relevant cases of fetal, neonatal, and infant hemivertebra in the literature. Infant was defined as age less than or equal to 12 months. The Cochrane database returned no results and ClinicalTrials.gov returned no publications during our search time period. (Figure 3).

The Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) database (http://compbio.charite.de/hpoweb/showterm?id=HP:0002937) was accessed on May 15, 2022 to download the gene association and syndrome association tables associated with HPO Term: HP:0002937 (Hemivertebrae, synonymous with Hemivertebra) as well as subclasses HP:0008467 (Thoracic hemivertebrae), HP:0025481 (Cervical hemivertebrae), and HP:0008439 (Lumbar hemivertebrae). Sacral hemivertebra was not an HPO term.

Study selection

2.2 |

Non-duplicate identified articles were independently reviewed by two authors (J.P. and E.R.), and relevant articles were selected by mutual agreement. A third author (H.A.K.) performed a tie-breaker if any disagreement was encountered in study selection. Studies were deemed eligible for inclusion in our review if they addressed the genetics of non-isolated hemivertebra, either directly or indirectly. Non-isolated hemivertebra refers to hemivertebra with malformations in other organ systems, including other skeletal malformations. If articles described non-isolated hemivertebra, with or without other vertebral abnormalities, they were included. We did exclude cases that discussed other vertebral anomalies if they did not describe hemivertebra. We excluded studies that described cases of hemivertebra outside of the fetal and infant period. Case series and genetic linkage studies with aggregate diagnoses associated with hemivertebra were excluded if details of the individual cases were not provided. Selected studies were further classified for review into the following groups: karyotype abnormalities, copy number variant changes, known syndromes associated with single gene disorders, known syndromes without known genes, and hemivertebra with no known syndrome or gene. Eligible studies ranged from years 1973 to 2022. Cases diagnosed clinically without a molecular confirmation were included.

For each article meeting inclusion criteria, the following data were extracted: age at diagnosis, ethnicity (if reported), hemivertebra level, prenatally and postnatally associated abnormalities, genetic evaluation performed and diagnosis, syndromic diagnosis, and outcomes. We extracted the diagnosis based on what the authors reported. Data extraction was independently completed by two authors J.P. and C.S., and any discrepancies were resolved by a third author, H.A.K. Data were tabulated using Microsoft Word and summarized using narrative review and descriptive statistics by J.P. and C.S., and any discrepancies were resolved by a third author H.A.K. Data were not reported uniformly in every case report or series. Any data points not reported in cases are explicitly stated in the tables as not reported (NR). There is an inherent risk of publication bias due to the majority of included studies being case reports and case series, in addition to reporting differences in older articles based upon available diagnostic testing technology.

RESULTS

3 |

Out of 453 records identified in our search, 256 records were screened. A total of 170 reports were sought for retrieval. Sixteen reports were identified via citation searching. In total, 100 studies were included in our review (Figure 3).

We identified 23 cases of non-isolated hemivertebra with karyotype abnormalities, 2 cases due to microdeletions, and 59 cases attributed to single gene disorders (Tables 1–3). All cases with genetic aberrations had other associated anomalies detected either by prenatal ultrasound or postnatal physical examination. We identified 18 additional cases of known syndromes without current known genes in association with non-isolated hemivertebra (Table S1). Our search also yielded 14 cases of non-isolated hemivertebra without a known syndromic association (Table S2). Additional case series detailing hemivertebra are described in Table S3. The HPO query identified 49 genes with a documented association with hemivertebra.

Twenty-three cases with karyotypic abnormalities were identified (Table 1).13–35 Eight cases (35%) had karyotypically diagnosed deletions (22q13 microdeletion, 7q terminal deletion, 6q25 terminal deletion, 17p terminal deletion, 4p deletion, 18q deletion, 4q deletion, 18p deletion), and one case (4%) had a marker chromosome (partial tetrasomy 4q).13–16,19,23,27,29,35 Notably, two of these cases had deletions in chromosome 18 and two additional cases had deletions in chromosome 4.23,27,29,35 Eight cases (35%) of trisomy were identified (trisomy 18q mosaic, trisomy 4 mosaic, partial trisomy 22, trisomy 8 mosaic, trisomy 9 mosaic, trisomy 7 mosaic, trisomy 7, trisomy 18 mosaic).18,20,21,25,28,32–34 Notably, two of the trisomies involved chromosome 18.18,34 Of these 23 cases, 10/23 (44%) were diagnosed prenatally, 9/23 (39%) were diagnosed at birth, and 4/23 (17%) were diagnosed before 1 year of age. All cases with reported prenatal ultrasound had fetal anomalies or other ultrasound findings detected. These included: facial abnormalities 5/15 (33%), fetal growth restriction 5/15 (33%), congenital heart defects 4/15 (27%), skeletallimb abnormalities 4/15 (27%), and central nervous system abnormalities 4/15 (27%). All cases had multiple associated anomalies detected postnatally or after postmortem exam, with the exception of four studies in which such an exam was not reported or autopsy declined (Table 1).13,15,16,23 Commonly diagnosed postnatal abnormalities included: facial 17/19 (90%), skeletal-limb 15/19 (79%), cardiac 9/19 (47%), central nervous system 8/19 (42%), gastrointestinal 7/19 (37%) and genitourinary 8/19 (42%). Outcome data associated with karyotype abnormalities were available for 13 patients. Seven patients (54%) had growth delay, five (38%) postnatal deaths between day of life 0 to 67, and only one patient (8%) was thriving at 4 months of age.

Our literature search identified two cases of microdeletions associated with non-isolated hemivertebrae (Table 2): one case of Williams-Beuren syndrome and the other with DiGeorge syndrome.36,37

We identified 59 cases of non-isolated hemivertebra attributed to single gene disorders with a known molecular etiology as confirmed in OMIM and/or HPO query (Table 3): Jarcho-Levin syndrome and other spondylocostal dysostoses (DLL3, MESP2, LFNG, HES7, TBX6, RIPPLY2),38–55 Craniofacial microsomia (SF3B2, MYT1),56–62 Robinow syndrome (ROR2, WNT5A, DVL1, DVL3),63–65 Klippel-Feil syndrome (GDF6),66,67 Alagille syndrome (JAG1, NOTCH2),68,69 caudal regression syndrome with peno-scrotal transposition (VANGL1),70,71 caudal duplication syndrome (AXIN1),72 congenital disorders of glycosylation (SLC35A3, ALG12),73,74 CHARGE syndrome (CHD7, SEMA3E),75 TARP syndrome (RBM10),76 chondrodysplasia punctata (EBP),77 ectodermal dysplasia, ectrodactyly and cleft lip/palate syndrome (TP63),78 unilateral abdominal wall hypoplasia (CHRM3),79 BRESEK/BRESHECK syndrome (MBTPS2),80 renal cysts and diabetes syndrome (HNF1β),81 congenital short bowel syndrome (CLMP),82 and EVEN-PLUS syndrome (HSPA9).83 These syndromes currently have a known molecular basis that was not always reported at the time of publication of the specified cases. Thirteen of the 59 cases (22%) were diagnosed prenatally, 30/59 cases (51%) at birth, and 16/59 cases (27%) postnatally. Prenatal ultrasound was reported in 23 cases, of which 22 (96%) had other associated findings detected including skeletal abnormalities, CNS abnormalities, renal anomalies and fetal growth restriction (FGR). All cases had additional postnatally detected anomalies, which included skeletal (54/59, 92%), facial (30/59, 51%), central nervous system (23/59, 39%) with 16 cases of neural tube defects (16/59, 27%); renal/genitourinary (18/59, 31%), gastrointestinal (17/59, 29%) and cardiac (16/59, 27%). Reported outcomes included: postnatal demise (13/48, 24%), psychomotor delay (8/48, 17%), growth delay (7/48, 15%), normal psychomotor development (10/48, 21%), and postnatal surgical repair of vertebral or other associated anomalies (9/48, 19%) (Table 3).

Jarcho-Levin syndrome and other spondylocostal dysostoses were the most commonly diagnosed single gene disorders (27/59, 46%). The majority of cases had multiple associated anomalies including facial dysmorphism, other skeletal anomalies such as fused vertebrae, spina bifida, renal anomalies, and anal atresia. Most cases were clinically diagnosed by easily recognizable features on either prenatal ultrasound or physical exam. Molecular genetic testing was not routinely pursued for this condition, though six causative genes have currently been identified.50,84–89 Craniofacial microsomia was the second most common diagnosed single gene disorder, associated with genetic variants in SF3B2. The craniofacial microsomia nomenclature currently includes the terms oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum, oculo-auriculovertebral dysplasia, hemifacial microsomia, facioauriculovertebral sequence, and Goldenhar syndrome. A recent systematic review confirms that hemivertebra are frequently found in craniofacial microsomia.90 Similar to Jarcho-Levin syndrome, molecular genetic testing was not routinely pursued for craniofacial microsomia in cases described in our review.

Molecular diagnosis was confirmed in six cases with single gene disorders. Candidate gene sequencing identified a causative variant in HES7 in a case of spondylocostal dysostosis.50 Exome sequencing in five patients revealed mutations in SLC35A3, ALG12, RBM10, HNF1β and HSPA9.73,74,76,81,83

Eighteen additional cases of known syndromes without current known genes were identified in association with non-isolated hemivertebra (Table S1). These included three cases of lumbocostovertebral syndrome, two cases of Aicardi syndrome, two cases of VACTERL/VATER-like association (vertebral anomalies, anal atresia, cardiac anomalies, trachea-esophageal fistula, renal anomalies, limb anomalies), two cases of diastematomyelia, two cases of a syndrome of aphalangy, hemivertebrae, and urogenital-intestinal dysgenesis, one case each of vertebral spinal dysplasia, anophthalmia-plus syndrome, Sakoda complex, Poland syndrome, omphalocele-exstrophy-imperforate anus-spinal defects (OEIS), omphalocele radial ray complex, and LACHT syndrome. Karyotype was performed in 13 cases and was reported as normal in all, with two cases that also reported normal chromosomal microarray,91 and one case of negative exome sequencing.92 When prenatal ultrasound findings were reported (n = 13), 12/13 (92%) had associated anomalies detected prenatally. Abnormalities included skeletal (6/13, 46%), central nervous system (3/13, 23%), gastrointestinal (3/13, 23%), genitourinary (3/13, 23%), cardiac (2/13, 15%), and facial (1/13, 8%). All 18 cases of hemivertebra described in Table S1 were confirmed postnatally, and all had additional anomalies detected postnatally.

Our search yielded 14 cases of non-isolated hemivertebra without a known syndromic association (Table S2). Karyotype was performed in six cases, all of which were normal. One case reported a duplication within 2p11.2 on chromosomal microarray.93 Hemivertebra was identified prenatally in 7/11 (64%) when prenatal ultrasound was reported. Seven (7/14, 50%) cases were identified postnatally. Additional postnatal abnormalities included: skeletal (10/14, 71%), cardiac (5/14, 36%), central nervous system (2/14, 14%), and abnormal facial clefting (2/14, 14%).

Additional studies describing cases of hemivertebra

3.1 |

Several studies of patients with hemivertebra further characterize associated malformations (Table S3).1,2,4,5,7,94–96 These series often described their results in aggregate and individual case data were not generally provided. In these series, the spectrum of abnormal cytogenetic results included trisomy 13, trisomy 16, trisomy 18, trisomy 22, 4p deletion syndrome, and 9q33.1q34.11 deletion.2,8 Monogenic disorders included spondylocostal dysostosis, caudal dysgenesis syndrome, Pfeiffer syndrome, Jarcho-Levin syndrome, and Goltz syndrome (also known as Gorlin syndrome).2,5,8

Human phenotype ontology search results

3.2 |

The HPO query identified 49 genes that had a documented association with hemivertebra. Twenty of these HPO genes were identified in our search (Tables 1, 3, and S3).22,38–65,68,75,77,80,93,97–99 The remaining 29 of 49 identified HPO genes associated with hemivertebra were not identified in our systematic review and are described in Table S4. Eight genes did not meet our age inclusion criteria and 12 were excluded as age was not specified in potential cases. The remaining 9 genes were not identified in our search, though described cases meet our inclusion criteria. These nine genes are highlighted in Table S4. Each syndrome reported in Table S4 is supported with a PMID reference as well as a Gene Reviews, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), or Orphanet source that demonstrates the association between the syndrome and hemivertebra. The HPO query also identified syndromes associated with hemivertebra that do not yet have known associated genes (Table S5). Each syndrome in Table S5 is also supported with an OMIM or Orphanet source. There were 14 genes associated with single gene disorder diagnoses in Table 3 based on OMIM that were not identified in the HPO query.

Frequency of hemivertebra

3.3 |

The frequency of hemivertebra in genetic syndromes varies. Ming et al reports only one case of hemivertebra among 108 (0.9%) patients with molecularly confirmed DiGeorge syndrome.100 In this series, the age of patients is reported as a range between DOL 1 and 36 years. Another study reports variable expressivity of hemivertebra in 11/74 (15%) of patients with Gorlin syndrome (nevoid basal cell carcinoma) between age 4 months to 87 years old.97 In a case series of 88 patients with Robinow syndrome between age 6 months to 39 years old, the frequency of hemivertebra is 22.7% in autosomal dominant and 97.5% in autosomal recessive Robinow syndrome.101

Other genetic causes of hemivertebra

3.4 |

The frequency of hemivertebra in nonsyndromic congenital scoliosis has been investigated as well. Wu and Takeda et al describe hemivertebra associated with a compound heterozygosity model: TBX6 loss-of-function variant in the setting of a risk haplotype (Table S3).102,103 Among 237 patients with nonsyndromic congenital scoliosis, 23 (9.7%) had TBX6 pathogenic variants, all of whom also had hemivertebra.102 Takeda et al builds on these findings, identifying the TBX6 null variant in the setting of the same risk haplotype in a different population of patients with nonsyndromic congenital scoliosis.103 In this study of 94 cases, 9 patients (9.6%) had the null variant but only 7 of these 9 (77.8%) had hemivertebra present. These results were not statistically significant regarding the presence of hemivertebra in patients with TBX6 pathogenic variants and risk haplotype versus those without the pathogenic variant.103 More recently, Liu et al has further characterized the genotype–phenotype relationship in TBX6-associated congenital scoliosis, demonstrating lower spine hemivertebra distribution in both humans and mice with the same compound inheritance model, and fewer overall vertebral and rib defects.104 Lastly, Chen et al describes a dose-dependent model where 50% of TBX6 expression is sufficient for normal spinal development.105 When TBX6 expression is less than 50% of normal, there is a dose-dependent phenotypic expression of vertebral malformations. These malformations range from mild, seen with compound heterozygote TBX6 variants where one allele represents the hypomorphic risk haplotype, to severe with spondylocostal dysostosis-like manifestations when both alleles are deleterious.105

Three additional interrelated studies further discuss associations found with multiple vertebral anomalies and neural tube defects. In 1975, Wynne-Davies et al conducted a survey of 337 patients with congenital vertebral anomalies and concluded that the presence of multiple vertebral anomalies is related to the presence of anencephaly and spina bifida.106 In patients with multiple vertebral anomalies there is a 3%–7% recurrence risk for neural tube defects for future siblings, whereas solitary hemivertebra appear sporadic with no recurrence risk of neural tube defects.106 Lendon and Wynne-Davies et al published two follow-up articles in 1981 with additional evidence that multiple vertebral anomalies and spina bifida were likely related, and that isolated hemivertebra and/or solitary vertebral body anomalies were a distinct entity from anomalies resulting in multiple defects.107,108

Finally, congenital niacin deficiency and VACTERL/VATER association may result in hemivertebra development. Shi et al describes variants in HAAO and KYNU, genes involved in the de novo synthesis of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), that resulted in defects in embryogenesis similar to VACTERL.109 NAD is produced via several pathways, one of which requires dietary niacin to complete synthesis. Therefore, niacin deficiency results in low NAD, which results in a phenotype with multiple anomalies including vertebral segmentation defects.110 Several studies identified in our systematic review describe in aggregate 20 cases of hemivertebra associated with VACTERL/VATER association (Table S3).1,2,4,5,8

DISCUSSION

4 |

Our study is a comprehensive systematic review describing known genetic diagnoses and syndromes associated with non-isolated hemivertebra in the fetal, neonatal, and infant periods. To our knowledge, this is the only systematic review describing the genetics of non-isolated hemivertebra. Our review is a readily accessible clinical guide for MFM specialists and geneticists counseling pregnant patients on this prenatal finding. Multiple organ systems are likely to have malformations associated with hemivertebra. This includes musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, central nervous system, genitourinary, gastrointestinal, and facial dysmorphisms. The pulmonary system was the least commonly affected. Therefore, we suggest a comprehensive fetal evaluation for every organ system when hemivertebra is diagnosed by prenatal ultrasound. Our review demonstrated 25 cases associated with cytogenetic abnormalities as well as 59 cases associated with single gene disorders associated with non-isolated hemivertebra. Molecular evaluation for single gene disorders was not performed in the majority of identified cases and many were diagnosed clinically. Genetic syndromes were notably common.

Many genetic syndromes have been associated with hemivertebra. Several signaling pathways are involved in somitogenesis, the formation of vertebral precursors, including the Wnt, FGF and Notch pathways.111 The genetic origin of hemivertebra has been studied in both mouse and rat models, and some genes have been demonstrated to be conserved in their human counterparts. Mutations in Dll3, Fgfr1, Dll1, and Tbx6 have been shown to interrupt normal somite patterning in animal studies resulting in severe vertebral and rib abnormalities.112–118 Similar abnormalities are seen in human studies involving changes to DLL3 and FGFR3.99,119

Strengths of our review are its comprehensive update of non-isolated hemivertebra genetic and syndromic associations, including query of the Human Phenotype Ontology database to broaden the inclusion of known genes and syndromes. Our study reinforces the importance of prenatal genetic workup for non-isolated hemivertebra with karyotype and chromosomal microarray as first line recommendations. Our main goal is to guide prenatal diagnosis and counseling of pregnant patients with non-isolated hemivertebra. That is why we chose the fetal, neonatal, and infant periods for what abnormalities would most likely be detected in the prenatal period. We thought that including infants up to 1 year of age may increase the acquisition of genetic etiologies that may manifest in the prenatal period as ultrasound has limitations in the detection of congenital abnormalities.

Limitations include genetic reporting differences in older articles due to the older genetic diagnostic testing technology that was previously available. Many of the single genes listed with single gene disorders described in case reports or series in Table 3 are extrapolations based on clinical diagnosis. Another limitation of our study relates to the classification of vertebral malformations. The current classification system recommends against using the term Jarcho-Levin as this has been replaced with spondylothoracic dysostosis.120 We used the diagnosis reported in the manuscripts and we did not reclassify the syndromes associated with the vertebral malformation. We also limited our search to cases diagnosed before 1 year of age, which may bias our search to cases with major anomalies that may have triggered larger clinical workup. The temporal association of hemivertebra and other anomalies is unclear: hemivertebra may be the initial finding or secondary following additional evaluation. Another limitation of our study was that other syndromes that may be associated with non-isolated hemivertebra have been excluded because they were not captured within our search terms and inclusion criteria, such as Pfeiffer syndrome.5,8,95 The 29 genes identified within the HPO query that were not picked up by our search were missed most likely due to age exclusion criteria or because age was not specified in potential cases (Table S4). Cases of hemivertebra diagnosed in the fetal, neonatal, and infant periods may be more often published when associated with congenital anomalies. Finally, data was not reported uniformly in every case report or series.

Since the objective of our study is to describe the cytogenetic, single gene and syndromic etiologies of non-isolated hemivertebra, we had to use case reports and small case series especially as they described the prenatal and the postnatal phenotypes in depth. Therefore, our study cannot determine the detection rate of abnormal cytogenetics or single gene disorders in non-isolated hemivertebra. Single case reports of non-isolated hemivertebra associated with abnormal genetic results are more likely to be reported resulting in publication bias. Large series are the appropriate studies that should be used if we were to determine the detection rate of genetic aberrations in non-isolated hemivertebra. The large case series in Table S3 presented their data in aggregate and thus we did not have individual data for each case.

We recommend a detailed ultrasound to evaluate fetal anatomy once hemivertebra is visualized on prenatal ultrasound given the association of hemivertebra with malformations in multiple organ systems. We recommend a fetal echocardiogram due to the considerable association of hemivertebra with congenital heart disease in our review. In addition, we recommend neurosonography for improved central nervous system prenatal phenotype evaluation. Also, we recommend fetal MRI if available, as this may provide higher-resolution prenatal phenotyping since multiple organ systems are likely to be affected with fetal abnormalities. Serial growth ultrasounds are recommended given the known association between fetal growth restriction and genetic syndromes. If there are no additional complicating factors, we suggest standard obstetric management for labor and delivery.

Prognosis for future pregnancies may include an increased risk of neural tube defects in future siblings. In patients diagnosed with multiple vertebral anomalies, there may be a 3%–7% recurrence risk for neural tube defects, and a 2%–3% recurrence risk for another child with multiple vertebral anomalies.106 Genetic counseling and prenatal ultrasound surveillance for subsequent pregnancies is recommended.

Cytogenetic analysis should be offered especially when multiple anomalies are present. We suggest starting with a karyotype or single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) chromosomal microarray when hemivertebra is identified, as multiple cases of chromosomal disorders with mosaicism are described in our systematic review. Exome sequencing should be the next line of investigation given its ability for molecular diagnosis for the diverse spectrum of single gene disorders associated with non-isolated hemivertebra.
