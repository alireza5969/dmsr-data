A systematic review of parenting scales measurement invariance/equivalence by race and ethnicity: Recommendations for inclusive parenting research

[http://orcid.org/0000-0001-8543-2061] Violeta J. Rodriguez

Dominique L. La Barrie

Miriam Zegarac

Anne Shaffer

 

Department of Psychology, University of Georgia

Abstract

The limited inclusion of racial/ethnic minorities in the development and validation of parenting measures limits our understanding of whether parenting constructs are valid in racial and ethnic minorities. Tests of measurement invariance/equivalence (MI/E) of parenting measures can help evaluate the validity of parenting constructs among racial/ethnic minorities. This systematic review summarized studies on MI/E of parenting constructs by race/ethnicity and evaluated the strength of the evidence. A literature search was conducted using various databases and references to retrieve studies from the United States. Ten studies were identified that tested for MI/E of eight parenting scales by race/ethnicity. Only one scale showed moderate evidence of MI/E, five showed weak evidence of MI/E, and two showed no evidence of MI/E. Most studies (80%) used factor analytic methods to test for MI/E, but only two studies (20%) examined all levels of invariance. These findings show that differences exist in how racial/ethnic minorities perceive parenting constructs. Further research is needed to develop more inclusive parenting measures, to protect against the ways in which biased measures may pathologize or misrepresent parenting practices among racial/ethnic minorities.

Due to the dynamic nature of parenting, operationalizing and quantifying parenting constructs can be challenging. An additional challenge in the quantification of parenting behavior and practices is that these constructs may vary depending on different developmental and demographic factors that influence parenting (e.g., parent age, education, culture, race). Beyond the challenge associated with the development of measures to quantify parenting behavior, the recommended validation procedures of measures can be extensive and require advanced psychometric expertise (Chan, 2014). Nevertheless, theory building requires the precise definition and operationalization of the implicated theoretical constructs, and without the use of valid measures, the accuracy of findings may be compromised. This could lead to erroneous or unfounded conclusions, which can be particularly troublesome when comparing racial or ethnic groups. Among many other factors, different levels of acculturation and socioeconomic status in racial and ethnic minorities may bias self-report measures for these groups. For example, in a measure of negative parenting practices, items referencing healthcare (e.g., doctors, hospitals) functioned differently in Black versus White participants, likely due to racial differences in healthcare access that do not necessarily reflect differences in parenting values or beliefs, compromising construct validity (Rodriguez et al., 2019). For the current review, our aim was to identify measures of parenting that have been validated across racial and ethnic groups in the United States, and we focused on self-report measures of parenting.

Self-reports of parenting are broad in scope but typically capture one or more of the following domains: parenting behavior (i.e., specific parenting practices, responses, action), parental beliefs and values (i.e., beliefs about child autonomy, socialization goals), parental self-perceptions (e.g., parenting confidence, self-efficacy), and parental knowledge (e.g., understanding of parenting principles). Although measures to quantify many aspects of parenting exist, psychometric data on parenting measures has been scarce, based on two comprehensive reviews in recent decades (Hurley et al., 2014; Locke & Prinz, 2002). In these reviews, a number of psychometric flaws in existing parenting measures have been identified. Locke and Prinz (2002) considered 76 questionnaires related to the measurement of parental discipline and nurturance. Locke and Prinz (2002) highlighted potential issues certain aspects of ineffective parenting practices (e.g., spanking, disciplining in anger) given that these may be influenced by social desirability bias. Other concerns about measuring discipline and nurturance included the dynamic and multidimensional nature of parenting. That is, several dimensions of parenting may need to be assessed, while accounting for the fact that parenting is dynamic across settings and contexts. Locke and Prinz (2002) also highlighted the lack of cultural appropriateness of measures, and the need to assess the equivalence of measures across different cultural groups. A more comprehensive review of parenting measures, which included constructs besides nurturance and discipline was conducted by Hurley et al. (2014). This review identified 164 measures, but only 25 (15%) of these provided psychometric data. For the measures with psychometric data, only five measures (20%) had strong psychometric properties, and 44% did not provide basic information on scoring. Hurley et al. (2014) therefore concluded that the level of psychometric evidence for the parenting measures identified is “dismal” (p. 820).

Across these reviews, the absence of minority groups in the validation procedures of parenting measures is notable. Slightly more than half (56%) of identified measures related to nurturance and discipline were developed and utilized with predominantly White participants (Locke & Prinz, 2002). In some of the remaining measures (39%), the validation samples included approximately 20% racial and ethnic minorities. Even with greater inclusion of racial and ethnic minorities (Locke & Prinz, 2002), it is unknown whether the constructs assessed by parenting measures yield bias-free scores across racial and ethnic minorities when used in racially and ethnically heterogeneous samples. It is also still unknown whether the constructs evaluated in parenting research have a similar meaning across these groups, which can help researchers trust the accuracy of comparisons. Previous reviews have identified important psychometric information about existing measures of parenting. However, less is known about psychometric properties relevant for racially and ethnically diverse populations, such as measurement invariance/equivalence (MI/E), as highlighted by Locke and Prinz (2002). The current review thus identified measures of parenting that had been tested for MI/E by race and ethnicity.

Ethnic and Racial Differences in Parenting

The psychological literature persists in addressing the degree to which aspects of parenting are universal, and which are culturally specific. While some theorists have argued that there is universality in the domains in which parents socialize their children (e.g., protection, control, guided learning; Grusec & Davidov, 2010), others have noted that there may be culturally specific differences in how these domain-specific goals are enacted and operationalized (Soenens et al., 2015). Despite the absence of transracially and transethnically validated tools, ethnic and racial differences have been reported in previous research. Below, we briefly review commonly reported racial and ethnic differences in parenting.

Different cultural, racial, and ethnic norms influence parenting practices as well as parental aspirations and perceptions of their children’s behavior (Knauer et al., 2017). Consequently, various demographic factors must be taken into consideration when assessing parenting and its relations to children’s development (Bornstein et al., 2013; Knauer et al., 2017; Quintana et al., 2006). For example, parenting practices and beliefs may differ based on whether parents endorse collectivistic or individualistic principles or standards of behavior, and these values may covary with race and ethnicity (Vargas & Kemmelmeier, 2013). Thus, although it may be universally agreed that a task of parenting is to teach children adaptive skills to function in their respective environments, parenting beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors may vary widely depending on the contexts and values of racial and ethnic groups in the United States. For instance, previous studies have concluded that White American parents are more likely utilize techniques of inductive reasoning with their children, which ultimately promotes autonomous decisions, consistent with individualistic cultural practices (McGroder, 2000). Similarly, studies have found that White American parents are more likely to emphasize self-direction (O’reilly et al., 1986; Querido et al., 2002) and developing a strong sense of happiness and self-esteem (Hill & Sprague, 1999; Robinson & Harris, 2013). These parenting practices are consistent with individualistic values that are intended to encourage children’s independence and self-sufficiency.

In contrast to White American parents, Black and Latinx American parents may be more likely to place a higher value on joint parenting, with accountability and responsibility diffused to a larger group beyond parents and immediate family members. That is, Black and Latinx American families are more likely to believe in collectivistic, community-centered parenting that pulls support from extended family members and friends (Sabogal et al., 1987). Research has concluded that Black American parents are more likely to utilize physical punishment techniques than White Americans (Querido et al., 2002) and that they place a higher importance on obedience and grades in school, particularly for boys (Hill & Sprague, 1999; Robinson & Harris, 2013). Due to their diverse racial composition, Latinx parents have been known to report an even greater variety of parenting practices. Domenech Rodríguez et al. concluded that Latinx parenting is “protective,” defined as being high on warmth and demandingness, as well as low on granting autonomy (Ayón et al., 2015; Domènech Rodriguez et al., 2009). Other researchers have described Latinx mothers as believing in “firm control”, described as being consistent and setting limits and boundaries (Fischer et al., 2009). When comparing different ethnic groups within Latinx communities, Dominican parents, who frequently identify as Afro-Caribbean, tend to use authoritative parenting, which typically involves communication with children about their decisions and resulting consequences (Varela et al., 2004). Alternatively, Mexican American parents are more likely to be authoritarian with their children, utilizing more controlling tactics and what has been described as inconsistent discipline (Calzada et al., 2017; Varela et al., 2004). However, inconsistent discipline in a collectivist environment may actually refer to the various parenting figures in a family with collectivist values (Dumka et al., 1996). Similarly, the constructs of protectiveness and control may be intended to foster interdependence and adherence to norms and respect for elders, important tenets of collectivistic value systems. Alternatively, protectiveness in parents among minorities may be an adaptive strategy to protect children from racism and discrimination.

These broad cultural differences highlight potential differences in the perceptions of effective parenting and normative processes within each racial and ethnic group. In addition, most measures of parenting have been developed in predominantly White samples and from Eurocentric perspectives (Friedlmeier et al., 2011), so measures may not function similarly in other groups. For example, individualistic practices that are more prevalent among White parents may be viewed as a standard and basis for comparison. When there is a lack of measurement research on other racial and ethnic groups, these implicit and explicit comparisons may not be sensitive to underlying cultural differences.

Psychometric Approaches to Establishing Transracial and Transethnic Measurement Invariance/Equivalence

Despite the aforementioned ethnic and racial differences in parenting practices, the validity of these comparisons remains unknown given the lack of psychometric data on racial and ethnic differences in parenting measures. One threat to validity that is particularly relevant to transracially and transethnically inclusive parenting research is a lack of measurement invariance/equivalence (MI/E), which reflects equality of measurement constructs (configural), factor loadings (metric), regression intercepts (scalar), and error/uniqueness (residual or strict), whereas the structural concern involves invariance of factor covariance and means. Without MI/E, questions arise regarding the accuracy of mean-level transracial and transethnic comparisons, the conceptualization of the construct, and the possibility of instruments measuring a different construct than intended, compromising its validity. When screening measures are used, a lack of MI/E can affect the number of individuals who are “caught” during the screening process (Gonzalez & Pelham III, 2020), potentially affecting eligibility for interventions or the level of perceived need. Given that a recommendation from a previous review of parenting measures (Hurley et al., 2014) is to develop norms that enable researchers and practitioners to reliably qualify parenting skills as “low” or “high” for clinical purposes and longitudinal change, bias in one item alone can dictate the need for intervention or improvement.

There are several types of research questions or study designs including racial and ethnic minorities that are affected by MI/E or the absence of MI/E. First, without testing for MI/E, there may be systemic ethnic or racial differences that preclude responding to rating scales in similar ways. For instance, if researchers were interested studying statistical or descriptive differences and similarities in levels of protection across racial or ethnic groups, MI/E would need to be established in order to determine whether these differences are real or are artificially inflated due to differences in how different groups interpret the measures. To reach accurate conclusions about these differences or similarities, a researcher would need to administer a scale that is invariant across groups. Second, MI/E across groups is necessary when researchers are interested in administering a scale to a racially and ethnically diverse group. With an increasing focus in racial and ethnic diversity in several disciplines, researchers may be tempted to administer existing scales developed in White American families to increasingly diverse samples. In these cases, even if no comparisons across groups are made, the absence of MI/E may affect observed relations due to nonequivalence of the scales in the subgroups of the samples. Therefore, the conclusions reached based on the administration of biased scales may yield inaccurate findings and conclusions. In this case, researchers may reach conclusions about a racially or ethnically heterogenous sample that are not applicable for some of the subgroups in the data. Third, with the increasing need of studying racial and ethnic minorities, researchers may use scales developed in predominantly White, non-Hispanic participants among homogenous samples of a different race or ethnicity. The relevance of MI/E in this case would emerge when the researcher wishes to argue that the underlying construct the scale purports to measure has the same meaning in that other racial or ethnic group. Measurement invariance/equivalence becomes less relevant when the construct under study is only relevant to a homogenous racial or ethnic group and the researcher develops and validates that measure for a specific use in that population (Knight et al., 2002).

Multiple methods of testing measurement equivalence exist, including multiple group confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA), multiple indicators multiple causes (MIMIC), item response theory (IRT) differential item functioning (DIF). Other tests of DIF such as the Mantel-Haenszel method, the delta method, and generalizability theory are non-IRT methods that are used to test MI/E in small sample sizes (for a review see Millsap and Everson (1993). The most commonly used methods of testing MI/E in parenting research are MGCFA and IRT DIF and are subsequently discussed. The recommended procedure for testing MI/E using MGCFA (Brown, 2014), is to first, conduct a CFA in each of the groups of interest and determine whether the groups interpret the measure similarly (dimensional invariance) – that is, whether the groups have the same factor structure. Second, tests of configural invariance are conducted, which help determine whether the factor structure of the measure provides an acceptable fit for the groups of interest, as defined by the same set of items across groups. In order to test configural invariance, the theoretical model is specified in each of the groups, allowing factor loadings and item intercepts to vary for each group. The model fit between groups is then compared; a good or acceptable fit suggests that the specified factor structure is similar for both groups. Third, tests of equality of factor loadings or metric invariance (Λg′=Λg′) are conducted to determine whether factor loadings are similar across the groups of interest. This time, the factor loadings are constrained to be equal across groups, while still allowing the item intercepts to vary. If the constraints on equal factor loadings result in diminished model fit, then it is assumed that the factor loadings are not similar across the groups of interest. Metric invariance would suggest that the conceptualization of the construct of interest is similar between groups, and that the items in the constructs are equivalently associated with the latent factors across the two groups. Fourth, scalar invariance (τg′=τg′)—or equality of indicator thresholds or intercepts— are tested to evaluate whether the item thresholds or intercepts are similar across groups. As in the previous step, thresholds or intercepts are constrained to be equal, and if this results in a reduction in model fit, it is concluded that item thresholds or intercepts are not equal across groups. Scalar invariance suggests that, independent of group membership, individuals who have the same value on the latent variable would obtain the same value on the observed variables. Therefore, the absence of scalar invariance would suggest that the means of individuals belonging to different groups cannot be accurately compared. Lastly, strict invariance (θg′=θg′), or equality of indicator residual variances, is tested in a similar fashion as the two previous steps, constraining residual variances to be equal and then assessing the change in model fit. Stricter levels of invariance, such as structural invariance, which includes invariant factor variances and factor covariances, can be tested using a structural equation modeling framework. A lack of strict invariance may point to differences in response patterns across groups, with different amounts of error. Partial MI/E may be established by allowing a few of the items to vary, while constraining other parameters to be equal, although the impact of partial MI/E on comparison of traits remains uncertain (Millsap & Kwok, 2004). However, a limitation of MGCFAs is that the findings are sample dependent, and reflect a characteristic of the sample, and not of the measure.

One method of overcoming the challenge of the sample-dependent estimates derived from CFAs is the use of IRT techniques to test for DIF. Item response theory methods yield sample-free estimates when sufficient heterogeneity is present in the sample and thus can be generalized to other samples. To this end, the use of IRT methods of assessing DIF may provide researchers with greater reassurance of a lack of bias across items when assessing parenting constructs. However, the level of computational complexity of IRT methods is much greater than those of MGCFA; as such, MGCFA remains the most commonly used method of testing for MI/E in parenting research. A couple of limitations associated with IRT include the frequent assumptions of unidimensionality and local independence, which may be untenable for multifaceted parenting constructs. Several IRT techniques can be used to test for DIF, including 1) estimating the area between two item response functions, 2) Lord’s χ, and 3) likelihood ratio tests (S. Kim et al., 1994). Item response theory models may be estimated differently depending on the dimensional nature of the latent trait, θ, including whether it is unidimensional (single trait) or multidimensional (multiple traits). The estimation of IRT models may also depend on whether response options are dichotomous or polytomous. Techniques for dichotomous IRT models differ depending on the parameters used by the models, including models that account for guessing, discrimination, and difficulty. Polytomous models include the partial credit model and the graded response model. Discussing these different methods is beyond the scope of the current review; the interested reader is referred to Teresi (2001) and Nering and Ostini (2011) for a review of IRT models and techniques.

Objectives of Current Review

Findings from empirical studies on MI/E of parenting constructs can help researchers understand the nature of differences across groups and can help evaluate the universality of parenting constructs across race and ethnicity. The current review will therefore summarize the literature on MI/E of parenting constructs and evaluate the strength of the evidence supporting the transracial and transethnic validity of parenting measures in the United States. The decision to limit the review to the United States was based on the additional potential confounds associated with international research as well as different reporting categories for race/ethnicity. That is, beyond differences in the meaning of constructs, international research requires greater adaptation to accommodate dialects in addition to translations into, or local adaptations of, the primary language. For conceptual reasons, translations of scales into different languages for use in the United States do not imply equivalence in participants’ native countries/continents because even though there may be similar cultural values within racial and ethnic groups, the context can be vastly different still in presence of acculturation. Similarly, scales validated for use internationally do not imply MI/E across racial and ethnic groups in the United States. This review will focus on the extant literature that has tested MI/E in self-report measures of parenting across racial and ethnic groups in order to better understand differences and similarities in parenting across racial and ethnic groups. Though measurement bias is not exclusive to self-report scale and may be present in observational systems, such systems require different psychometric considerations (Hallgren, 2012). As noted above, the goal of this review is to contribute to our understanding of whether/how universal these parenting measures have been shown to be across race and ethnicity in the United States and determine at what level of evidence these scales have been shown to be valid across races/ethnicity. To this end, we aimed to identify measures shown to be robust to racial and ethnic differences in previous research, as evidenced by MI/E using factor analytic or IRT methods.

Method

Search Strategy and Study Selection

A literature search was conducted using the PsycINFO, PubMed, Mental Measurements Yearbook, MEDLINE, Tests in Print, and Social Services Abstracts databases to retrieve studies on MI/E of parenting constructs. The eligibility criteria were 1) the study sample included parents in the United States, 2) the study reported the results of the tests of MI/E of a self-report measure and the method used to arrive at these conclusions, and 3) tests of MI/E were by race/ethnicity and not other types of MI/E (e.g., temporal, sex). The final search was conducted on January 24, 2020. Although initial search terms included several synonyms of “measurement invariance” and “measurement equivalence”, umbrella terms were decided using multiple database thesauruses. The final search terms therefore included (“measurement invariance” OR “differential item functioning” OR “test bias” OR “cultural test bias”) AND (“race” OR “ethnicity”) AND (parent*). No language or publication date limitations were placed on the search. To identify other articles assessing MI/E, the reference lists of the identified articles were reviewed to identify other empirical studies of MI/E of parenting constructs.

Data Extraction

One coder extracted the name of the scale, sample characteristics (age, gender, race, ethnicity), and parent/caregiver characteristics (gender, race, ethnicity, clinical vs. nonclinical), language translation or adaptation, and types of analyses conducted (factor analyses, differential item functioning). Using Endnote citation management system (X9), all search results were aggregated, and duplicates removed. The data extracted were then reviewed using a descriptive and narrative approach to summarize the key findings. Figure 1 presents the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram for the current review (Moher et al., 2009). A PRISMA 2020 Checklist is also included as supplemental material (Page et al., 2020), though the systematic review was planned prior the publication of the 2020 guidelines. As noted in Figure 1, initially, n = 207 studies were identified through database searches; an additional n = 42 studies were identified using the ancestry method. From these n = 249 studies, n = 59 duplicate studies were removed, and n = 190 studies were screened. Based on the title and abstract, n = 164 studies were excluded given that these studies were either outside of the United States, measured non-parenting related constructs, or simply referenced measurement invariance without conducting these tests. After excluding these studies, n = 26 full texts were reviewed; n = 166 studies were excluded after the full text review (reasons listed in Figure 1).

Quantification of the Strength of the Evidence

In order to quantify the strength of the evidence for the validation of scales for transracial and transethnic groups, evidence of MI/E of parenting constructs across race and ethnicity is classified as “none”, “conflicting”, “weak”, “moderate”, and “strong”, as in previous research (Stevanovic et al., 2017). These levels of evidence for MI/E are described below:

None

“None” refers to scales that have been shown to lack invariance, or scales that have only demonstrated dimensional (same number of factors), configural (similar factor structure, including the same set of items), or metric (the factors have an equivalent meaning) invariance. Metric invariance would also fall in this category along with scales identified to have DIF that is more than negligible in more than a few items.

Conflicting

“Conflicting” evidence refers to mixed evidence on MI/E reported by two or more samples or studies. For scales with conflicting evidence, if there are three studies or more, the level of evidence will be evaluated based on the findings of the majority, but the overall level will be classified one level below.

Weak

“Weak” evidence points to at least one study or sample showing full or partial invariance for at least two racial or ethnic groups. Similarly, a study or sample showing no items with DIF or a few studies with DIF on any items would also be considered “Weak” evidence.

Moderate

“Moderate” evidence indicates that at least one study has shown a scale to be fully or partially invariant (scalar) for at least three racial or ethnic groups. Moderate evidence may also be demonstrated by strong (scalar) invariance for two racial or ethnic groups across multiple studies, or no DIF or only a few items with DIF in one study.

Strong

“Strong” evidence refers to at least one study or sample showing full or partial strong (scalar) invariance/equivalence across at least five racial or ethnic groups, or full or partial strong (scalar) invariance/equivalence for at least three ethnic or racial groups across multiple studies. Strong invariance maybe demonstrated when the scale has no DIF in items, or only a few items with DIF across at least five racial or ethnic groups.

Results

Demographic Characteristics of Study Samples

Study sample sizes ranged from n = 184 (Dumka et al., 1996) to n = 55,386 (Chen & Zhu, 2017). Five studies reported parents’ age, and nine reported their gender. Parents’ ages ranged from M = 30.5 (SD = 8.1) (Kong et al., 2015) to 41.5 (SD = 10.6) years of age (Lindhiem et al., 2019). In the majority of studies, parents were mostly mothers, with percentages varying from 64% (Lindhiem et al., 2019) to 100% (Kong et al., 2015), with only one sample being 100% fathers (Sotomayor-Peterson et al., 2013). The most commonly studied racial and ethnic groups were Black, White and Latinx; however, one study included Lumbee Native Americans (Halberstadt et al., 2013). A total of eight measures were identified to have been tested for MI/E across ten different studies (refer to Table 1 for a list of measures). A total of four studies (Anderson et al., 2005; Dumka et al., 1996; Scott, 2012; Sotomayor-Peterson et al., 2013) reported having translated the measure into Spanish or the measure was already available in Spanish. A summary of demographic characteristics by study is presented in Table 1.

Measurement Invariance/Equivalence Methods

As outlined in Table 2, there were differences in how MI/E methods were applied across studies. The majority of studies (n = 8; 80%) tested MI/E using confirmatory factor analysis. Two (Chen & Zhu, 2017; Lindhiem et al., 2019) used DIF (DIF contrast, difference in item characteristic curves using logistic regression, and Mantel-Haenszel chi-square). When using factor analytic methods, only two studies (Feaster et al., 2010; Perez et al., 2018) tested for all levels of measurement and structural invariance. One study tested for all levels of measurement invariance, but not structural invariance (Kong et al., 2015). In another study, configural, metric, and strict invariance were tested, but scalar was not (Anderson et al., 2005), although it would typically be tested following configural and metric invariance before strict invariance. In two studies, strict and structural invariance were not tested (Halberstadt et al., 2013; Sotomayor-Peterson et al., 2013). In one study (Dumka et al., 1996), functional (comparing the magnitude of pairs of variables across groups) and scalar invariance were tested, but no other forms of invariance were considered. Lastly, in one other study (Scott, 2012), due to a lack of configural invariance, no other forms of invariance were tested.

For MI/E using DIF methods, different methods were used. In one study (Chen & Zhu, 2017), a Rasch (one-parameter logistic) model (1PL) was applied and results were interpreted using Educational Testing Service (ETS) criteria. In the second study (Lindhiem et al., 2019), a three-parameter logistic (3PL) model was used; results were confirmed using the Mantel-Haenszel chi-square.

Summary of Findings by Domain and Strength of Evidence

Measurement invariance/equivalence findings by scale and domain are summarized in Table 2. Overall, two scales (Child Feeding Questionnaire and Parental Involvement) were shown to have no evidence of MI/E by race and ethnicity. Five scales (Parenting Practices Questionnaire, Parenting Involvement Inventory, Fathers’ Caregiving and Nurturing, Parents’ Beliefs About Children’s Emotions Questionnaire, and Parenting Self-Agency Scale) had weak evidence of MI/E by race and ethnicity, and one scale (Knowledge of Effective Parenting Test) had moderate evidence of MI/E.

Parenting Practices/Behaviors

A detailed summary of the MI/E findings by scale and domains are presented in Table 2. The overall the strength of the evidence for measures in the parenting practices/behaviors domain was None to Weak. The strength of the evidence for each of the scales in this domain are described below.

The Child Feeding Questionnaire (CFQ) was the parenting practices measure most commonly tested for MI/E, in three different samples (Anderson et al., 2005; Kong et al., 2015; Perez et al., 2018). The CFQ assesses parents’ perceptions and concerns about child obesity and their attitudes towards feeding practices. The CFQ is composed of 31 items that load on seven subscales, including perceived responsibility (for child feeding), parent perceived weight, perceived child weight, concerns about child weight, monitoring (of eating), restriction (of access to food), and pressure to eat. Of these factors, perceived responsibility and concerns about child weight refer to protecting their children from making unhealthy food choices, whereas the restriction, pressure to eat, and monitoring refer specifically to controlling their behavior through pressure or limiting access to food. The strength of the evidence for the CFQ was categorized as Conflicting given that configural and metric measurement invariance (MI) were reached in some of the samples, but only configural MI was reached two other samples (Kong et al., 2015; Perez et al., 2018).

The Parental Involvement (PI) scale was examined by Chen and Zhu (2017) and included subscales on school/home environment (e.g., meeting child’s teacher, attending an open house, parent-teacher conference, school event or fundraiser), family educational investment (e.g., dance lessons, organized clubs, art lessons), and family routines (e.g., eating breakfast/dinner together). The School/Home Environment and Family Educational Investment subscales were mapped on to group participation given that these subscales refer to learning in group settings, whereas Family Routines includes items referencing more one-one-one opportunities for learning. The strength of the evidence for the PI scale was categorized as None because there were items with DIF in two different subscales for several of the groups tested (White, Asian, and Black parents).

The Parenting Practices Questionnaire (PPQ) (Feaster et al., 2010) includes four subscales: positive parenting, discipline effectiveness, avoidance of discipline, and monitoring, which are part of the parental supervision and discipline interview used in the Oregon Youth Study and Pittsburgh Youth Study (Thornberry et al., 1995). Positive parenting includes items such as mutual affection and praise of desired behaviors. Discipline effectiveness, avoidance of discipline, and monitoring includes items referencing the implementation of chores and monitoring of daily activities. The strength of the evidence for the PPQ was categorized as Weak given that only partial scalar MI was reached for three different groups (Hispanic, Black, and White non-Hispanic parents).

The Parental Involvement Inventory (PII; (Scott, 2012) is composed of four subscales: parent-perceived amount of teacher communication, parent mastery orientation, parent involvement, and parent performance orientation. The teacher communication subscale refers to the amount of communication the parent perceives having with teachers about their child’s school. Parent mastery orientation refers to the parent’s degree of involvement in helping their child learn information. Parent involvement refers to how involved the parent is with their child’s schooling at home. Lastly, parent performance orientation refers to how aware the parent is of their child’s school performance. The strength of the evidence for the PII was categorized as None because dimensional invariance could not be established and therefore, no other levels of MI were tested.

The Fathers’ Caregiving and Nurturing (FCN; (Sotomayor-Peterson et al., 2013) was derived from the Questions for Fathers and Other Important People Survey. The items are related child caregiving, specifically feeding, preparing meals, playing activities, putting to sleep, as well as bathing and dressing the child. The strength of the evidence for the FCN was considered Weak given that only metric and scalar MI were established, but no other types of MI were tested.

Parental Values/Beliefs

The Parents’ Beliefs About Children’s Emotions Questionnaire (PBACE; (Halberstadt et al., 2013) is a 33-item questionnaire with 7 factors: cost of positivity (e.g., perceiving positivity to be costly), value of anger (i.e., parents valuing children’s anger), manipulation (i.e., thinking children use emotions to manipulate), control (i.e., thinking children can control their emotions), parental knowledge (i.e., not wanting to know about children’s feelings), autonomy (i.e., believing children can cope alone), and stability (i.e., believing children’s emotions are stable). The strength of the evidence for the PBACE was categorized as Weak given that the majority of subscales only reached partial MI.

Parental Self-Perceptions

The Parenting Self-Agency Scale (PSAS; (Dumka et al., 1996) has two different versions (five-item and ten-item). The items in the scale assess parenting confidence (10-item version) as well as a range of items referencing reciprocity (five-item version). The 10-item version of the PSAS was mapped on to the group participation domain, given that most items reference parents’ confidence in their ability to teach their children in a one-on-one context. The strength of the evidence for the PSAS, and therefore, the parental self-perceptions domain, was categorized as Weak given that only functional and scalar MI were reached for only two groups (White and immigrant Mexican mothers).

Parental Knowledge

The Knowledge of Effective Parenting Test (KEPT; (Lindhiem et al., 2019) is a test of different effective parenting strategies with items on praise, reward/point systems, attending and ignoring, commands/expectations, consequences, time-out, or two or more skills. The strength of the evidence for the KEPT, the only scale identified assessing parental knowledge, was categorized as Moderate because the final KEPT did not have items with significant DIF for Black, White and Hispanic parents.

Discussion

Despite the importance of MI/E to transracial and transethnic research, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first systematic review to summarize studies on MI/E by race and ethnicity of parenting constructs. Evidence of MI/E strengthens the validity of parenting constructs as broadly applicable and salient across groups and increases the confidence that mean-level comparisons among groups can be interpreted accurately. This systematic review summarized the literature on MI/E by race and ethnicity for self-report measures of parenting, included measures of parenting practices/behaviors, parental values/beliefs, parental self-perceptions, and parenting knowledge. We evaluated the strength of the evidence supporting the transracial and transethnic validity of parenting measures quantifying these domains.

This systematic review found that there were only eight scales that have been tested for MI/E across race or ethnicity. The limited psychometric evidence of parenting measures identified in this study is consistent with previous reviews of parenting measures. A comprehensive systematic review (Hurley et al., 2014) on the psychometric quality of parenting scales identified 25 parenting measures, although it did not include data on the psychometric validity of the measures across racial and ethnic groups. Hurley and colleagues concluded that the number of studies with adequate psychometric data was “dismal” given that less than half received a high score on psychometric quality. An older review on the measurement of parental discipline and nurturance reported that at least half (56%) of the measures identified were developed in completely White samples (Locke & Prinz, 2002). When Locke and Prinz’s review was published, the authors reported that the examination of MI/E across minority participants, including basic examinations such as differences in factor structures, was rare. It is not surprising, therefore, that the earliest study examining MI/E identified in the current review was Dumka et al. (1996), despite not setting any publication date limitations on the search. The racial and ethnic groups in which MI/E was assessed across studies were primarily White, Black, and Latinx. One study included Asian participants (Lindhiem et al., 2019), but was underpowered to assess MI/E in this group. Another study (Halberstadt et al., 2013) included Lumbee Americans, although scalar invariance was not reached for most PBACE subscales.

Among the types of parent self-report measures, parenting practices/behaviors were those most commonly tested for MI/E. These included the Child Feeding Questionnaire, Parental Involvement, Parenting Practices Questionnaire, Parenting Involvement Inventory, and Fathers’ Caregiving and Nurturing scales. Other domains of parenting, including parental values/beliefs, parental self-perception, and parenting knowledge only included one scale each, including the PBACE, Parenting Self-Agency Scale, and the Knowledge of Effective Parenting Test, respectively. The strength of the evidence for most of the parenting domains was None to Weak, with the exception of parenting knowledge, for which one scale (KEPT) demonstrated Moderate evidence of MI/E (Lindhiem et al., 2019). However, the KEPT does not measure what is typically the focus of parenting studies covered in other domains (i.e., parenting behaviors). Notably, for instance, widely used measures of parenting, such as the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire (Frick, 1991), the Parent Child Conflict Tactics (Straus et al., 1998), and the Parenting Scale (Arnold et al., 1993), did not emerge as having been tested for MI/E among racial and ethnic minorities. Therefore, no measure that assesses perceptions of parental behaviors or beliefs emerged as having support for MI/E.

The lack of evidence supporting MI/E suggests that mean-level comparisons of parenting domains by race and ethnicity should be approached cautiously, and researchers should exercise caution in using existing self-report parenting measures in racially and ethnically diverse samples. The lack of MI/E also poses risks to the validity of the measures, because MI/E may point to the measurement of construct-irrelevant content. In those cases, MI/E compromises the generalizability of scores across racial and ethnic groups. Most importantly, a lack of MI/E across constructs may point to a lack of applicability of that specific construct to one of the groups. While conducting research among racially and ethnically diverse populations is essential to the development of the field, doing so in a manner that accurately represents historically underrepresented groups is also important.

Findings from this review also showed that the methods for testing MI/E varied across studies. Although the majority of studies used factor analytic methods, there were differences in how authors tested different levels of invariance. In fact, only two studies examined all levels of invariance using the recommended steps (Brown, 2014). Studies that used DIF, similarly, used different methods to evaluate the level of DIF. Factor analytic methods lend some support for the validity of parenting domains across racial and ethnic groups. However, findings from factor analytic methods are sample-dependent and tests of MI/E using these methods may not generalize to other samples. The increase in availability of software packages to assess MI/E using non-sample-dependent methods in recent years, such as Rasch models, may help stimulate research using non-sample-dependent analyses. These methodological differences may increase difficulty in evaluating research supporting the generalizability of parenting domains across race and ethnicity.

In the studies included in this systematic review, major sources of invariance appeared to be based on linguistic differences. For instance, Anderson et al. highlighted the use of the words “junk foods” given that these words can have different meanings to parents, particularly because eating habits can vary substantially by race and ethnicity (Hiza et al., 2013). These words may also be unknown to those who have not reached English proficiency or have low levels of acculturation. Anderson et al. also found differences in the construct of perceived weight, a construct that may vary by racial and ethnic expectations of weight (Huey, 2013). Similarly, Chen and Zhu (2017) found that the original conceptualization of some of the constructs, such as parental involvement and family educational investment, did not consider potential ethnic differences in their meaning. Certain aspects of educational investment, such as immigration for the purposes of educational achievement, may not be relevant to certain racial and ethnic groups, but very relevant to others. Therefore, in developing measures, not only is it important to assess whether an item defines a construct, but also whether an item is relevant to populations of interest. Comparable findings were found by Dumka et al. (1996), in which measurement differences in inconsistent parenting practices were found between White and Mexican parents. Specifically, among White mothers, inconsistent discipline was associated with aversive child behaviors, but among Mexican mothers, this association was not found. The authors concluded that these differences may have emerged from potential cultural differences in approaches to child-rearing. Whereas inconsistent discipline reports by Mexican mothers may involve other family members in child discipline, consistent with the collectivistic approach found in some Latinx families, reports of inconsistent discipline by White mothers may refer to the child actually being inconsistently disciplined because the mother was primarily or solely responsible for disciplining aversive child behavior. These findings emphasize the need to consider how language and context influence meaning, and how constructs may be understood differently among different racial and ethnic groups due to linguistic differences and cultural practices that influence understanding of language. These considerations should be made in the conceptualization phases of future measure development.

Recommendations for Inclusive Parenting Research

Increasing Racial and Ethnic Diversity

1.

Findings point to a lack of MI/E of parenting scales in racially and ethnically diverse samples. These findings appear to be rooted in broader measurement problems in the field of parenting, specifically the use of predominantly White, middle-class families in validation samples (e.g., Locke and Prinz, 2002; Hurley et al., 2014)). Increasing racial and ethnic diversity in validation samples may be one way by which psychometric properties in racial/ethnic minorities may be improved.

In terms of representation within study samples, future research should include additional racial and ethnic groups. For example, Asians currently have the fastest growth rate among major racial or ethnic groups in the US (Lopez et al., 2017). However, only two studies in the current review included Asians (Chen & Zhu, 2017; Lindhiem et al., 2019), and these studies were not sufficiently powered to conduct any analyses with this group. Further research is needed to examine the validity of parenting measures among Asian parents compared with other groups. Given that approximately 200 participants per group are needed to test for MI/E using factor analytic methods, and even more for IRT-based approaches or scales with a high number of items, data sharing and collaborative approaches may be necessary to increase the racial and ethnic diversity of parenting samples, particularly for groups with low representation (e.g., Asian, Native American races).

Increased Utilization of Culturally Sensitive Methods

2.

With increasing racial and ethnic diversity, researchers should be cautious to include marginalized groups intentionally and sensitively. For example, community-based participatory research is a burgeoning scientific approach for engaging marginalized groups which may enhance rapport and trust with marginalized communities that have been historically included from research (Satcher, 2005). In developing a new conceptualization of parenting to match the evolving demographics of the nation, exercising cultural humility may help support researchers’ openness to new perspectives.

Similarly, qualitative methods have been known to enhance content validity in psychological assessment, but these methods were generally underutilized (Vogt et al., 2004). While the focus on this review was on quantitative measures and methods, future research may benefit from incorporating qualitative approaches. Only one study collected qualitative data to understand how parents interpreted items (Scott, 2012). Qualitative interviews, including cognitive interviews (i.e., asking a respondent about how they interpret questionnaire items), may be useful tools in measure development and adaptation when working with underrepresented groups, specifically to understand how parents interpret items, and what their motivations are in responding to items given that these may differ across groups. Qualitative methods may also provide context for the meaning of different practices across racial/ethnic groups.

Testing for Measurement Invariance/Equivalence

3.

Given the increasing racial and ethnic diversity of the United States and parenting research, further consideration should be given to testing whether existing parenting scales can be used among racial and ethnic minorities. One way of moving the field forward may be to test these measures for MI/E in racially and ethnically diverse samples.

Modifying and Validating Existing Scales for Diverse Samples

4.

For items identified to be biased, improvements—either through the removal of items, addition of new items, or modification of existing items—to existing parenting scales may be needed. Additional item generation or modifications to existing items should be followed with thorough validation procedures and evaluation of relevant psychometric properties.

Using Existing Guidelines for Item Development

5.

Considering the major sources of linguistic differences identified in the studies reviewed, researchers may benefit using established methods for item development. For example, the Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Qualitative Item Review (DeWalt et al., 2007) may help identify problematic items (e.g., inconsistencies with domain definitions, semantic redundancy, item narrowness/confusion/vagueness) when considering different populations of interest as well as their needs and characteristics.

Development of Statistical Guidelines Applicable to Parenting Research

6.

Several different strategies were used to assess measurement invariance by race and ethnicity when applying both factor analytic and IRT strategies. The development of statistical guidelines applicable to parenting research may help reduce the use of both inconsistent, incorrect, and/or inappropriate analytical strategies to facilitate the aggregation and interpretation of findings. Feaster et al. (2010) provided a thorough example of testing both structural and measurement invariance with the goal of helping researchers understand and apply appropriate analyses, but broader uptake of these practices is needed in parenting research. Second, because factor analytic methods do not provide sample-independent estimates, IRT approaches may be preferable; however, only two studies reviewed used these methods.

Facilitating Training in Measurement Methods

7.

Recent methodological developments to conduct IRT analyses have combined different strategies to assess DIF using an IRT framework and Monte Carlo simulations (Choi et al., 2011), but still require advanced psychometric expertise in performing and interpreting these analyses. As such, methodological advances are needed in this area to facilitate these analyses in user-friendly platforms. Incorporating these analyses in more user-friendly platforms may help facilitate training in measurement methods to encourage researchers to test for MI/E and developing better tools for assessing racially and ethnically diverse samples.

Limitations

In evaluating findings from this review, some limitations must be noted. First, searches were limited to studies in the United States. This was decided prior to conducting the review to simplify its scope, as international comparisons can be more complex. In addition, within this US-based review, the racial and ethnic groups examined were based on grouping used in the reviewed studies. However, census data has shown that racial and ethnic identifications may vary over time; from 2000 to 2010, 6.1% of racial and ethnic self-identifications changed (Liebler et al., 2017). Further, heterogeneity is present among racial and ethnic groups that was not accounted for in the current study. For example, Valentine (1971) proposed a bicultural model of African Americans, suggesting that the Black community draws from both African American values, behaviors, and social lives, while simultaneously drawing from Eurocentric aspects from the dominant culture. Significant heterogeneity also exists among Latinx and Asian individuals. To encourage a better understanding of racial and ethnic minorities, Parham (2002) has suggested shifting to a model of cultural and ethnic identity instead of racial identity, and that may be a direction for future family research. There was also little diversity in parent sex given that most samples were composed of mothers.

As part of the data extraction process, only one reviewer extracted the data (first author), which may have biased the extraction process. Further, review was not registered in any in any platforms, which may affect its transparency and reproducibility. Lastly, this review was limited to self-report measures given that other sources of bias are introduced in observational systems (e.g., rater bias) and controlling for such sources of bias require different methods beyond the scope of this paper (i.e., Hallgren, 2012).

Conclusions

Parenting measures are an important link between the evolving theoretical models of parenting and the actual experiences of parents that require assessment and intervention. In general, this systematic review suggests that there is a need for studies examining MI/E of existing parenting scales. The lack of strong evidence supporting the MI/E of parenting scales suggests that further research is needed to revise or modify scales for use and comparison in diverse racial and ethnic groups. As with most psychological research, most of the literature on parenting, including the research that has defined effective and “normative” parenting, has revolved around middle-class White families in the United States. Thus, establishing MI/E across racial and ethnic minorities can help researchers develop a broader understanding of effective and normative parenting processes, and how these processes affect developmental outcomes more generally.
