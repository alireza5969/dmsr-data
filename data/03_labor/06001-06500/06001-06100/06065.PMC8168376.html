“Community-based Advance Care Planning Interventions for Minority Older Adults: A Systematic Review”

Abstract

Racial/ethnic minority older adults in the United States often experience access, language, and cultural barriers to advance care planning. A systematic review was conducted to summarize and appraise the current science on community-based interventions aimed at increasing advance care planning in racial/ethnic minority older adults. Five articles met the inclusion criteria, which represented four unique interventions in Asian American (n=2) and Hispanic (n=2) communities only. Two interventions were nurse-led workshops and two were one-on-one social worker-led sessions. Outcomes measured were intention to or completion of advance directive or advance care planning discussion, an improvement in advance directive knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, and comfort related to advance planning. Interventions increased intention to or completion of advance directives and improved advance care planning knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes. Results were inconclusive regarding promoting advance care planning discussions. Further high-quality research is needed to address the diverse needs and barriers to advance care planning.

Introduction

The end-of-life experience for many Americans is often characterized by suboptimal management of distressing symptoms, increased suffering for patients and families, and treatment misalignment with patients’ preferences and goals (Institute of Medicine [IOM], 1997, 2015). These suboptimal experiences often impact adults over the age of 60, many of whom have multiple chronic conditions, advanced illness, and some whom have a decline in decision-making capabilities (IOM, 2015; He et al., 2018). Therefore, it’s recommended that all patients, especially older adults, engage in advance care planning to align end-of-life treatments with patients’ preferences and goals (IOM, 1997, 2015). Advance care planning is a process in which individuals learn, prepare, and decide on potential future health care decisions in the event that they are no longer able to communicate their preferences (National Institute on Aging [NIA], 2018). For example, advance directives are a component of advance care planning, which are legal, living documents that formalize these end-of-life care preferences (NIA, 2018). Advance directive completion and advance care planning discussions are part of the larger process of advance care planning (Lum et al., 2015), which has resulted in increased use of palliative care, reduced hospitalizations, and improved quality of end-of-life care (Brinkman-Stoppelenburg et al., 2014).

Despite increased attention towards advance care planning (IOM, 2015), engagement remains nominal with approximately 30% of adults completing an advance directive in the United States (U.S.) (Yadav et al., 2017). These rates are lower in racial/ethnic minority older adults who have over 70% lower odds of advance care planning compared to non-Hispanic Whites (Harrison et al., 2016). Of the estimated 52.5 million older adults in the U.S., 13.9% are foreign-born (Mizoguchi et al., 2019) and 23% are racial/ethnic minorities (Administration for Community Living [ACL], 2018). Racial/ethnic minority older adults are a growing population expected to reach approximately 28 million by 2040 (ACL, 2019). The growth of racial/ethnic minority older adults has implications for the health care system as this population experiences poorer health outcomes, higher rates of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and activities of daily living impairment compared to non-Hispanic Whites (IOM, 2003; Ng et al., 2015). Frailty, a leading cause of death in older adults (Kulmala et al., 2014), is also higher among racial/ethnic minorities compared to non-Hispanic Whites (Bandeen-Roche et al., 2015). The coronavirus pandemic has further highlighted health disparities for racial/ethnic minority older adults and the importance of engaging them in the advance care planning process proactively (Curtis et al., 2020; Hopkins et al., 2020), as they share a disproportionate burden of the casualties (Webb Hopper et al., 2020).

The reason for these health disparities in advance care planning is multifactorial and includes: lack of access, language, and cultural barriers (Hong et al., 2018; Mayeda & Ward, 2019). For example, racial/ethnic minorities may not have access to advance care planning resources due to their high rates of uninsured (Sohn, 2017) and large proportions living in concentrated poverty (Meade, 2014). However, health disparities exist even when adjusting for socioeconomic determinants of health (Ng et al., 2015).

Most racial/ethnic minority older adults reside in the community (IOM, 2010), which make primary care settings ideal locations to discuss advance care planning for those who are insured. However, primary care providers often do not offer advance care planning due to lack of time or the challenge of effectively engaging racial/ethnic minority patients with diverse languages and cultural values (IOM, 2015). Most foreign-born older adults in the U.S. are from Latin America and Asia, many have limited English proficiency (Mizoguchi et al., 2019), and often receive inadequate health care compared to non-Hispanic Whites (Kim et al., 2011).

Racial/ethnic minority older adults are a vulnerable population, with complex health needs, that experience barriers to advance care planning (Harrison et al., 2016; Hong et al., 2018; Mayeda & Ward, 2019). To address these challenges, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine have recommended researchers design community-based interventions to increase advance care planning use in racial/ethnic minorities, especially ones that are culturally tailored and nurse-led (IOM, 2003; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2017). Therefore, our objective is to systematically review, summarize, and compare the efficacy of community-based interventions designed to increase advance care planning use among racial/ethnic minority older adults.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al., 2009).

Search Strategy

We developed a systematic search strategy and reviewed it with the Columbia University Irving Medical Center Informationist, who is a systematic review specialist. We conducted searches using three electronic databases: PubMed, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and Embase. The PubMed Medical Subject Headings, CINAHL subject headings, Embase Emtree search terms, and keywords included the domains of advance care planning (e.g., advance directives) and racial/ethnic minority group. We linked search terms and key words with Boolean operators. See Supplementary Table 1 for the full search strategy. Search filters were not applied in the database searches to obtain broad results.

Eligibility Criteria

We included all peer-reviewed articles published up to April 2020. Furthermore, we included articles describing randomized and non-randomized studies of interventions to increase advance care planning in racial/ethnic minority older adults. Interventions were held in community settings (e.g., non-acute settings, places of worship) to address access barriers for the large population of community-dwelling older adults. We included peer-reviewed study reports (i.e., no grey literature or commentaries) to focus on articles with high scientific rigor in which the study populations had a mean age 60 or older, and an intervention was tested in the U.S. to improve advance care planning. We excluded studies in which participants included non-Hispanic Whites (i.e., not a racial/ethnic minority) as these interventions did not target on the population of interest. International and non-English language articles were excluded as we expected different health care barriers faced by racial/ethnic minorities and differences in advance care planning processes in other countries. We also excluded studies if they targeted caregiver, family, or surrogates due to wide variation in demographics.

Study Selection

Two researchers (LVE and CC) independently screened all studies identified in the systematic search by using Covidence, a web-based platform, to manage the title, abstract, and full-text screening. In the first stage, a title and abstract screening excluded studies deemed not meeting the inclusion criteria. The second stage was a full-text assessment of the remaining articles and inclusion was based on the criteria previously listed. We included articles without abstracts or with other ambiguities in the second stage. Additionally, researchers performed ascendency and descendancy searches on the final included articles to find studies not identified in the systematic search.

Data Collection

Eligible articles were independently reviewed, and data was systematically abstracted with a standardized data extraction form by reviewers (LVE and CC). We collected the following study characteristics in detail: objective, setting, sample characteristics, design, intervention characteristics, outcomes, and outcome descriptions. A systematic quality appraisal of each study was also conducted (see below).

Quality Appraisal

Two researchers (LVE and CC) independently appraised the quality of the included articles using the Downs and Black (1998) checklist. Results were compared, inconsistencies were discussed, and a third reviewer (PSW) adjudicated as needed. The checklist has been psychometrically tested and is valid for appraisal of interventional studies (Downs & Black, 1998). This modified 26-item checklist assesses the quality of randomized and non-randomized health care interventions. It provides the following standardized appraisal criteria: 1) study reporting (e.g., clear objectives, descriptions); 2) external validity (i.e., generalizability of results); 3) internal validity (i.e., bias and confounding); and 4) power. All checklist items are weighted equally whether or not the relevant data was reported (i.e., YES = 1 and NO = 0), except for Item 5. Item 5 is weighted higher (i.e., YES = 2, PARTIALLY = 1, and NO = 0) and evaluates the reporting of principal confounders for cohorts. Item 8 (i.e. reporting adverse events) was excluded as the reviewers deemed it not applicable. Item 27 (i.e., power) was scored as a binary indicator (i.e., YES = 1 and NO = 0), which allowed for a simplified audit for power. The total scores range from 0 - 27. A modified score categorization classified the quality of included studies: excellent (25 - 27), good (19-24), fair (14 - 18), and poor (≤ 13).

Synthesis of Results

We used Donabedian’s Healthcare Quality conceptual framework (Donabedian, 1966) to summarize our analysis through structure, processes, and outcomes. Structure included setting, sample size, participant characteristics, and study design. Processes included intervention characteristics, such as follow-up time, modality, and language. Outcomes included intervention results. A meta-analysis was not performed due to the heterogeneity and small sample of studies meeting the inclusion criteria.

Results

Study Selection

The initial search yielded 1,823 articles including 562 duplicates. Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flow diagram (Moher et al., 2009) of the study selection process with reasons for exclusion at each stage. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, the full text of 47 articles were reviewed. Of these, five articles were determined to meet the inclusion criteria and aim of this systematic review. No additional articles were found through ascendency and descendancy searches.

Study Characteristics

All included articles were published in the last ten years, from 2010 to 2019. Table 1 presents detailed study characteristics for the included articles. Of note, two articles reported on different outcome measures from the same intervention (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015). Three articles included interventions developed for Asian Americans (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2017) and two were developed for Hispanics (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010; Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019). In three articles, 99% or all participants were foreign-born (Lee et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2017), and another article reported the majority (61%) of study participants were foreign-born (Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019). One article did not indicate participants’ place of birth but stated that most study participants spoke only Spanish (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010). Study participants mean age ranged from 61 to 73.5 years old and most were female.

The four intervention study designs include one randomized posttest only (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010), two single group, repeated measures (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2017), and one randomized controlled trial (Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019). The interventions were conducted in participants’ homes (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010), a community center (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015), churches (Sun et al., 2017), and both in participants home and over the phone (Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2017; methods were published separately for Nedjat-Haiem et al. 2019). Three articles reported on interactive educational workshops (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2017) and two reported on one-on-one interviews (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010; Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019). All studies involved a follow-up survey to measure outcomes at either two weeks (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010), one month (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015), three months (Sun et al., 2017), and one did not indicate follow-up time (Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019).

Three articles measured the outcomes of 1) intention to complete or completion of advance directive and 2) advance care planning discussions with family, friends, or health care providers (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015; Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2017). Advance directives are formal documentation of end-of-life medical care choices to family and health care providers (NIA, 2018). Advance care planning discussions are conversations on patients’ end-of-life treatment preferences with family, friends, or health care providers (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019). While Hinderer & Lee (2019) and Lee et al. (2015) reported on the same study, Lee et al. measured the additional outcome of increased advanced directive knowledge. Heyman and Gutheil (2010) measured changes in attitudes toward and comfort with end-of-life care planning. Additionally, Sun et al. (2017) measured changes in advance directive related beliefs and attitudes. Table 2 presents a summary of the intervention results.

Quality of Included Articles

Table 3 presents details of the quality appraisal. The quality scores ranged from 14 (fair) to 21 (good), with the majority (n = 4) categorized as fair quality. All authors clearly stated the objective of the study, main outcomes, and characteristics of study participants. Two articles did not provide characteristics of patients lost to follow-up, which limits reporting data on the studies (Lee et al., 2015; Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019). Lee et al. (2015) did not report distributions of principal confounders for the participants, but this was published as a brief report. Hinderer and Lee (2019) reported external validity well (3/3), clearly presenting the population from which participants were recruited. Others utilized convenience sampling, diminishing the study’s representativeness of the sample population (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2017). All authors failed to report internal validity (i.e., bias and confounding) particularly for blinding of either study participants or those measuring the main outcomes. Only Hinderer and Lee (2019) reported a power analysis and sample size calculation before initiating the study.

Study Findings

Advance Directives

Four articles investigated methods to encourage intention to or completion of advance directives (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015; Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2017). In three studies, investigators implemented culturally tailored interventions in Asian Americans (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2017). The study described by both Lee et al. (2015) and Hinderer and Lee (2019) implemented bilingual (Chinese and English), nurse-led seminars at a Chinese community center. This intervention resulted in a 20% increase in advance directive completion one-month post-intervention among participants who did not have an advance directive (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015). Lee et al. reported that increased age was significantly correlated with advance directive completion and 90% of participants stated that the intervention increased their intention to complete an advance directive. Sun et al. (2017) implemented a two-session bilingual (Chinese and Vietnamese), church-based workshop for Chinese and Vietnamese Americans. Intention to complete an advance directive among participants significantly increased from pre-intervention to post-intervention (p < 0.01) (Sun et al., 2017). Advance directive completion was significantly correlated with longer length of time residing in the U.S., greater English proficiency, and having better family support for advance directives (Sun et al., 2017).

Nedjat-Haiem et al. (2019) randomized Hispanics to an advance care planning education program with motivational interviewing or to a control group that received advance care planning education only for the outcome of advance directive completion. Motivational interviewing is a reflexive approach to target readiness to engage in end-of-life discussions, identify barriers to advance care planning, and explore importance and desire to complete an advance directive (Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019). Social workers provided bilingual (English and Spanish) one-on-one motivational interviewing for the intervention group. Controlling for demographic and covariate factors, the intervention resulted in a significant increased likelihood post-intervention of advance directive completion for those in the intervention group compared to the control group (OR = 6.901; p < 0.05) (Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019).

Advance Care Planning Discussions

Four studies measured the intention to have an advance care planning discussion, having an advance care planning discussion, or both (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015; Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2017). A bilingual, nurse-led, culturally sensitive educational workshop in an Asian American sample found that among participants who had not had a previous advance care planning discussion, 35% reported a discussion one-month post-intervention (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015). Furthermore, increased age (p = 0.019) and female gender (p = 0.03) were significantly correlated with having an advance care planning discussion (Lee et al., 2015). Among participants without a prior advance care planning discussion, approximately 36% reported having a discussion one-month post-intervention (Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Lee et al., 2015). In the study by Sun et al. (2017), the proportion of participants with the intention to have an advance care planning discussion significantly increased immediately post-intervention from 70% to 85% (p < 0.01). Three months after the intervention, 25% of participants reported completing an advance care planning discussion (p < 0.01) (Sun et al., 2017). At baseline, those who were married, had a prior intention to complete an advance directive, and reported family support to complete an advance directive were more likely to have an advance care planning discussion post-intervention (Sun et al., 2017).

Notably, Nedjat-Haiem et al. (2019) found that motivational interviewing resulted in decreased intention to engage in advance care planning discussions compared to control group participants that did not receive motivational interviewing. Nedjat-Haiem et al. (2019) acknowledged significant baseline differences in which the intervention group had fewer participants in the 65+ age group compared to controls (p < 0.25).

Attitudes, Knowledge, and Beliefs

Attitudes towards advance care planning were measured in three articles (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010; Hinderer & Lee, 2019; Sun et al., 2017), advance directive knowledge was measured in two articles (Lee et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2017), and beliefs towards advance directives in one article (Sun et al., 2017). Heyman and Gutheil (2010) examined Hispanic attitudes toward and comfort with advance care planning while acknowledging the role of social and cultural beliefs. Control group participants received a standard bilingual (English and Spanish) proxy form with written instructions. There were two intervention groups, and each consisted of one-on-one educational interventions in their homes, led by bilingual (English and Spanish) social workers. Conversation A was considered culturally moderate because it was in Spanish and discussed topics such as advance directives, role of the health care agent, and importance of advance care planning (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010). Conversation B was considered culturally intensive because it included themes identified through focus groups with Hispanic older adults and their family members on topics important for end-of-life discussions (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010). Both intervention groups resulted in improved attitudes towards advance care planning compared to controls (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010). However, Conversation A significantly improved both attitudes toward and comfort with advance care planning, compared to Conversation B and the control group (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010). The authors acknowledge that Conversation B may have been too culturally intensive (e.g., burden, family relationships, spirituality) given the short time of only one session and the sensitive nature of end-of-life discussions (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010).

Hinderer and Lee (2019) reported that attitudes towards advance care planning increased immediately post-intervention and resulted in a significant positive relationship with advance directive completion. Further, Lee et al. (2015) reported that the intervention significantly increased advance directive knowledge from pre-intervention to immediately post-intervention and remained constant at one-month follow-up. Sun et al.’s (2017) church-based, nurse-led intervention resulted in an increased proportion of participants who correctly answered questions on advance directives (p ≤ 0.05). This intervention also increased supportive attitudes and beliefs towards advance care directives (p < 0.01) immediately post-intervention (Sun et al., 2017).

Discussion

Although the body of evidence is limited and study quality mixed, our findings add to the knowledge of methods to overcome advance care planning barriers for racial/ethnic minority older adults. We found that interventions tailored for racial/ethnic minority older adults increased advance care planning use, particularly with advance directives. The included studies implemented culturally tailored interventions, in participants’ primary language, and in familiar locations, which addressed the cultural, language, and access barriers often faced by racial/ethnic minority older adults. Additionally, this paper builds upon literature related to health disparities surrounding end-of-life outcomes, concluding that interventions focused on increasing access and improving communication appears to reduce barriers experienced by racial/ethnic minorities (Mayeda & Ward, 2019).

Notably, the nurse-led interventions were consistently the most effective at increasing advance care planning use (Lee et al, 2015; Hinderer & Lee, 2019). Nurses have a unique role as the most trusted profession to provide person-centered care, particularly for older adults in community settings. Our findings indicate opportunities for gerontological nurses to expand imperatives of cultural, linguistic, and health literate appropriate care when engaging racial/ethnic minority older adults in advance care planning. Indeed, nurses should create spaces of cultural humility when engaging with racial/ethnic minority older adults to appreciate and acknowledge their values and health beliefs (Robinson-Lane & Booker, 2017). Resources should be allocated to facilitate these imperatives by training nurses in cultural competency and understanding how culture influences advance care planning.

Racial/ethnic minorities may experience increased difficulty in understanding advance care planning, particularly for those with limited English proficiency (Kim et al., 2011). Previous studies have found that older adults with limited English proficiency lacked information on their medical condition and expressed difficulty understanding written information provided by doctors (Kim et al., 2011). All included interventions were bilingual and resulted in increased advance care planning engagement, promoting the efficacy of bilingual intervention. However, Sun et al.’s (2017) finding that greater English proficiency was associated with advance directive completion suggests that although bilingual interventions help, nurses should consider that they may not eliminate language barriers in advance care planning when educating patients.

We found that the one-on-one interventions resulted in mixed findings for their intended outcomes. In the included studies with Hispanic older adults, comfort towards advance care planning did not increase (Heyman & Gutheil, 2010) and participants were significantly less ready to engage in advance care planning discussions (Nedjat-Haiem et al., 2019). Motivational interviewing was initially designed to treat addictions, has evolved to guide resistance with changes, such as health behaviors (Schneider et al., 2017), and is encouraged for advance care planning (Pollak et al., 2011). Nedjat-Haiem et al. (2019) acknowledge that motivational interviewing possibly made participants more aware of their lack of readiness to engage in advance care planning discussions. More research is needed to determine if these results are specific to this population of Hispanic older adults or the one-on-one intervention methods.

Our findings suggest that although researchers are implementing interventions to increase advance care planning in racial/ethnic minority older adults, these interventions targeted Asian and Hispanic communities. This is important as Asian and Hispanics are the fastest growing U.S. immigrant populations (Radford, 2019). However, more research is needed to address the needs of other U.S. racial/ethnic minority groups. For example, African American older adults experience significant health disparities at end-of-life (Burgio et al., 2016); yet, no advance care planning interventions designed for or implemented among this population were identified. Future advance care planning interventions in racial/ethnic minority older adults should include samples that reflect the diversity among U.S. racial/ethnic minority older adults.

Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review on community-based interventions to increase advance care planning in racial/ethnic minority older adults. This is important given the growing population of racial/ethnic minority older adults and their low advance care planning engagement. Our systematic review was guided by PRISMA guidelines (Moher et al., 2009), including a comprehensive review of existing literature, and quality appraisal. There are also limitations to this paper. First, it is possible that there were studies that met the inclusion criteria but were not identified. However, this is unlikely given the robust search methods conducted by the reviewers. Second, only articles published in English and regarding U.S. populations were included. This may limit the external validity of our review and future research should include international comparison for racial groups. Third, grey literature was excluded but may have provided new knowledge on research in this area that has not yet been peer-reviewed. Lastly, due to the small sample of articles included, findings and implications need further evidence. However, this review highlights the need for interventions to engage racial/ethnic minority older adults in advance care planning.

Conclusion

In conclusion, studies included in this review indicate that interventions tailored for racial/ethnic minority older adults increased advance care planning use, particularly with advance directives, consistent with previous evidence in wider groups (Brinkman-Stoppelenburg et al., 2014; IOM, 1997, 2015). Findings from this systematic review provide preliminary evidence for nurse-led, community-based, culturally tailored, interventions to increase advance care planning use among racial/ethnic minorities. Efforts should be increased to train gerontological nurses in cultural competency and more high-quality rigorous research is needed that includes additional racial/ethnic minority groups such as African Americans.
