LI-RADS Treatment Response Algorithm for detecting incomplete necrosis in hepatocellular carcinoma after Locoregional treatment: A Systematic review and meta-analysis using individual patient data

Abstract

Purpose:

To perform a systematic review and meta-analysis using individual patient data to investigate the diagnostic performance of Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS) Treatment Response (TR) algorithm for detecting incomplete necrosis on pathology.

Methods:

PubMed and EMBASE were searched from Jan 1, 2017 until October 14, 2020. Studies reporting diagnostic accuracy of LI-RADS TR algorithm on CT or MRI for detecting incomplete necrosis on pathology as a reference standard were included. Sensitivity and specificity were pooled using random-effects model. Subgroup analyses were performed for LRT type, and imaging modality.

Results:

Six studies (393 patients, 534 lesions) were included. Pooled sensitivity was 0.56 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.43–0.69) and specificity was 0.91 (95%CI 0.84–0.96). Pooled sensitivity was highest using arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE) (0.67 [95%CI 0.51–0.81]), followed by washout (0.43 [95%CI 0.26–0.62]) and enhancement similar to pretreatment (0.24 [95%CI 0.15–0.36]). Among lesions with incomplete necrosis, 2% (95%CI 0.00–0.05) manifested as washout but no APHE; 0% (95% CI 0.00–0.02) as enhancement similar to pretreatment without both APHE and washout. Pooled sensitivity was lower after ablation than embolization (0.42 [95%CI, 0.28–0.57] vs. 0.65 [95%CI, 0.53–0.77], p=0.033). MRI and CT were comparable (p=0.783 and 0.290 for sensitivity and specificity).

Conclusions:

LI-RADS TR algorithm shows moderate sensitivity and high specificity for detecting incomplete necrosis after LRT. APHE is the dominant criterion, a washout contributes to small but meaningful extent, while the contribution of enhancement similar to pretreatment may be negligible. LRT type may affect performance of the algorithm.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common cancer and is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Loco-regional therapy (LRT) for HCC has been widely applied not only for the definitive treatment of early-stage HCC [2, 3], but also for neoadjuvant therapy to downsize tumor burden or bridge therapy to liver transplantation [4]. Given its increased usage for HCC, treatment response assessment after LRT has become crucial as it guides further management. The modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) remains the standard criterion for evaluation of HCC response to LRT, reporting overall response at the patient level, and it hinges on the finding of tumor viability through visual assessment of arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE) in the treated area [5]. The Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS) Treatment Response (TR) Algorithm was introduced in 2017 to standardize interpretation and reporting of LRT-treated HCC, assessing treatment response at each lesion level [6, 7].

The LI-RADS TR algorithm expands on mRECIST by relying not only on APHE, but also on the imaging features of “washout” and “enhancement similar to pretreatment” to improve detection of viable HCC after LRT. According to the LI-RADS TR algorithm, treated HCC can be categorized as TR Viable, TR Equivocal, or TR Nonviable, based on the presence of the above features. These categories communicate an estimated likelihood of tumor viability, and LI-RADS TR algorithm was designed to facilitate consistent reporting and multidisciplinary discussion for consensus management.

Several studies evaluating the diagnostic performance of LI-RADS TR algorithm have been published [8–13]. However, the diagnostic performance of LI-RADS TR algorithm requires systematic validation for several reasons. First, the contribution of imaging features of the TR Viable category other than APHE is not well established. Additionally, it is unknown whether LI-RADS TR algorithm works similarly across different LRTs. Lastly, the performance of LI-RADS TR algorithm when using magnetic resonance image (MRI) vs. computed tomography (CT) is yet to be compared. To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis using individual patient data (IPD) from all published studies assessing the diagnostic performance of LIRADS TR algorithm.

Materials and methods

Literature search

This meta-analysis was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis of Diagnostic Test Accuracy (PRISMA-DTA) guidelines [14]. This study used publicly published data and was exempt from institutional review board oversight. PubMed and EMBASE databases were searched until October 14, 2020, using a search query based on keywords of “Liver”, “LI-RADS”, “CT” and “MRI” as shown in the supplementary table. The references of the retrieved articles were screened to find additional eligible studies.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they fulfilled the following patient, index test, comparison, outcomes, and study design (PICOS) criteria: (1) patients (P) who underwent LRT for HCC; (2) LIRADS TR algorithm assessed on CT or MRI was used as the index test (I); (3) pathological results after hepatic resection or transplantation were used as the reference standard or comparator (C); (4) outcome (O) was defined as the sensitivity and specificity for detecting incomplete necrosis; and (5) we allowed any type of prospective or retrospective cohort studies (S).

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria were (1) studies with fewer than ten patients; (2) other publication types (i.e., case reports or reviews); (3) studies without pathologic assessment for determining tumor viability after LRT (i.e., viability was determined using additional imaging or follow-up such as cone-beam CT angiography; and (4) studies focusing on different topics. (i.e., inter-reader agreement or survival analysis stratified to TR categories). For studies with overlapping patients, we used only non-overlapping patients from the corresponding studies after verification by the authors of the original studies.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data from original articles regarding patient, study, and MRI or CT characteristics were retrieved using a standardized form. We requested anonymized IPD from the authors of the included studies for the purpose of this meta-analysis. When the entire IPD could not be provided, we requested specific subgroup-level population data tailored to our outcomes. Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies [15]. Two reviewers (T.H.K. and S.W) independently performed the literature search, study selection, data extraction and quality assessment, followed by discussion with a third reviewer (R.K.G.D.) in case of disagreement.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data from the included studies were reconstructed in 2 * 2 tables (true positive, false negative, false positive, and true negative). When there was more than one result in a study owing to assessment by multiple readers, we used the average value from 2 * 2 tables by each reader. The primary outcome was the diagnostic performance in terms of the summary sensitivity and specificity of the LI-RADS TR algorithm when using only the “TR Viable” category for detecting incomplete necrosis on pathology. Secondary outcomes were as follows: (1) the diagnostic performance of “TR Viable” and “TR Equivocal” combined as a single category to detect incomplete necrosis on pathology, (2) the diagnostic performance of each of the imaging features of APHE, washout, and enhancement similar to pretreatment, (3) lesions with incomplete necrosis that were detected with (i) washout without APHE, (ii) enhancement similar to pretreatment without APHE, or (iii) enhancement similar to pretreatment with neither APHE nor washout, (4) diagnostic performance of the LIRADS TR algorithm stratified to (i) LRT type (ablation vs. embolization), (ii) surgery type (hepatic resection vs. transplantation), (iii) imaging modality (MRI vs. CT), and, (iv) MRI contrast agent type (extracellular agent (ECA) vs. hepatobiliary agent (HBA)).

The pooled sensitivity and specificity with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated using the bivariate and hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic (HSROC) models [16]. HSROC curves with 95% confidence region and prediction region were plotted [17]. The pooled proportions were calculated using a random-effects model, with generation of forest plots and 95% CIs. We planned to use the Deeks’ funnel plot and Deeks’ asymmetry test for assessing publication bias if there were more than 10 included studies [18, 19].

Heterogeneity was evaluated using several methods. First, both Cochran’s Q test with p <0.1 suggesting statistical significance [20] and inconsistency index (I2) using the following criteria [21]: 0–40%, insignificant heterogeneity; 30–60%, moderate heterogeneity; 50–90%, substantial heterogeneity; and 75–100%, considerable heterogeneity. In addition, we tested for the presence of a “threshold effect”, a positive correlation between sensitivity and false positive rate, by visually analysis of the coupled forests and correlation analysis, where Spearman correlation coefficient greater than 0.6 was considered positive [22].

The ‘midas’ module in Stata 13.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) and ‘mada’ and ‘meta’ package in R software version 3.6.3. (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) were utilized for statistical analyses with p <0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Literature search

3.1.

The initial systematic search found 538 studies. After removal of 185 duplicates, 353 articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Of these, 33 potentially eligible studies were reviewed and 27 studies were excluded based on full-text review. Ultimately, 6 studies assessing the diagnostic performance of LI-RADS TR algorithm for detecting incomplete necrosis were included [8–13]. The study selection process is shown in Figure 1.

Characteristics of included studies

3.2.

The patient and study characteristics are described in Table 1. Among treated lesions, which ranged from 53 to 206 lesions per study, the proportion of lesions with incomplete necrosis on pathology ranged from 39% to 78%. Using IPD, we excluded (i) partially overlapping patients between two studies [10, 11], and (ii) patients without pathology as reference standard [10], and separated data for lesions evaluated only on CT in two studies [12, 13]. For lesions which were evaluated both on MRI and CT in two studies (132 and 81 lesions in Park et al [11] and Seo et al [12], respectively), the results of MRI were representatively used for overall pooling. Therefore, a total of 393 patients with 534 lesions were analyzed in our meta-analysis (379 lesions on MRI and 368 lesions on CT). Patients were treated with ablation in two studies [8, 9], embolization in one [13], and various LRTs in the other three studies [10–12]. All six studies used MRI and three also included CT [11–13]. The MRI and CT characteristics are summarized in Table 2. HBA was used in whole or in part in five studies [9–13], whereas one study exclusively used ECA [8].

Quality assessment

3.3.

The quality of included studies was considered excellent (Figure 2). Regarding patient selection, high risk of bias was present in one study [12] as patients with more than 5 hepatic nodules were excluded whereas other studies either picked representative nodules [10, 11] or assessed all nodules assessable on imaging [8, 9, 13]. For the index test domain, one study [9] showed high risk of bias as it allowed readers to review multiple prior post-treatment MRIs when applying the LI-RADS TR algorithm, and therefore the TR categorization was performed with consideration of temporal changes in addition to the defined imaging criteria in the TR algorithm. All other studies included review of pre-treatment imaging but no other prior examinations. Nevertheless, this was considered not to be a significant source of concern for applicability as in real clinical practice, temporal changes are generally taken into account when assessing treatment response. Regarding the reference standard domain, one study [12] was considered to have high risk of bias as histopathological assessment was performed with correlation to preoperative imaging. Regarding the index test domain and flow and timing domain, there were no concerns for risk of bias.

Overall diagnostic performance of the LI-RADS TR algorithm for detecting incomplete necrosis.

3.4.

The sensitivity and specificity of the individual studies (n = 6) that reported diagnostic performance of the TR Viable category for detecting incomplete necrosis ranged from 0.30 to 0.79 and from 0.83 to 1.00, respectively. The coupled forest plot of the sensitivity and specificity is shown in figure 3. The Q-test indicated the presence of heterogeneity (Q = 12.379, p = 0.001) with considerable heterogeneity in sensitivity (I2 = 80%) but not in specificity (I2 = 28%). The possibility of a threshold effect was suggested on visual assessment of the coupled forest plots of sensitivity and specificity with corresponding correlation coefficient of 0.839 (95% CI 0.087–0.982). The pooled sensitivity for the TR Viable category for detecting incomplete necrosis was 0.56 (95% CI 0.43–0.69) with a specificity of 0.91 (95% CI 0.84–0.96). The area under the HSROC curve was 0.86 (95 % CI 0.82–0.88) (Figure 4). When combining the TR Viable and Equivocal categories for detecting incomplete necrosis on pathology, the pooled sensitivity was 0.73 (95% CI 0.60–0.84) with a specificity of 0.82 (95% CI 0.74–0.88, supplementary figure 1). As only six studies were included, publication bias could not be assessed.

Diagnostic performance of each imaging feature and incremental value of findings other than APHE.

3.5.

The pooled sensitivity and specificity for each imaging feature is summarized in Table 3. Additional data for the diagnostic performance for each imaging feature including APHE and washout was available in five studies [8, 10–13], and for similar to pretreatment in four studies [8, 11–13]. The pooled sensitivity for detection of incomplete necrosis was substantially higher using APHE (0.67 [95% CI 0.51–0.81]) than washout (0.43 [95% CI 0.26–0.62]) and enhancement similar to pretreatment (0.24 [95% CI 0.15–0.36]).

Among lesions with incomplete necrosis, the pooled proportion of lesions showing washout without APHE was 2% (95% CI 0.01–0.05), and the proportion of those with enhancement similar to pretreatment without APHE was 1% (95% CI 0.00–0.04) (Figure 5A, 5B). The proportion of incomplete necrosis detected by the presence of enhancement similar to pretreatment with neither APHE nor washout was 0% (95% CI, 0.00–0.02) (Figure 5C).

Diagnostic performance stratified by LRT type, surgery type, imaging modality, and MRI contrast agent type.

3.6.

Table 4 summarizes the subgroup analysis for the diagnostic test accuracy of the TR Viable category stratified by LRT type, surgery type, imaging modality, and MRI contrast agent type. The number of evaluated lesions in the subgroup analysis are as follows: ablation (164 lesions) vs. embolization (296 lesions), hepatic resection (93 lesions) vs. transplantation (441 lesions), MRI (379 lesions) vs. CT (368 lesions) and ECA (85 lesions) vs. HBA (294 lesions), The pooled sensitivity was significantly lower in lesions after ablation (0.42 [95% CI, 0.28–0.57]) compared to those after embolization (0.65 [95% CI, 0.53–0.77], p = 0.033). Regarding the surgery type, lesions after transplantation showed significantly lower sensitivity compared to those after hepatic resection (0.52 [95% CI, 0.42–62] vs. 0.81 [95% CI, 0.68–0.93], p = 0.008). MRI and CT were comparable in terms of their sensitivity (0.60 [95% CI 0.46–0.74] vs 0.56 [95% CI 0.37–0.75]; p = 0.783) and specificity (0.90 [95% CI 0.84–0.97] vs 0.93 [95% CI 0.87–1.00]; p = 0.290). Both ECA and HBA showed similar diagnostic performances (sensitivity: 0.64 [95% CI 0.40–0.88] vs 0.64 [95% CI 0.47–0.81]; p = 0.818, specificity: 0.85 [95% CI 0.68–1.00] vs 0.92 [95% CI 0.84–1.00]; p = 0.511).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we used IPD to assess the diagnostic performance of the LI-RADS TR algorithm on CT or MRI in LRT-treated HCCs for detecting incomplete necrosis on pathology. The LI-RADS TR algorithm showed moderate sensitivity (0.56 [95% CI 0.43–0.69]) and high specificity (0.91 [95% CI 0.84–0.96]) for detecting incomplete necrosis. As the LI-RADS TR algorithm is designed to evaluate HCC after LRT at the lesion level, high specificity has benefit in preventing unnecessary re-treatment or unjustified exclusion from liver transplantation. Meanwhile, moderate sensitivity of the LI-RADS TR algorithm may reflect that imaging has inherent limited capability to detect microscopic residual disease on pathology [23]. In fact, the summary diagnostic performance of the LI-RADS TR algorithm is slightly lower than that of LR-5 category in LI-RADS for the diagnosis of HCC (sensitivity: 0.70 [95% CI 0.61–0.78] and specificity: 0.91 [95% CI 0.89–0.93]) [24]; this is intuitively plausible considering the multitude of etiologies for decreased sensitivity in detecting viable tumor, including post-treatment changes of the tumor and surrounding parenchyma and smaller residual tumor size. Although we were unable to evaluate the diagnostic performance of LIRADS TR algorithm stratified to the viable tumor size, we speculate that the sensitivity of LIRADS TR algorithm will be substantially greater for detecting macroscopic viable tumors compared with microscopic ones [25]. Therefore, future studies evaluating the LIRADS TR algorithm should be done with stratification to the viable tumor size on histopathology.

Among imaging features described in the TR Viable category, we found APHE to be the dominant feature for detecting incomplete necrosis demonstrating the highest sensitivity of 0.67. Regarding the incremental value of the other two features of the TR Viable category that distinguishes it from mRECIST for detecting incomplete necrosis, using washout without APHE additionally identified 2%. This is in agreement with a previous study before the establishment of the LI-RADS TR algorithm which found that 4% of viable HCCs after various LRTs showed washout without APHE on MRI [25]. In contrast, the incremental value of enhancement similar to pretreatment was minimal, if not negligible. Using this feature only additionally detected 1% of the lesions with incomplete necrosis, nearly none of them (0% [95% CI 0.00–0.02]) being identified without having concurrent washout. This raises important implications for future revisions of the LI-RADS TR algorithm, which is intended to be a “dynamic document” expected to be refined with accumulation of scientific evidence. Based on our results, we speculate that there is sufficient rationale for washout to be included as one of the features for TR Viable category. However, with nearly no additional viable tumor being detected by the sole use of enhancement similar to pretreatment, this feature could be considered for removal from the TR Viable category.

Interestingly, higher sensitivity was noted with APHE when compared to TR Viable category for incomplete necrosis detection (0.67 vs. 0.56). Studies have shown that some post-treatment observations which demonstrate APHE but not specific enough to be considered as viable neoplasm, are often downgraded and assigned either TR Equivocal or TR Nonviable category, which reduces the sensitivity of the TR Viable category when there is indeed incomplete necrosis on pathology [10, 11]. The TR Equivocal category was designed for equivocal imaging findings seen after LRT, as there is certainly a grey zone in real clinical practice when a radiologist may have a difficulty interpreting a treated lesion as either viable or nonviable [26]. To address these equivocal cases, there have been attempts to improve prediction of HCC recurrence after LRT using “ancillary features” on T2-weighted image, hepatobiliary phase (HBP), and diffusion weighted image [27–29]. Early studies show that using ancillary features improve sensitivity while maintaining high specificity, however this should be further validated in prospective studies [10, 11]. Regarding the sensitivity of washout, as five of six studies used HBA on MRI in whole or in part and as it is recognized that HBA shows lower depiction of washout on portal venous phase compared with ECA [30], possible improvement for the sensitivity of washout may be expected by either extending washout on HBA to the transitional or HBP [31], or including ECA only.

In the subgroup analysis, the LI-RADS TR algorithm showed lower sensitivity after ablation compared with embolization. As recurrent or residual tumor is commonly located along the periphery of an ablation zone [23], we speculate that a rim of enhancement, an expected post-treatment enhancement pattern after ablation, may obscure small areas of APHE representing viable tumor. Furthermore, ablation is usually recommended for smaller HCCs (< 3cm), whereas embolization is reserved for larger or unresectable cases [2, 32]. Therefore, different baseline tumor characteristics and the resulting efficacy in tumor control between these LRTs may have also influenced the performances of LI-RADS TR algorithm. Based on the surgery type, lesions after transplantation showed significantly lower sensitivity compared to those after hepatic resection (0.52 vs. 0.81). Although this finding may be related to other factors such as extent of HCC or degree of liver function that could affect which surgery type the patient could have received, we assume that as patients undergoing transplantation are more likely to have advanced liver cirrhosis than those undergoing hepatic resection, treatment response assessment in cirrhotic background is expected to be more challenging [33]. Indeed, there was variability in the proportion of patients with Child-Pugh score B or C in the included studies; 21.7% in a study with patients undergoing either hepatic resection or transplantation [11], and 54% in a study with patients undergoing transplantation only [12]. Furthermore, higher performance for hepatic resection can also be attributed to the fact that two studies which included patients with hepatic resection had majority of patients with hepatitis B virus infection [10, 11], which promotes HCC development in the absence of cirrhosis [34]. Lastly, regarding imaging modality, MRI and CT showed comparable performance for incomplete necrosis detection. As only three studies were available for analyzing the diagnostic performance of CT [11–13], further studies with larger number of participants with head-to-head comparison between these two imaging modalities is warranted.

Our study has a few limitations. First, this meta-analysis included a relatively small number of studies (n = 6), all performed retrospectively. However, it is noteworthy that this meta-analysis incorporates all of the current available evidence which comprehensively evaluates LI-RADS TR algorithm using pathology as a reference standard. Furthermore, by gathering IPD from each of the included studies, we were able to find meaningful results including the value of each TR Viable imaging feature and subgroup analyses. Second, although we managed to find difference in performance of LI-RADS TR algorithm between ablation and embolization, we were unable to pool diagnostic performances for each specific LRT modality (i.e., radiofrequency ablation (RFA), microwave ablation, transarterial embolization or transarterial chemoembolization (TACE)) and for the other less commonly performed LRTs (i.e., drug eluting bead TACE, transarterial radioembolization, or combined RFA-TACE) due to small number of patients that underwent these procedures. Future studies evaluating the LI-RADS TR algorithm on each specific LRT modality are warranted. Furthermore, even when identical LRT types are used, drug dosage or applied energy can be different per institutional preferences, which could not be assessed as part of this meta-analysis. Similarly, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), which has become an additional option in the non-surgical management of HCCs with good local control rates, was not evaluated in our current meta-analysis. Treatment response assessment after SBRT is unique compared to other LRTs in that APHE is known to persist post-treatment, even up to 3 years [35], in effectively treated HCCs, and therefore, could be considered part of ‘expected’ post-treatment enhancement pattern [36–38]. Third, there is a possible selection bias in current IPD meta-analysis as we only included patients with histopathologic results after hepatic resection or transplantation and these are not common procedures in majorities of patients who receive LRT. Furthermore, as treated lesions with wide range (from 0% to 99%) of tumor necrosis were uniformly categorized into pathologically viable tumor, the performances of LIRADS-TR algorithm and clinical significance according to the tumor necrosis percentage may still require further investigations. Nevertheless, using histopathology obtained after resection and transplantation can be considered the most robust reference standard to answer the clinical question of our meta-analysis. In addition, although applying such strict inclusion criteria may raise a concern for the exclusion of many patients, we only had to exclude a portion of patients in one of the included studies [10] which is the only study on LI-RADS TR algorithm published up to now that included patients that had both histological diagnosis and clinical imaging-based diagnosis after LRT as the reference standard. This may implicate that despite applying such inclusion criteria, it does not deviate from the majority of the evidence that has been published. Lastly, there were considerable heterogeneity in the sensitivity (I2 = 80%), which warrants caution to generalize the results of the current meta-analysis. Although we observed a substantial difference between LRT type and surgery type at subgroup analyses, other factors including threshold effect among included studies, variation in the size of viable tumors and differences in patient population may also have contributed to this heterogeneity.

In conclusion, the LI-RADS TR algorithm shows moderate sensitivity and high specificity for detecting incomplete necrosis after LRT. APHE is the dominant criterion and washout may contribute to a small but meaningful extent for detecting additional viable lesions, whereas enhancement similar to pretreatment may be negligible. Differences in expected post-treatment appearances after different LRTs or surgery types may affect the performance of LI-RADS TR algorithm.
