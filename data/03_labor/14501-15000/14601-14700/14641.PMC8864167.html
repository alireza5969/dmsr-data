Pediatric Obesity Prevention and Treatment Among Hispanics: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Abstract

Introduction:

Rates of pediatric obesity in the U.S. are highest among Hispanics. There is no existing meta-analysis of the effects of obesity interventions among Hispanic youth. This systematic review and meta-analysis assesses the effects of obesity prevention and treatment interventions on Hispanic youth’s weight status and lifestyle behaviors.

Methods:

PubMed, PsycInfo, and Scopus were searched between January 1, 2000 and October 30, 2020. Interventions with ≥50% Hispanic youth aged 0–18 years were included. Using a weighted inverse variance procedure, fixed effects and random effects models were run for an overall effect size based on the Qtotal test statistic. Hedges’ g was calculated for outcomes of interest between baseline and post-intervention separately for studies with multiple versus single conditions. Continuous and categorical moderators were also examined.

Results:

A total of 1,103 articles were screened, of which 117 were included in the narrative synthesis and 105 in the meta-analysis (n=49,276 youth). The overall effects for RCT/quasi-experimental studies on BMI status (g= −0.15, SE=0.03, 95% CI= −0.20, −0.10), waist circumference (g= −0.15, SE=0.10, 95% CI= −0.35, −0.05), physical activity (g=0.12, SE=0.05, 95% CI=0.03, 0.22), fruit and vegetable (g=0.08, SE=0.02, 95% CI=0.03, 0.12), and sugar-sweetened beverage intake (g= −0.07, SE= 0.03, 95% CI= −0.13, −0.01) were small. Intervention effects varied by participant developmental stage, SES, study setting, and lifestyle behavior target.

Discussion:

Beyond developing more impactful interventions to address obesity among Hispanic youth, findings highlight the need for targeted policies and more easily disseminable interventions that can spread small effects across a population for maximal public health impact.

INTRODUCTION

Nearly 20% of U.S. youth are obese, with higher rates among ethnic minority and lower-SES groups.1 Hispanic youth have the highest prevalence (26%) relative to non-Hispanic White (14%), Black (22%), and Asian youth (11%), with 9% severely obese.1 Given estimates that more than a quarter of the U.S. population will be Hispanic by 2060,2 effectively addressing pediatric obesity among Hispanics is critical to the future health of the nation.

There is currently no published meta-analytic study that provides an overall quantitative summary of the effects of obesity interventions on Hispanic youth, making it difficult to ascertain how well existing interventions are working to improve their weight status and associated lifestyle behaviors (e.g., physical activity [PA], sedentary behavior, diet). Throughout the last decade, however, there have been 10 systematic reviews examining obesity prevention and treatment among Hispanic youth in the U.S.3-12 These reviews have reported mixed results4,6 or only individual study effect sizes suggesting small-to-moderate intervention effects on youth weight status.3,5,6 These reviews concluded that interventions with significant findings were generally grounded in theory,3,8 and had older children, parental involvement,3,5,8,9,11 multiple settings,9 longer implementation periods,3,10 and higher ratings of study quality compared with other studies.6 Notably, each of the aforementioned reviews included only 4–24 interventions; was limited to examining studies targeted at specific age ranges (e.g., only preschool),11 intervention designs (e.g., only RCTs), settings (e.g., only school-based), or types (e.g., treatment only); and focused almost exclusively on weight status, rather than lifestyle behaviors.

This study aims to evaluate the state of the pediatric obesity intervention literature among Hispanic youth by: (1) offering a quantitative perspective through meta-analysis and including overall and moderator analyses, and (2) providing a more comprehensive overview of the field by broadening the inclusion criteria, specifically by various research designs and settings, including both prevention and treatment interventions, reporting effect sizes for lifestyle behaviors in addition to weight status variables, and including interventions that target youth across development. The goal is to provide a central resource for intervention scientists working towards preventing and reducing pediatric obesity among Hispanic youth by answering the following research questions:

  1. How well do obesity prevention and treatment interventions for Hispanic youth aged 0–18 years work in terms of changing their weight status, PA, sedentary behavior/screen time, and diet (fruits, vegetables, sugar-sweetened beverages)?

  2. How do intervention effects vary as a function of participant (i.e., developmental stage, age, sex, weight status, percentage Hispanic, SES), intervention (i.e., theoretical framework, setting, lifestyle behavior targets, degree of parental involvement, cultural targeting, duration), and study characteristics (i.e., design, timepoint of post-intervention assessment, risk of bias)?

METHODS

Search Strategy and Eligibility Criteria

Adhering to PRISMA guidelines,13 an electronic search for peer-reviewed articles published January 1, 2000–October 30, 2020 was conducted in PubMed, PsycInfo, and Scopus. Search dates were based on previous reviews that found most pediatric obesity–related interventions involving Hispanics6 or the family system14 were published after 2000. The search strategy (Appendix Table 1) included the index and keywords: Hispanic/Latino, overweight/obesity, health promotion, treat/prevent, and intervention. The criteria for inclusion were: (1) any pediatric obesity prevention or treatment intervention, trial, or program, including RCTs and nonrandomized trials (with and without comparison conditions: “non-randomized controlled studies” and “pre–post studies,” respectively), at any phase of research (e.g., pilot, efficacy); (2) sample included ≥50% of participants self-identifying as Hispanic5,6; (3) sample included participants aged 0–18 years; (4) outcomes of interest included weight status, BMI, BMI-z, BMI percentile, PA, sedentary behavior, dietary intake; (5) intervention was conducted in the U.S.; and (6) article was written in English and published in a peer-reviewed journal so as to provide a best-evidence synthesis.15 EndNote Desktop, version X8 and EndNote Web were used to access all search results after removing duplicates. Existing reviews’ reference lists were handsearched as were clinicaltrials.gov and Google Scholar for main outcomes articles of protocol/baseline papers identified by the search.

Study Selection and Coding of Data

The study flow diagram (Figure 1) shows the process of article selection. During this process, an author (MK) randomly selected 10% of references to review and had a high percentage agreement regarding study eligibility during Phases 1 (93.4%) and 2 (98.3%). All authors independently extracted information including participant and intervention characteristics, outcomes assessed, and effect size data using a Microsoft Excel data extraction sheet that was pilot tested. Two authors independently conducted a risk of bias assessment using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool.16 Discrepancies were resolved by discussion with the lead authors. Studies were also categorized based on participant, intervention, and study characteristics to facilitate moderator analyses. Continuous moderators were mean age, percentage female, and percentage Hispanic. Categorical moderators were: developmental stage (1, infancy [birth–1 year]; 2, early childhood [2–5 years]; 3, childhood [6–11 years], 4, early-to-middle adolescence [12–15 years], 5, late adolescence [16–18 years]); baseline weight status of sample (1, normal weight/mixed weight; 2, overweight/obese); SES (1, low income; 2, not low income); theoretical framework (1, no theory reported; 2, theory reported); primary study setting (1, individual; 2, family; 3, school; 4, community; 5, primary care); lifestyle behavior targets (1, PA/sedentary behavior only; 2, diet only; 3, multicomponent; 4, medical; 5, other); parental involvement (1, no involvement or passive/low or parents attended <50% of sessions; 2, active/high [parents attended ≥50% of sessions]); cultural targeting strategies17 (1, none or linguistic strategies only; 2, linguistic strategies with constituent-involving or sociocultural strategies); intervention duration (1, <6 months, 2, 6–12 months; 3, ≥12months); study design (1, pre–post, 2, RCT; 3, non-randomized controlled study); timepoint of post-intervention assessment (from ≤1 week through 4 years); and overall risk of bias16 (1, low; 2, high; 3, unclear). Consistent with a previous systematic review,5 baseline weight status was used as a proxy for prevention versus treatment.5

Effect Sizes and Associated SEs

The continuous, dependent variables of interest were youth outcomes of BMI status, waist circumference, body fat percentage, and lifestyle behaviors associated with obesity: PA, sedentary behavior (i.e., screen time), and diet (i.e., fruit/vegetable intake, sugar-sweetened beverages). Using reported means and SDs, Cohen’s d was calculated to quantify the standardized mean difference of the outcome between intervention and control. When other test statistics (e.g., t or F statistics, ORs) were reported, other formulas were used to convert effect sizes to a standardized mean difference Cohen’s d.18 Three methods for calculating the effect sizes were used based on study design: pretest–post-test control group design,19 post-test control group design,20 or single group pretest–post-test design.21 The standardized mean difference for both the treatment and control groups was calculated by using the unbiased estimate of Hedges’ g.22 Because some of the studies had small sample sizes that could introduce small sample bias, authors corrected for computed effect sizes. Overall, Hedges’ g was deemed an appropriate effect size for the current study. When possible, sample size was based on the analytic sample (versus baseline). Unbiased effect sizes were calculated from Cohen’s d and multiplied by a correction factor of J, g = J × d.23 The same correction factor was applied to the effect sizes’ associated SDs and SEs. Cohen’s d and J correction factor were calculated using the formulas outlined in the Appendix.

Some studies reported multiple measures of variables (i.e., separate fruits and vegetables versus a single outcome), which would violate the assumption of independence.24 Prior to analyses, the 2 effect sizes were averaged to handle dependency issues, so each sample would contribute only one independent effect size per outcome.25 Studies that reported multiple time points contributed a single effect size per outcome. Specifically, the most immediate time point following post-intervention was selected unless reported statistics could not be extracted, then other subsequent time points were used. Using post-intervention data allowed for the analytical sample size to be maximized. Effect sizes were classified as: small (0–0.2), medium (0.21–0.50), and large (≥0.80).26 Detected outliers were removed from analyses to increase the precision of estimated effects.27 Study effect sizes were displayed visually using Forest plots.

Similar to a prior meta-analysis,28 overall effects and moderator analyses were run on outcomes separately for effect sizes from RCT/non-randomized controlled studies and those from pre–post studies so that the results could inform future studies’ power calculations and sample size estimations on multiple outcomes of interest for different study designs. For studies that did not include sufficient information to calculate effect sizes, study authors were contacted for details.

Statistical Analyses

The metafor package in RStudio, version 0.99.903 was used for all analyses. An inverse-variance weighted mean effect was calculated.29-31 If the Qtotal statistic was significant (indicating effects were from >1 population), a random effects model was used to account for the significant amount of heterogeneity among effect sizes, computed using the restricted maximum likelihood estimation method. A series of moderator analyses were conducted, each with 1 variable (which for categorical moderators, is similar to a weighted ANOVA model with random effects), and a mixed effects model was used if within-group variations were significant.32

RESULTS

A total of 117 studies were included in this narrative synthesis (Appendix Tables 2-3, Appendix References), 105 of which were included in the meta-analysis with 251 effect sizes. Studies included 49,276 youth, with 51% female, 76% Hispanic, and a mean age of 8.1 (SD=4.0) years. Forty-eight percent of studies included youth in the normal or mixed weight status at baseline, and 73% included low-income samples. Studies were conducted primarily in the family (43%) and school settings (30%), with fewer at the community (14%), primary care (11%), and individual levels (2%). Most were published after 2013 (75%) and took place during childhood (47%); remaining studies took place in early childhood (27%), early-to-middle adolescence (19%), late adolescence (4%), and infancy (4%). There were 71 RCTs/non-randomized controlled studies and 34 single-group pre–post studies. Of the 251 total effect sizes, there were 103 BMI status (67 RCT/quasi, 36 pre–post), 24 waist circumference (14 RCT/quasi, 6 pre–post), 10 body fat percentage (6 RCT/quasi, 4 pre–post), 36 PA (23 RCT/quasi, 13 pre–post), 30 sedentary behavior (21 RCT/quasi, 9 pre–post), 29 fruit and vegetable (16 RCT/quasi, 13 pre–post), and 19 sugar-sweetened beverage effect sizes (13 RCT/quasi, 6 pre–post). The reporting of results is focused on RCT/non-randomized controlled studies; findings from single-group pre–post studies are summarized in the Appendix.

Publication Bias

A funnel plot was created for a visual inspection of publication bias (Appendix Figure 1) and an Egger’s regression test of an intercept was conducted. The effect sizes were significant (z= −4.81, p<0.0001), suggesting some evidence of publication bias. Copas selection analyses using the R package copas were run to explore the potential impact of publication bias via sensitivity analysis.33,34 Overall effect size estimates that take potential publication bias into account are provided in Appendix Table 4.

BMI Status

Sixty-seven studies with RCT/non-randomized controlled designs reported extractable information for BMI status. There was a significant amount of heterogeneity between studies between studies (Qtotal [66]=194.23, p<0.0001; I2=66%). Under the random effects model, the overall estimated intervention effect was −0.15 (SE=0.03, 95% CI= −0.20, −0.10), indicating that interventions produced slight but significant reductions in BMI status compared with controls (Figure 2).

Moderator analyses indicated that study design, developmental stage, SES, theory, study setting, lifestyle behavior targets, and degree of parental involvement significantly explained variation in effect sizes (Table 1). Both RCTs (g= −0.15, SE=0.03, 95% CI= −0.21, −0.08) and non-randomized controlled studies (g= −0.15, SE=0.05, 95% CI= −0.25, −0.05) had small effects on youth BMI status. Post-hoc Tukey’s analysis revealed that interventions conducted during infancy (g= −0.45, SE=0.08, 95% CI= −0.62, −0.29) and early-to-middle adolescence (g= −0.27, SE=0.06, 95% CI= −0.38, −0.15) showed significantly larger effects than those conducted in early childhood (g= −0.13, SE=0.04, 95% CI= −0.21, −0.05), late adolescence (g= −0.09, SE=0.21, 95% CI= −0.49, 0.32), and childhood (g= −0.03, SE=0.04, 95% CI= −0.11, 0.04). Studies involving higher-income youth had moderate effects on weight reduction (g= −0.23, SE=0.06, 95% CI= −0.35, −0.12), and those with lower-income youth had small effects (g= −0.12, SE=0.03, 95% CI= −0.18, −0.07). Theoretically-based interventions (g= −0.14, SE=0.03, 95% CI= −0.20, −0.07) and those that did not report theory (g= −0.18, SE=0.05, 95% CI= −0.28, −0.08) both had small effects. Studies conducted in the primary care (g= −0.28, SE=0.08, 95% CI= −0.44, −0.13), family (g= −0.19, SE=0.06, 95% CI= −0.30, −0.08), and school settings (g= −0.12, SE=0.04, 95% CI= −0.21, −0.04) showed significant reductions in BMI status. Diet-focused interventions (g= −0.25, SE=0.08, 95% CI= −0.42, −0.09) had moderate effects, whereas multi-behavior (g= −0.13, SE=0.03, 95% CI= −0.19, −0.08) and PA interventions (g=0.05, SE=0.15, 95% CI= −0.25, 0.34) both had small effects. Interventions that required high parental involvement (g= −0.16, SE=0.03, 95% CI= −0.23, −0.10) had small, though slightly higher group mean effects than those with lower or no parental involvement (g= −0.12, SE=0.05, 95% CI= −0.21, −0.03).

Waist Circumference

Fourteen studies with RCT/non-randomized controlled designs reported extractable information for waist circumference. There was a significant amount of heterogeneity between studies (Qtotal [13]=60.13, p<0.0001; I2=78%). Under the random effects model, the overall estimated intervention effect was −0.15 (SE=0.10, 95% CI= −0.35, −0.05), indicating that interventions had a small but significant reduction in waist circumference compared with controls (Appendix Figure 3). Results of categorical moderator analyses are summarized in Appendix Table 6. Interventions conducted primarily in the community setting (g= −0.34, SE=0.14, 95% CI= −0.63, −0.06) had moderate effects on waist circumference compared with controls.

Body Fat Percentage

Six studies with RCT/non-randomized controlled designs reported extractable information for body fat percentage. The Qtotal statistic (Q[5]=9.08, p=0.11) was not significant, and a fixed effect model showed no significant beneficial intervention effects on body fat percentage (g=0.08, SE=0.07, 95% CI= −0.06, 0.21) (Appendix Figure 5).

Physical Activity

Twenty-three studies with RCT/non-randomized controlled designs reported extractable information for PA. There was a significant amount of heterogeneity between studies (Qtotal [22]=60.67, p<0.0001; I2=64%). Under the random effects model, the overall estimated intervention effect of 0.12 (SE=0.05, 95% CI=0.03, 0.22) showed a slight increase in PA relative to controls (Appendix Figure 7). Study design and SES significantly explained variation in effect sizes (Table 2). RCTs (g=0.15, SE=0.06, 95% CI=0.04, 0.26) had small though slightly higher group mean effects on PA compared with non-randomized controlled studies (g=0.05, SE=0.10, 95% CI= −0.13, 0.24). Studies with higher-income samples had moderate effects on PA (g=0.28, SE=0.12, 95% CI=0.06, 0.51), whereas those with lower-income samples had small effects (g=0.09, SE=0.06, 95% CI=−0.02, 0.20).

Sedentary Behavior

Twenty-one studies with RCT/non-randomized controlled designs reported extractable information for sedentary behavior. The Qtotal statistic (Q[20]=23.40, p=0.27) indicated that all observed effect sizes had one true effect size underlying all studies, so a fixed effects model was run. The overall effect was not significant (g= −0.04, SE=0.03, 95% CI= −0.09, 0.02) (Appendix Figure 9).

Fruit and Vegetable Intake

Sixteen studies with RCT/non-randomized controlled designs reported extractable information for fruit and vegetable intake. The Qtotal statistic (Q[15]=17.22, p=0.31) was not significant, and a fixed effect model showed a very minimal, significant intervention effect favoring interventions over controls (g=0.08, SE=0.02, 95% CI=0.03, 0.12) (Appendix Figure 11).

Sugar-Sweetened Beverage Intake

Thirteen studies with RCT/non-randomized controlled designs reported extractable information for sugar-sweetened beverage intake. Given the Qtotal statistic (Q[12]=22.55, p=0.03), a fixed-effects model showed a significant effect of small magnitude favoring interventions over controls (g= −0.07, SE= 0.03, 95% CI= −0.13, −0.01) (Appendix Figure 13).

Risk of Bias

The risk of bias assessment is summarized in Appendix Figure 15 (across studies) and Appendix Table 9 (for individual interventions). Of the 105 interventions in the meta-analysis, 16% received a low, 20% received a high, and 67% received an unclear risk of bias rating. Results from the risk of bias moderator analyses are summarized in Tables 1-2 and Appendix Tables 5-8.

DISCUSSION

The purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to understand the effects of obesity interventions on the weight status and lifestyle behaviors of Hispanic youth. Results showed small-to-large effect sizes ranging from 0.01 to 1.18, with the majority of effects being small. Moderator analyses for weight-related outcomes in RCT/non-randomized controlled studies revealed that interventions conducted during infancy and early-to-middle adolescence, with those in higher-income youth, in primary care settings, and diet-focused had moderate effects on BMI reduction, and those conducted in community settings had moderate effects on waist circumference reduction; studies conducted in childhood and those focused exclusively on PA performed relatively poorly. Interventions also had no overall effect on body fat percentage. The overall intervention effects for lifestyle behaviors indicated existing interventions have small effects on PA and fruit, vegetable, and sugar-sweetened beverage intake and no effects on sedentary behavior.

Results from this paper comprehensively summarize the state of the obesity intervention literature among Hispanics and may be used by intervention scientists to calculate effect sizes and identify gaps in the field. With the exception of Ash et al.,35 who found Hispanics to be well represented in family-based childhood obesity interventions, the general consensus among prior reviews is that there is a dearth of pediatric obesity interventions among Hispanics. This review identified 117 interventions that included ≥50% Hispanic youth, the majority of which (76%) had samples composed of ≥75% Hispanics. The search results suggest an encouraging proliferation of interventions among Hispanics over the previous 7 years, with 75% of those included published after 2013. The search also revealed several forthcoming interventions,36-44 all of which involve parents.

Findings on the overall effects of interventions on youth weight status are consistent with previous meta-analyses.45 Kobes and colleagues45 conducted a meta-synthesis of 26 meta-analyses and found BMI status to be the most frequently reported outcome, with an overall effect of −0.12 on youth. Although this synthesis focused on Hispanics, results are similar. Of the 105 studies in this meta-analysis, 90% reported BMI status outcomes. Intervention effects on less commonly reported outcomes (waist circumference, body fat percentage) were also examined. Regardless of study design, interventions produced small reductions in BMI status and waist circumference, indicating that obesity interventions for Hispanic youth seem to be performing similarly to those aimed at the general population.

This study also examined a range of moderators, including those related to participant characteristics. Findings for the infancy stage provide preliminary evidence that early intervention can be effective in preventing excess adiposity, which other research has shown to be a critical period with great preventive potential.46 Consistent with previous literature, however, there were only a few studies conducted during infancy relative to other stages.47 Sex, percentage Hispanic, and weight status were not significant moderators, which is mostly consistent with Kobes et al.,45 who showed these characteristics had little to no impact on the magnitude of effect sizes. However, intervention effects on BMI status did vary by SES. The moderate and small effects observed for higher- and-lower income samples, respectively, shed light on the relevance of examining SES within racial/ethnic minority groups.48

Effects on BMI status for RCTs/non-randomized controlled studies also varied by intervention-related characteristics. Interventions conducted in primary care, family, and school settings showed significant reductions in Hispanic youth’s BMI status. A previous systematic review among the general population found the strongest support for school-based interventions with a home element over exclusively community- or home-based studies.49 Similarly, a review of obesity interventions among Hispanics concluded there is sufficient evidence to recommend school-based interventions with parental involvement for Hispanic youth in the U.S.5 Results from this meta-analysis differ in that the largest effects were observed in primary care, which may be attributed to the fact that 89% of interventions in this setting were treatment-focused. Notably, the effect of primary care interventions on Hispanic youth’s weight status is consistent with a previous meta-analysis on pediatric weight management interventions in primary care among the general population (Cohen’s d= −0.26).50 With regard to the family setting, previous reviews have identified parental involvement as critical to reducing Hispanic youth’s weight status.3,8,51 Results indicated that family-based interventions and those that required more active parental participation both had significant though small effects.

The extant literature consistently has recommended targeting multiple behaviors, such as diet and PA together, in order to maximize weight reduction among both the general49 and Hispanic youth populations.8 Results of the current study indicated that dietary interventions compared with PA or multi-behavioral interventions showed more promise in reducing weight among Hispanic youth. However, there were only 7 studies focused on exclusively diet and 2 studies on PA; the remaining 57 targeted multiple behaviors.

For PA outcomes, results were consistent with existing meta-analyses among the general pediatric population. Metcalf and colleagues52 found a small effect of 0.12, or an increase of approximately 4 minutes of overall PA among youth. These authors also examined moderators and found no significant differences in age, study duration, or setting. This study identified significant variation in effect sizes for RCTs/non-randomized controlled studies based on SES and time point of post-assessment. Like previous studies,53 Hispanic youth from higher-income families had moderate increases in PA whereas those from lower-income families had small increases. Notably, unlike prior reviews that have identified stronger PA effects in males versus females,53,54 sex was not a significant moderator. Overall, other reviews echo the challenges associated with increasing youth PA.55

There are several noteworthy findings from pre–post studies (summarized in the Appendix). One of the largest pre–post effects on weight status was for studies conducted in medical settings, which were primarily bariatric surgery interventions. Although cultural targeting was not a significant moderator for RCT/non-randomized controlled studies, it was for pre–post studies. Culturally targeted interventions showed small though slightly higher group mean effects than those with no cultural targeting or just linguistic strategies. Previous studies have described a lack of cultural relevance of interventions as contributing to smaller effects for Hispanics, with 1 systematic review concluding that culturally competent interventions are most effective at reducing obesity.11 This was only supported for pre–post studies in this meta-analysis, though the majority of studies did incorporate linguistic strategies (76%).

Limitations

Among the study’s limitations were the use of only 3 search databases, exclusion of gray literature, and evidence of publication bias. To address these limitations, the authors provided overall effect size estimates that accounted for potential publication bias. The majority of studies had an unclear overall risk of bias rating, signaling the need for intervention scientists to more consistently report important indicators of study quality. Interventions included in this review were also very heterogeneous, largely due to authors’ intent to be inclusive regarding participant age range and characteristics as well as intervention targets and study design. Though this may have limited the ability to make stringent comparisons, the goal was for this review to be comprehensive. Potential variation across effect sizes was explored through running moderator analyses to empirically show differences or similarities in mean effect sizes across different moderators. For example, although 24% of participants across included studies were non-Hispanic, there was no significant variation across studies by the moderator percentage Hispanic. Other potential moderators, such as intervention contact time or measurement type, were not evaluated. Tests of multiple moderators for each outcome may have increased the Type 1 error rate. Another limitation is that results from RCT/non-randomized controlled studies for weight status may have been slightly underestimated given the exclusion of 3 studies’ outliers. Lastly, the majority of effect sizes were based on data collected directly post-intervention versus longer follow-ups as a way to maximize the analytical sample size. As such, results reflect immediate post-intervention rather than a priori hypothesized or longer-term effects.

CONCLUSIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

This meta-analysis is the first comprehensive and rigorous quantitative synthesis of the pediatric obesity intervention literature among Hispanics. Results indicate that existing interventions have a significant though small impact on the weight status, waist circumference, PA, and diet of Hispanic youth, with no impact on their body fat percentage or sedentary behavior. Obesity and its associated lifestyle behaviors are complex, multi-determined, and difficult to change. Though often dismissed, small effects that accumulate over time can scale up to have a consequential public health impact.56,57 For example, a population-wide mean reduction of only 2-mmHg in diastolic blood pressure among adults can result in a 17% decrease in the prevalence of hypertension.58 Similarly, compared with inactivity, engaging in any activity has been associated with significantly lower mortality over a 20-year period.59 Beyond the need to develop more efficient, impactful interventions (e.g., using innovative methodological approaches such as the Multiphase Optimization Strategy),60 findings from this meta-analysis suggest it may equally important to develop strategies for more effectively spreading small effects across a population—either through targeted public health policies or by making existing interventions more easily disseminated Existing obesity-related interventions for Hispanic youth do not seem to be packaged in a way that facilitates their widespread reach. Only 4% of the 117 interventions included in this review, for example, leveraged technology for intervention delivery despite Hispanics’ widespread and growing access.61 To address the widening health disparities among this growing segment of the U.S. population, it is imperative for interventions to adopt new approaches for designing with dissemination in mind.
