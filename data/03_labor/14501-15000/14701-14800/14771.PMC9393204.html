Treatment of Lesser Metatarsophalangeal Joint Instability With Plantar Plate Repair: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Abstract

Recognition of metatarsophalangeal joint plantar plate injuries has improved over time as the condition has become more widely understood and identified. With the diagnosis of a plantar plate injury as a subset of metatarsalgia becoming more common place, there are multiple surgical options that have been utilized to address the condition. Direct repair of the plantar plate has emerged as the treatment of choice for foot surgeons with a tendency to favor a direct dorsal approach for the repair. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis using preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis guidelines, to determine the magnitude of change that can be expected in visual analog scale pain and American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society scores postoperatively. A total of 12 studies involving 537 plantar plate tears were included who underwent direct repair of the plantar plate through either a dorsal (10 articles) or plantar approach (2 articles). Summary estimates were calculated which revealed improvement in visual analog scale pain (pooled mean change of −5.01 [95%CI −5.36, −4.66] pre-to postoperative) and improvement in American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society scores (pooled postoperative mean improvement 40.44 [95%CI 37.90, 42.97]) of patients within the included studies. Random effects models were used for summary estimates. I2 statistic was used to assess for heterogeneity. We concluded there is a predictable level of improvement in pain and function in patients undergoing a direct dorsal approach plantar plate repair with follow-up out to 2 years.

The plantar plate is a rectangular, fibrocartilage structure, residing on the inferior surfaces of the lesser metatarsophalangeal joints (1,2). Fiber orientation of the plantar plate suggests that it withstands tensile loads in line with the plantar fascia, as well as compressive loads from the metatarsal head (2). Repetitive overloading of the lesser metatarsophalangeal joints leads to plantar plate attenuation or rupture resulting in metatarsophalangeal joint instability (2).

Plantar plate injuries have been studied for more than 20 years. Multiple methods of direct repair of plantar plate injuries have been described over the years including: dorsal approach with osteotomy, dorsal approach without osteotomy, plantar approach, combined dorsal and plantar approach, suture button technique, and purely arthroscopic techniques. However, to date, there have been only a select number of clinically-based outcomes studies published on the topic, and most have been grossly underpowered.

The purpose of this study was to assemble all existing peer reviewed studies on direct surgical repair of plantar plate injuries, and to better understand what is the expected mean improvement in visual analog scale (VAS) for pain and American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) scores when undergoing direct repair of the plantar plate.

Materials and Methods

We followed standard methodology for performing a systematic review using preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis guidelines. A systematic review of studies published in MEDLINE and the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature databases was performed from their inception in their entirety through June 1st, 2020 to identify studies that evaluated the effects of direct operative repair of plantar plate injuries. Two authors (R.J. and M.C.) independently evaluated the eligibility of studies with an initial title and abstract review. The authors were blinded to each other’s decisions. Articles of irrelevant subject matter were excluded immediately. Studies meeting the initial inclusion criteria of subject and population were considered for a full-text review by 2 blinded reviewers. Disagreements in the review process were settled by a third reviewer when necessary (A.F.). Studies were required to meet all inclusion criteria and agreement by the third reviewer (A.F.) for entry into the meta-analysis.

Search Methods

A search strategy consisting of a combination of medical subject headings (MeSH) terms and keywords was developed and exploded to generate sets for the themes “plantar plate” and “treatment.” Our full search strategy can be found in Fig. 1. No language limits were applied. In addition to a database search, a reference review was performed of all included studies and of previous reviews on the topic. We additionally sought to identify unpublished studies by pulling relevant titles and abstracts of scientific conference proceedings of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons, American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society and American Podiatric Medical Association over the past decade (from January 2010 to June 1, 2020).

Study Eligibility

The inclusion criteria included any original study published in a peer reviewed journal that evaluated patients who were treated for lesser metatarsophalangeal joint plantar plate injuries with direct repair via a dorsal or plantar approach with minimum follow up time of 6 months or greater.

Included studies had to provide a clear description of the technique used and needed to confirm the diagnosis of a plantar plate injury by either ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging, or intraoperative inspection. Prospective and retrospective studies were included. Studies that used cadaver or animal models, focused on indirect repair or radio-frequency shrinkage, and case studies (where n ≤ 2) were excluded. Our primary outcome of interest was the mean difference in pain and functionality measured pre and postoperatively via VAS pain scales and AOFAS scores.

Data Collection and Analysis

Relevant data was extracted from included studies using a piloted standardized data collection form. Data which included pre and postoperative VAS or AOFAS scores were required for inclusion into the meta-analysis. Additional data including study demographics (i.e., author, year), country, setting, patient demographics and procedure type were also extracted (Fig. 2). Summary estimates for mean VAS for pain and mean AOFAS scores were generated from included studies. Analysis was performed in STATA (Stata-Corp LLC, College Station, Texas) 17 using random effects models to evaluate the mean difference between pre and postoperative outcomes following direct plantar plate repair. I2 statistic was used to test for heterogeneity. We employed the guidelines outlined in the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) working group handbook (3) where 40% is considered low heterogeneity, 30% to 60% is moderate, 50% to 90% is substantial and 75% to 90% is considerable. A funnel plot was not created to assess for publication bias since less than 10 studies (recommended minimum amount) (4) were included in the meta-analysis. Publication bias was instead assessed qualitatively in combination with assessing study quality. Study quality was assessed using the CARE case report guidelines (5).

Sensitivity analyses were performed as an attempt to decrease the pooled heterogeneity and test robustness of results via exclusion of outliers. Outliers were assessed with visual inspection of confidence intervals and identified as those with little or no overlap with other included studies, as outlined in the GRADE handbook (3).

Results

A total of 12 studies (6-17) with 537 plantar plate tears were included in our analysis. The preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis flow diagram (Fig. 2) explains the process utilized to identify the studies while Fig. 3 lists the defining characteristics of the included studies.

Level 4 evidence (case series) was the most frequent study design, but 2 studies had a comparison group (4, 6). Studies were generally well conducted in the context of case series, with transparent reporting and only low/medium risk of bias. Direct repair from a dorsal approach was the dominant procedure reported in the literature compared to a plantar approach (10 articles vs 2 articles respectively).

Summaries of our statistical analyses are given with forest plots and can be found in Figs. 4-6 Fig. 4. displays the mean reduction in VAS between pre-operative and postoperative scores in all the studies that reported VAS. The pooled mean change in VAS pain from pre-to postoperative was −5.24 (95% CI, −6.09 to −4.39) among all the studies that reported VAS Fig. 5. displays mean reduction in VAS after sensitivity analysis which removed studies contributing to the initial high heterogeneity. The pooled mean change in VAS pain from pre-to postoperative after sensitivity analysis was −5.01 (95% CI, −5.36 to −4.66) among articles that examined plantar plate repair from a dorsal approach (n = 100 joints) Fig. 6. displays the mean improvement in AOFAS score after sensitivity analysis. The pooled postoperative mean improvement in AOFAS score was 40.44 (95% CI, 37.90-42.97).

Discussion

This is not the first attempt to summarize the growing literature on surgical repair of the plantar plate. Elmajee and colleagues (18) recently published a strictly qualitative synthesis on plantar plate repair in 2017. However, their analysis had only 162 patients, while we incorporate additional subjects with a total of 537 plantar plate tears. Furthermore, our analysis represents a clear improvement on this prior work of Elmajee and colleagues as we performed a meta-analysis and derived pooled estimates regarding the expected improvement in VAS pain and AOFAS scores postoperatively. In our work, the studies that reported on patients who underwent plantar plate repair from a dorsal approach had low heterogeneity after the sensitivity analysis (less than 40% for both AOFAS and VAS pain) and similar VAS pain and AOFAS values in all analyses. This suggests the observed 5 point mean reduction in VAS pain and nearly 40-point increase in AOFAS scores postoperatively is likely quite accurate, as the estimates appear to be robust to change with varying conditions/assumptions.

We conclude the dorsal approach is predictable as we saw a large mean change (improvement) in both VAS pain and AOFAS scores (with relatively tight 95% confidence intervals) once summary estimates were generated in both pain and function in patients undergoing direct dorsal repair at 1 year follow up. We could not conclude the same for a plantar approach as only 2 articles reported on this technique and neither provided preoperative VAS or AOFAS scores (8,13).

In addition, we would suggest that a Weil osteotomy is also a critical component to the success of this procedure. Although dorsal repair of the plantar plate has been described without the use of a Weil osteotomy, we feel that caution should be used if hoping to extrapolate our findings to performing a dorsal repair without a concomitant Weil osteotomy. This is because the overwhelming majority of subjects used in deriving summary estimates for change in VAS pain and change in AOFAS incorporated a concomitant Weil metatarsal osteotomy. In fact, there is actually very limited outcomes data in the published literature (n = 14 subjects) performing a dorsal approach repair without a concomitant lesser metatarsal osteotomy (7).

It is important to recognize the limitations in our work. For instance, there still remains a paucity of literature on the long-term outcomes of plantar plate repair. Despite the generally favorable outcomes reported in the short and intermediate term with direct repair of the plantar plate, there is little long-term data with follow up greater than 2 years. It is therefore important to recognize that there is uncertainty at this time as to what kind of longevity or long-term sequela may occur. Another important limitation involves the articles (predominantly case series) we had to draw on. The lack of a control group, and predominantly level 4 evidence we had to use, certainly hinders the strength of any conclusions drawn. Additionally, the original analyses exhibited high heterogeneity (>80%) suggesting pooling this data may not render a reliable estimate. In an attempt to lower the heterogeneity and increase the confidence of the results, the authors performed a sensitivity analysis (by design) to exclude outlier studies. This brought the heterogeneity to an acceptable level, allowing the pooling of results and therefore increasing confidence in the estimates. However, the initial effect estimate should be interpreted with caution and a range of values may offer a more conservative and accurate assessment of postoperative outcomes (i.e., a decrease in VAS pain can be expected to range from 5.02 - 5.24 and an increase in AOFAS score may be expected to range from 37.19 to 40.44). Lastly, it is clear that there were a significantly greater number of publications that studied a dorsal approach (compared to a plantar approach), and that most dorsal approach investigations used proprietary instrumentation to repair the injured plantar plate. It is important to recognize that the authors themselves reported a financial relationship with the manufacturer of the instrumentation in 2/3rds (6/9) of the studies included. This could potentially manifest as an overly optimistic portrayal of this technique in the reported literature and could serve as a source of potential bias. However, the studies not reporting a relationship (n = 3) also reported very similar results in both magnitude and direction.

In conclusion, there is considerably more published data in existence on dorsal approach as compared to plantar approach concerning direct repair of the plantar plate. Through our analysis, there is a predictable level of improvement in pain and function in patients undergoing a direct dorsal approach plantar plate repair out to 2 years postoperatively. Additional work with longer term follow-up and comparison groups is warranted.
