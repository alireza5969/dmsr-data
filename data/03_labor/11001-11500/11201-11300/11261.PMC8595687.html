Abstract

Background:

Treating acute pain among persons with opioid use disorder (OUD) on opioid agonist therapy (OAT) is complex, and the therapeutic benefits of opioids remain unclear when weighted against their abuse potential and respiratory depressant effects.

Methods:

We conducted a systematic review of experimental pain studies examining opioid-induced analgesia among persons with OUD on OAT. We searched multiple databases from inception to July 30, 2021. Study quality was assessed by previously established validity measures.

Results:

Nine studies were identified, with a total of 225 participants, of whom 63% were male, and 37% were female. Six studies included methadone-maintained persons with OUD; four studies included buprenorphine-maintained persons with OUD; and three studies included healthy persons as comparison groups. Either additional doses of OAT or other opioids – morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, or remifentanil – were administered. In seven studies, persons with OUD on OAT did not experience analgesia, despite receiving opioid doses up to 20 times greater than those clinically used to treat severe pain among the opioid naïve. Conversely, in two studies, high-potency opioids did produce analgesia, albeit with greater abuse potential. Notably, persons with OUD on OAT remained vulnerable to respiratory depression.

Conclusions:

Although persons with OUD on OAT can derive analgesic effects from opioids, high-potency compounds may be required to achieve clinically significant pain relief. Further, persons with OUD on OAT may remain vulnerable to opioid-induced abuse potential and respiratory depression. Together, these finding have clinical, methodological, and mechanistic implications for the treatment of acute pain in the context of OAT.

Introduction

1.

Opioid agonist treatment (OAT), which includes methadone and buprenorphine, is the primary treatment modality for opioid use disorder (OUD), which may also be treated with extended-release intramuscular naltrexone (Sofuoglu et al., 2019). Since OAT is highly effective in reducing the risk of overdose deaths and preventing the transmission of HIV and hepatitis C (Sofuoglu et al., 2019), increasing the capacity of healthcare systems to offer OAT remains a critical public health goal in the United States (Williams et al., 2019). As a growing number of persons are maintained on methadone and buprenorphine for OUD, management of acute pain in this population has emerged as a clinical challenge (Urman, 2018). Persons maintained on OAT may experience acute pain due to trauma, or infection, or malignancy, and may also require surgery. In those circumstances, treatment with additional opioids may be necessary to provide effective analgesia. However, long-term opioid exposure leads to a host of neuroadaptations resulting in tolerance to their analgesic effects – which is further complicated by a tendency for clinicians to interpret complaints of pain as “drug-seeking” behavior (Carusone et al., 2019; da Cunha, 2015; Ene et al., 2008; Paschkis and Potter, 2015). Taken together, these clinical challenges underscore the need to optimize the use of opioids while managing acute pain among those who are maintained on OAT.

Methadone, a long-acting, synthetic, full agonist opioid, is used to treat OUD and, to a lesser extent, chronic pain. Both the analgesic and opioid withdrawal-suppressing effects of methadone are attributed to its full agonism at the mu-opioid receptor (MOR) – the main target for the analgesic, respiratory-depressing, and reinforcing effects of opioids (Matthes et al., 1996). Long-term methadone treatment also leads to desensitization of MORs, thereby blocking euphoric effects from other opioid agonists, such as heroin. Methadone-maintained persons also develop tolerance to the analgesic effects of other MOR agonists when they are prescribed therapeutically, creating a clinical challenge when opioid-derived analgesia is acutely required.

Unlike methadone, buprenorphine is an opioid with a singular pharmacology: It is a partial agonist at the MOR, and an antagonist at the delta (DOR) and kappa opioid receptors (KOR) (Parida et al., 2019). The initial therapeutic effects of buprenorphine for OUD are attributed to its ability to stimulate MOR when most of these receptors are not occupied by MOR agonists, during early opioid withdrawal, thereby resulting in withdrawal suppression. Subsequently, due to buprenorphine’s high affinity for the MOR, and its slow dissociation, it can displace other MOR agonists, thereby antagonizing their activity and preventing euphoria. Yet, although buprenorphine may induce less MOR desensitization and opioid tolerance than methadone, its capacity to displace other MOR agonists was hypothesized to impair opioid-induced analgesia (Bryson, 2014; Bryson et al., 2010; De Aquino et al., 2021; Stromer et al., 2013). However, more recent reports suggest that opioid-induced analgesia for acute pain can be appreciated among persons receiving buprenorphine maintenance (Haber et al., 2019; Kornfeld and Manfredi, 2010; Macintyre et al., 2013; Quaye et al., 2020).

Several clinical guidelines have been proposed to manage acute pain among persons maintained on OAT (Acampora et al., 2020; Alford et al., 2006; Buresh et al., 2020; Freye and Hartung, 2005; Goel et al., 2019; Quaye and Zhang, 2018). For instance, among methadone-maintained persons needing surgery, both the continuation of oral methadone throughout the perioperative period and providing methadone in divided doses have been recommended (Coluzzi et al., 2017; Cornett et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2005). For those who receive buprenorphine and need surgery, historical recommendations for a 72-hour abstinence from buprenorphine have gradually yielded to recommendations to reduce the dose before the procedure, or even to continue the usual dose. Further, high-dose oral and intravenous (IV) opioid analgesics have been recommended for the acute analgesic needs of persons with OUD receiving OAT. Hence, taken together, the current clinical guidelines are inconsistent regarding whether the daily dose of OAT should be withheld or lowered, and regarding what type of additional opioid agonists – whether short- or long- acting, or low- or high-potency – should be used (Anderson et al., 2017). The ideal clinical care of persons of OUD should balance the need for adequate analgesia while minimizing the risk of returning to non-medical use of opioids. Inappropriate management of acute pain among persons with OUD receiving OAT can have consequences that range from longer hospitalization and higher risk of pain chronification, to adverse effects from prolonged and/or high-dose opioid exposure – including sedation and relapse (Aasvang and Kehlet, 2004; Katz and Seltzer, 2009; Macrae, 2008; Myers and Compton, 2018).

In clinical settings, multiple individual and context-dependent factors may impact the response to analgesic medications, making the evaluation of opioid analgesia challenging (Olesen et al., 2012). Controlled, human-laboratory pain assessments may be better suited for investigating the analgesic properties of drugs (Arendt-Nielsen et al., 2007) and providing direction for subsequent translation to the clinical environment. Experimental studies of opioid-induced analgesia among persons maintained on OAT have produced mixed results, with some even reporting opioid-induced increases in pain sensitivity. To our knowledge, these conflicting findings have not been appraised and synthesized. Hence, in this systematic review, we summarize and appraise controlled studies examining the analgesic effects of opioids for acute experimental pain among persons receiving OAT for OUD.

Methods

2.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

2.1

We conducted a systematic search of the literature in accordance with recommendations from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Moher et al., 2009). A search strategy was generated in consultation with a medical professional with extensive experience in research informational retrieval (OG) (Hausner et al., 2015, 2016). Subject headings (MeSH and EMTREE) and free terms of relevance to the review were selected from the PubMed and EMBASE controlled vocabulary thesauruses. Additional terms were identified using PubMed PubReMiner (Koster, 2004) and MeSH on Demand (U.S. National Library of Medicine, 2021), as well as by reviewing abstracts of relevant papers.

The following databases were searched, without language or date restrictions: MEDLINE (Ovid), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Ovid), EMBASE, Scopus, and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global (ProQuest). Conference proceedings abstracts were searched within the EMBASE database. The full search strategies for the Ovid and EMBASE interfaces are reported in the supplementary material (Appendix 1). The Cochrane Highly Sensitive Search Strategies for identifying randomized trials in MEDLINE and EMBASE were adapted and added to the searches of these databases. All the searches were conducted during July of 2021. Notably, the search strategy was validated with a sensitivity of 100%, such that all relevant studies known to the authors were identified.

A total of 3446 citations were retrieved (Figure 1) and imported into Rayyan, a collaborative systematic review software (Ouzzani et al., 2016). Duplicates were first automatically detected, and then manually verified to be removed. Eligible studies included: 1) Controlled, experimental human studies in which participants with OUD were maintained on either methadone or buprenorphine; 2) Participants received an opioid pharmacological intervention for acute analgesia; and 3) The study outcomes included pharmacological response to evoked – or experimentally-induced – pain. Conversely, studies were excluded if: 1) They were observational; 2) They were not an original investigation; 3) They were not peer-reviewed; and 4) Assessment of evoked pain was not a study outcome.

Three investigators (JPD, SP, and OG) independently screened the title and abstract of the search results for full text retrieval. The same group of investigators screened the full text articles for inclusion, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. The clinicaltrials.gov registries for eligible studies were excluded if they did not provide results other than those available in the corresponding published reports. Likewise, if no results had yet been reported, entries for ongoing and finished studies were excluded.

Two investigators (JPD and SP) independently reviewed articles for inclusion and study quality. Both investigators examined the methodology component independently of the study results. Overall study quality was defined as high for well-performed, placebo-controlled, randomized trials, as these exhibit the best internal validity. Open-label trials were considered of lower quality due to numerous potential biases. Using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) framework, we qualitatively evaluated all studies for their propensity for the most common and important biases – such as selection, confounding and misclassification –, thereby allowing their final GRADE classification (Table 1).

Outcome Measures

2.2.

The primary outcome of interest was the difference in pain response before and after receiving an opioid. Specifically, pain responses were indexed by pain tolerance in the Cold Pressor Test (CPT), the analgesic measure reported across all studies. Notably, the CPT is an evoked pain procedure that has demonstrated high predictive validity for opioid-induced analgesia (Conley et al., 1997; Eckhardt et al., 1998). Although studies have used variations of this technique, the procedure typically begins with immersion of the participant’s hand into a warm-water bath (37 °C) for 3 minutes; subsequently, the hand is immersed into a cold-water bath (0–5 °C), and the participant is asked to tolerate the stimulus for as long as possible before withdrawing the hand. Trials are typically truncated at 5 minutes, and tolerance is measured by the number of seconds the hand remains in the cold-water bath. Appendix 2 outlines the instruction set of the CPT used in each included study. Data collected included: 1) The sample size in the active treatment group; 2) The sample size in the placebo control group; 3) The mean baseline pain score and standard deviation before the intervention; and 4) The mean post-intervention pain score (Higgins et al., 2017).

When available in the included studies, we also examined secondary outcomes related to specific adverse effects of acute opioid administration: 1) Abuse potential, as indexed by semi-structured questionnaires and visual analog scales (VAS); and 2) Respiratory depression, as indexed by either respiratory rate or oxygen saturation.

When data was only available in plot format, efforts were made to contact the authors of primary studies. Subsequently, if access to primary data remained unavailable, Plot Digitizer software was used to extract data from publications (Kadic et al., 2016). Since significant study heterogeneity existed with respect to study procedures (Appendix 2), it was decided, a priori, that quantitative data pooling was inappropriate. Lastly, publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and the Egger’s test. Data management and all statistical analyses were completed using STATA/IC v16 (StataCorp).

Results

3.

Nine clinical studies provided relevant data for our research aim and met inclusion criteria, and were therefore included in the final review (Figure 1) (Agin-Liebes et al., 2021; Athanasos et al., 2019; Athanasos et al., 2006; Compton et al., 2000; Doverty et al., 2001; Hay et al., 2008; Huhn et al., 2019; Murnion et al., 2020; Nielsen et al., 2019). Combined, these nine studies had a total number of 225 participants, 63% who were male and 37% who were female. Among these studies, two were open-label, and seven were randomized, placebo-controlled trials (RCT). A total of six studies included methadone-maintained, and four studies included buprenorphine-maintained persons with OUD; and three studies included healthy persons as a comparison group. All studies administered either additional doses of OAT or at least one other opioid agonist – either morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, or remifentanil – which vary in potency. Further, all studies reported pain tolerance, derived from the CPT, and measured in seconds, as an analgesic outcome. Conversely, the typical adverse effects of opioid agonists administered – abuse potential and respiratory depression – were inconsistently reported. The findings of each study, as well as the equivalent opioid dose administered, in morphine milligram equivalents (MME), are summarized in Table 1.

Analgesic Response

3.1

Among studies that enrolled methadone-maintained participants, an open-label study by Hay and colleagues reported statistically significant, dose-dependent increases in CPT pain tolerance induced by the acute administration of intravenous (IV) remifentanil (0.5 to 3.5 μg/kg/min) (Hay et al., 2008). Conversely, in another open-label study, Doverty and colleagues found that IV morphine infusions aiming to produce a pseudo-steady state (11.9 mg and 14.8 mg, respectively) increased pain tolerance in CPT among healthy persons, but not in those maintained on methadone (Doverty et al., 2001). Similarly, in an RCT, Athanasos and colleagues found that IV morphine (11.9 and 55.2 mg, respectively) increased pain tolerance among healthy persons, but not in methadone-maintained individuals (Athanasos et al., 2006). In another RCT, Compton and colleagues did not find an increase in CPT pain tolerance following the administration of oral hydromorphone (2 mg) to participants maintained on methadone (Compton et al., 2000). In another RCT of persons maintained on methadone, Murnion and colleagues did not find increases in CPT pain tolerance produced by either acute oral oxycodone (5 mg), or an additional 30% of daily dose of methadone (Murnion et al., 2020). Finally, in a recent study, Agin-Liebes and colleagues reported that escalating doses of IV hydromorphone (up to 32 mg) did not produce analgesia, as measured by CPT pain tolerance, among methadone-maintained individuals. Hence, except after receiving IV doses of the high-potency opioid remifentanil (Hay et al., 2008), methadone-maintained persons appeared to experience no analgesic effect, as measured by CPT pain tolerance, regardless of study design and of whether additional methadone or other opioids were administered.

With respect to opioid analgesic responses among buprenorphine-maintained persons, an RCT by Huhn and colleagues found that an acute cumulative dose of up to 32 mg of IV hydromorphone significantly increased CPT pain tolerance (Huhn et al., 2019); however, an additional dose of up to 16 mg of IV buprenorphine – given over the maintenance sublingual (SL) dose of 16 mg, for a total cumulative dose of 32 mg – did not produce an analgesic response (Huhn et al., 2019). In another RCT, Athanasos and colleagues administered IV morphine doses of 55.2 and 11.9 mg to achieve pseudo-steady state concentrations among buprenorphine-maintained persons and healthy persons, respectively. They found an increase in pain tolerance in the healthy control but not in buprenorphine-maintained participants (Athanasos et al., 2019). In another RCT, Nielsen and colleagues found that additional SL doses of buprenorphine (1.5 to 2 times the daily dose) did not increase pain tolerance among buprenorphine-maintained participants (Nielsen et al., 2019). Murnion and colleagues reported that neither PO oxycodone (5 mg) nor an additional 30% of daily dose of SL buprenorphine increased pain tolerance among buprenorphine-maintained persons (Murnion et al., 2020). Similar to the findings among methadone-maintained persons, a positive analgesic effect was only noted with a large IV dose of a potent opioid (hydromorphone) (Huhn et al., 2019); otherwise, persons maintained on buprenorphine were unable to appreciate analgesia with IV morphine and PO oxycodone, or with additional doses of SL buprenorphine.

Table 1 summarizes the opioid doses administered in each study, as well as the mean pain tolerance and standard deviation, indexed by the CPT, before and after the study medication. Collectively, the results show that persons with OUD receiving OAT did not experience significant increases in pain tolerance, despite receiving over 55 mg of IV morphine; and required either up to 3.5 μg/kg/min of remifentanil, or 32 mg of IV hydromorphone to derive analgesic effects. Notably, clinical management protocols for severe acute pain, such as in the emergency room setting, typically involve the administration of 2.5 mg of IV morphine, or 1–2 mg of IV hydromorphone (Chang et al., 2013; Patanwala et al., 2010); similarly, the induction of analgesia with remifentanil involves an infusion of approximately 0.1 μg/kg/min, or a dose 14 times lower than that required by persons with OUD receiving methadone to experience analgesia (Burkle et al., 1996). Thus, despite receiving opioid doses up to 20 times higher than those clinically used for severe pain, in most cases persons with OUD receiving OAT did not derive analgesia – except for the administration of high-potency opioids, such as remifentanil and hydromorphone, which did produce analgesic effects.

Adverse Effects

3.2

Only four studies reported measures related to abuse potential (Table 1). Notably, analgesic doses of IV remifentanil did not produce significant increases in abuse potential assessments among persons receiving methadone maintenance (Hay et al., 2008); IV hydromorphone, although ineffective for analgesia among methadone-maintained persons, did not increase measures of abuse potential either (Agin-Liebes et al., 2021). In contrast, PO oxycodone, IV hydromorphone, and additional doses of buprenorphine (up to 16 mg IV, with up to 32 mg considering the SL and IV cumulative dose) caused increases in measures of abuse potential (Huhn et al., 2019; Murnion et al., 2020), as measured by a VAS, among persons receiving buprenorphine (Huhn et al., 2019, Murnion et al., 2020) and persons receiving methadone (Murnion et al., 2020).

Measures of respiratory depression were reported in five studies, in which IV remifentanil, IV morphine, IV hydromorphone and additional doses of either PO methadone or SL buprenorphine induced some respiratory depression, as measured by respiratory rate or oxygen saturation (Athanasos et al., 2019; Athanasos et al., 2006; Hay et al., 2008; Murnion et al., 2020) (Athanasos et al., 2006; Hay et al., 2008; Huhn et al., 2019).

Thus, despite their noted tolerance to opioid-induced analgesia, methadone- and buprenorphine-maintained persons remained moderately vulnerable to the abuse potential and respiratory depressant effects of opioids – especially given the high doses that were required to achieve analgesia, indicating that appropriate monitoring may be required in clinical settings (Table 1).

Quality Ratings of Studies

3.3

Using the GRADE criteria to appraise the quality of the evidence, seven studies provided high-quality evidence, and two studies provided low-quality evidence, primarily due to a lack of blinding to the intervention (Table 1). Across the seven high-quality studies, only one found an analgesic effect of the administered opioid (IV hydromorphone 32mg) (Athanasos et al., 2006; Hay et al., 2008; Huhn et al., 2019); in the three studies that included healthy individuals as a control group, an analgesic effect was experienced by healthy persons in two of them (Athanasos et al., 2019; Athanasos et al., 2006). Only three out the seven high-quality evidence studies reported both analgesic outcomes as well as measures of abuse potential and respiratory depression. Finally, there was evidence of publication bias, as indicated by funnel plot asymmetry (Figure 2) and the Egger’s test (p = 0.006).

Discussion

4.

In this systematic review, we examined the association between acute opioid administration and experimental pain outcomes among persons with OUD who are maintained on either methadone or buprenorphine therapy. Data were extracted from a total of nine studies which provided comparisons between various opioid agonists on analgesia indexed by measures of experimental (CPT) pain tolerance, as well as on common adverse effects of opioids – abuse potential and respiratory depression. First, acute opioid administration was associated with negligible analgesic effects in both methadone- and buprenorphine-maintained individuals. Indeed, our systematic review found that, in seven studies, methadone- or buprenorphine- maintained persons experienced no analgesia despite receiving opioid doses 10–20 times greater than those typically used to treat severe pain among opioid naïve persons (Table 1) (Chang et al., 2013; Patanwala et al., 2010). Such findings underscore a remarkably high level of tolerance to opioid-induced analgesia in this clinical population. Still, data from two individual studies indicated that IV remifentanil and IV hydromorphone were able to produce analgesia, suggesting that high-potency opioids, by providing greater MOR activation, may overcome chronic neuroadaptations in the nociceptive pathways that ultimately render individuals tolerant to the analgesic effects of opioids.

Second, our systematic review shows that, although the adverse effects of opioids were inconsistently reported, opioid-induced analgesia was often accompanied by an increase in measures of abuse potential. This finding underscores that the analgesic and rewarding effects of opioids are closely linked, and is dovetailed by a remarkable overlap between the clinical features and neural substrates of pain and opioid addiction – pain relief, itself, is rewarding, and can produce striatal dopamine release (De Aquino et al., 2020; Elman and Borsook, 2016). Third, despite their suboptimal analgesic responses, methadone- and buprenorphine-maintained persons were not immune to the respiratory depressant effects of opioids. Taken together, these findings indicate that, at neurobiological level, that analgesia, abuse potential, and respiratory depression may require different thresholds of MOR activation, and that the level of tolerance induced by OAT may be greater for analgesia than it is for reward or respiratory depression. Further, these findings have implications for the treatment of acute pain in the context of OAT, as well as for the methodologically sound development and mechanistic understanding of novel analgesic pharmacotherapies for this unique clinical population.

Clinical Implications

4.1

This systematic review has implications for providing acute opioid-induced analgesia for persons with OUD receiving OAT. Although preliminary evidence indicates that high-potency opioids are required to produce notable analgesia in this population, their use in the clinical setting must balance their potential therapeutic and adverse effects. It is possible that maximal analgesia and minimal abuse potential may be achieved by giving preference to compounds with specific pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic properties.

Remifentanil, for instance, is 100 times more potent than morphine (Glass et al., 1993), whereas hydromorphone is 20 times more potent (Wallenstein et al., 1990). Still, in this systematic review, we found that, unlike hydromorphone, an analgesic dose of remifentanil did not increase measures of abuse potential among persons with OUD receiving OAT (Hay et al., 2008; Huhn et al., 2019). Although remifentanil’s structure resembles that of other fentanyl analogues, this compound has some unique features. First, remifentanil has a fast onset and offset of action: Following IV administration, it has a rapid distribution, and a half-life of less than 5 minutes – over 30 times shorter than hydromorphone (Lj and Perry, 2005). Further, unlike hydromorphone, remifentanil is metabolized to nearly inactive substrates (Glass et al., 1993). Collectively, these properties may make remifentanil easier to titrate in specialized clinical settings, such as emergency or operating rooms, as it can be administered in relatively high doses, with minimal accumulation. Other emerging evidence indicates that remifentanil may produce analgesia in clinical contexts: For example, persons who received one month of opioid therapy for back pain still experienced remifentanil-induced increases in CPT pain tolerance (Chu et al., 2006). Yet, it is important to highlight that, in the current systematic review, both high-dose remifentanil and hydromorphone resulted in some respiratory depression, and that high-potency and short-acting opioids may have a higher risk of acute opioid withdrawal (Comelon et al., 2016), which can negatively impact the pain response due to withdrawal-induced hyperalgesia (Arout et al., 2015). Further, it is unclear how the widespread availability of fentanyl derivatives in the illicit drug supply, seen especially in North America in recent years, may influence the analgesic response among persons with OUD receiving OAT (Comer and Cahill, 2018). Considering these therapeutic tradeoffs, future well-controlled studies should examine the optimal opioid dose, formulation, and administration schedule to manage acute pain in the context of OAT.

Methodological Implications

4.2

We found that only three of the included studies reported both analgesic outcomes as well as measures of abuse potential and respiratory depression. Therefore, future studies should combine measures of therapeutic and adverse effects, consistent with existing guidelines, such as the Addiction Clinical Trial Translations, Innovations, Opportunities, and Networks (ACTTION), a consensus for standardizing the study of analgesics in addiction (Smith et al., 2015). Other methodological enhancements include consistent reporting of baseline chronic neuropathic pain, which is highly prevalent among persons with OUD who receive OAT, and can affect the experimental pain response (Compton et al., 2020). It is also important to consider differences in the baseline dose of methadone and buprenorphine, as well as in the timing of the experimental pain assessment in relation to last dose (Table 1). In both studies that demonstrated analgesic efficacy, the opioid was administered at trough methadone or buprenorphine levels (Hay et al., 2008; Huhn et al., 2019); in others, however, opioid administration at trough OAT levels did not produce analgesia (Athanasos et al., 2019; Athanasos et al., 2006; Compton et al., 2000; Doverty et al., 2001). Notably, the analgesic effects of methadone and buprenorphine may be both time- and dose-dependent, and are generally shorter in duration than their withdrawal suppression effects, which may specifically affect experimental pain assessments conducted at peak OAT levels (Levine et al., 1983). This variation in analgesic effects is in part due to individual differences in elimination half-lives, which can result in distinct rates of accumulation, with potential impact on analgesia (Mercadante et al., 1996). Lastly, evidence suggests that variations in thermal stimuli as small as 2°C may affect the performance on the CPT – underscoring the importance of providing a precise and constant temperature to increase the validity and reliability of results (Mitchell et al., 2004).

Mechanistic Implications

4.3

This systematic review also underscores the need to understand, at the mechanistic level, how opioid agonists exert their analgesic effects against the backdrop of OAT-induced neuroadaptations. At present, the level of MOR receptor occupancy that blunts opioid-induced analgesia remains to be investigated in both preclinical and translational studies. For instance, to our knowledge no positron emission tomography (PET) studies examining the analgesic efficacy of opioid agonists among persons with OUD who receive OAT exist. Prior studies have generally focused on the level of MOR occupancy required to blunt rewarding effects and improve the outcomes of OUD (Greenwald et al., 2014). Thus, clarifying the level of MOR occupancy that may optimize the analgesic response to other opioid interventions may guide the dosing schedule of OAT, which could be strategically modified before an opioid is administered for analgesia. Furthermore, distinct forms of OAT may differentially influence the pain response. As reviewed elsewhere, methadone and buprenorphine may have distinct effects on MOR downregulation and desensitization, which could thereby influence the analgesic response produced by other opioid agonists (De Aquino et al., 2021). Finally, the degree to which the analgesic response to OAT is influenced by factors such as the level of lifetime exposure to opioids, as well as changes in nociceptive systems produced by non-opioid drug use, remains to be explored (Trafton et al., 2004).

Limitations

4.4

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review of the association of acute opioid administration with experimental pain response among persons with OUD who receive OAT, and it has several noteworthy strengths. First, published and accepted guidelines to conduct and report systematic reviews were used (Moher et al., 2009). A highly sensitive search strategy was used across several electronic databases, which yielded high-quality studies providing data on CPT pain tolerance, which is an outcome with high predictive validity for opioid-induced analgesia (Conley et al., 1997; Eckhardt et al., 1998). Further, three independent reviewers performed all stages of the review, with good interrater reliability.

Still, despite its notable strengths, this systematic review was limited to studies of experimental pain. To produce evidence that definitively supports the generalizability of the current findings, research must be conducted in the clinical setting, when acute, opioid-analgesia is required – such as in trauma or perioperative circumstances. The lack of other pain measures included in this systematic review is another limitation, given that pain is a multi-faceted experience, with affective and cognitive domains, which are empirically modulated by opioid agonists (Martinez-Calderon et al., 2021). Although a wealth of research has consistently demonstrated that complex pain processes and pharmacological analgesia can be evaluated using laboratory pain assessments, such as the CPT (kumar Reddy et al., 2012), there is still significant variability in CPT procedures (Appendix 2); such heterogeneity made quantitative analyses of opioid-induced analgesia unfeasible. Moreover, the reviewed studies lacked consistent reporting of abuse potential and adverse effects. Future studies should not only rigorously gather data on adverse effects of opioids, but also employ dynamic pain assessment techniques, such as the quantitative sensory testing tools of temporal summation of pain, and conditioned pain modulation – doing so may not only increase the validity of the measures, but also provide insights about pain processing mechanisms, with implications for the analgesic response in clinical settings (Arendt-Nielsen et al., 2007; Edwards et al., 2016; Yarnitsky et al., 2010).

Second, as outlined above, the studies examined also had important limitations. Blinding procedures used in placebo-controlled opioid studies are limited when not using active controls with psychoactive effects. Persons with extensive history of opioid use are likely able to distinguish between active and placebo opioids for this reason. Additional research is needed, therefore, to determine to what degree expectancy for opioid-induced reward or other psychoactive effects influence the pain treatment response.

Third, to our knowledge, no studies, thus far, have examined how current non-medical opioid use among methadone- or buprenorphine-maintained individuals may affect the experimental pain treatment response. It is possible that the relapsing-remitting nature of non-medical opioid exposure dysregulates pathophysiologic processes that decreased the likelihood of experiencing opioid-induced analgesia, particularly in the context of illicit fentanyl use. Future research should examine whether the analgesic effects of opioid interventions differ as a function of non-medical opioid use. Nonetheless, the results of this review bring clarity to the mixed findings reported in experimental studies of opioid-induced analgesia among persons with OUD receiving OAT.

Conclusion

5.

Treating acute pain with opioids among persons with OUD who are maintained on methadone and buprenorphine is challenging and requires consideration of tradeoffs between therapeutic and adverse effects. This systematic review has shown that, in a well-validated model of experimental pain, despite receiving opioid doses up to 20 times greater than those used to clinically treat severe pain, persons with OUD who receive OAT experienced little to no analgesia. Opioid-induced analgesia in this clinical population may be enhanced by using high-potency opioids, whose abuse potential and respiratory depressant effects may be mitigated, in specialized clinical settings, by careful selection of agents with singular pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic properties – for example those with a fast onset and offset of action, as well as inactive metabolites, thereby allowing an easier titration that favors pain relief over undesired effects. While the cumulative research compiled in this review has characterized the current knowledge on how opioids provide an analgesic response among persons with OUD who are maintained on OAT, further translational research, as well as well-designed randomized, placebo-controlled studies, are needed to ascertain whether the therapeutic potential of specific opioids can be fully realized.

Highlights

  • Individuals who are on OAT show tolerance to the analgesic effects of opioids.

  • High-potency opioids are more likely to produce analgesia in this population.

  • Individuals on OAT remain vulnerable to respiratory depressant effects of opioids.

  • Novel approaches are needed to treat pain in individuals who are maintained on OAT.
