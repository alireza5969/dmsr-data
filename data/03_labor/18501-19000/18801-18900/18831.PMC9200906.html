Taste receptors in chronic rhinosinusitus, what is the evidence? A systematic review

Abstract

Background:

Bitter and sweet taste receptors (T2Rs and T1Rs), respectively, are involved in the innate immune response of the sinonasal cavity and associated with chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS). Growing evidence suggests extraoral TRs as relevant biomarkers, but the current understanding is incomplete. This systematic review synthesizes current evidence of extraoral taste receptors in CRS.

Methods:

PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, Web of Science, and Scopus were reviewed in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systemic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and included studies of genotypic and phenotypic T2R/T1R status in CRS patients.

Results:

Twenty-two studies with 3845 patients were included. Seventeen studies evaluated genotype and 10 evaluated taste phenotypes. Four of 6 studies examining the haplotype distribution of the T2R, TAS2R38, demonstrated increased AVI/AVI haplotype (“nontaster”) frequency in CRS. Meanwhile, 2 studies demonstrated decreased bitter sensitivity in CRS with nasal polyposis (CRSwNP), whereas 3 other studies reported decreased bitter sensitivity only in CRS without nasal polyposis (CRSsNP). Findings regarding sweet sensitivity were mixed. Three studies with cystic fibrosis patients (n = 1393) were included. Studies investigating the association between clinical outcomes and TAS2R38 alleles were limited, but the nonfunctional combination of AVI/AVI was associated with increased utilization of sinus surgery and, in CRSsNP patients, with poorer improvement of symptoms postoperatively.

Conclusion:

Both genotypic and phenotypic assessments of T2Rs suggest a potential association with CRS, particularly CRSsNP. However, limited evidence and mixed conclusions cloud the role of T2Rs in CRS. Future investigations should aim to increase diverse populations, broaden institutional diversity, examine T1Rs, and utilize uniform assessments.

An increasing body of evidence supports the extraoral functionality of bitter and sweet taste receptors (T2Rs and T1Rs, respectively) in the innate immune function of the sinonasal cavity. TRs offer a potentially relevant clinical biomarker and novel therapeutic target for CRS. In recent years, the effect of TRs on the pathophysiology and clinical characteristics of different CRS phenotypes, particularly in those patients without nasal polyposis (CRSsNP), has been increasingly investigated.

Extraoral T2Rs are a family of bitter-sensing G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) found to be widely expressed throughout the body, including ciliated cells and solitary chemosensory cells (SCCs) within the sinonasal cavity. In this region, T2Rs have potential immuneprotective effects that are not yet fully understood.1,2 One subset of T2R-mediated immune responses is activated in response to bacterial pathogens, particularly Gram-negative organisms (eg, Pseudomonas aeruginosa). Specifically, the detection of bacterial quinolones and acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs) leads to increased ciliary beat frequency and enhanced nitric oxide (NO) production.2,3 Calcium-dependent NO production results in stimulation of mucociliary clearance and direct antibacterial effects, which are linked to significant difference to clear and kill bacteria depending on TAS2R38 gene polymorphisms.4 A possible distinct subset of T2Rs are expressed on SCCs and drive AMP release, governed by T1R2 and T1R3 activity.5 In addition, tuft cells or brush-cell SCCs have a role in mucosal type 2 inflammation, including interleukin-25 signaling and cysteinyl leukotriene receptors (CysLTs). Thus, there is a possible link between tuft-cell (SCC/brush-cell) T2Rs and polyps.6 Bitter TRs have been associated with potential correlation with clinical course and innate immune response beyond bacterial infections and have been highlighted in the coronavirus-19 (COVID-19) pandemic.7,8

T1Rs are the major type of sweet-sensing GPCR found on SCCs and have an antagonistic function to T2Rs. T1R activation by sweet substances inhibits T2R’s protective signaling through opposing effects on calcium signaling, but the role in immunity is still unclear. One theory is that decreased sugar concentrations by bacteria diminish T1R-inhibitory signaling, propagating T2R protective function.2 It has been shown that the T1R2 and T1R3 subunits of the sweet TR are present in human airway SCCs; respiratory isolates of Staphylococcus species, but not P aeruginosa, produced D-amino acids that activate the sweet TR and influence antimicrobial innate immune defense responses.9

In the initial investigations examining the role of T2Rs in CRS, TR status has been studied both genotypically and phenotypically. Genotype studies screen for bitter TR genotypes (eg, TAS2R38) and their specific haplotypes, including PAV (“taster” or functional receptor) and AVI (“nontaster” or nonfunctional receptor) in addition to other less common mutations, using DNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction sequencing from sinonasal or saliva samples. Based on the genotyping, an individual is then characterized as a “supertaster” (PAV/PAV), “taster” (PAV/AVI), or “nontaster” (AVI/AVI), with non-tasting status correlated with a nonfunctional bitter TR.10,11 Meanwhile, phenotypic studies use the detection of bitter compounds such as phenylthiocarbamide (PTC), quinine, and denatonium as a proxy for TR status. Like genotype studies, phenotype methods can characterize “tasters” and “nontasters,” and they can also quantify a range of tasting ability. Oral tasting assessment has been demonstrated to be an accurate proxy for sinonasal TR status.12

Over the past decade, multiple authors have demonstrated an association between extraoral TR haplotype and functionality with increased sinonasal immune response and CRS, suggesting implications for both the understanding and treatment of this pervasive disease. We sought to systematically synthesize a coherent characterization of the role of TRs in CRS from available genotypic and phenotypic studies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1 |

A systematic review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systemic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines to assess the relationship between CRS and bitter TR genotype and function. The Problem, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) framework was not found to be applicable to this systematic review because no particular interventions or comparison groups were sought. Rather, this was a broader literature search aiming to summarize the current evidence base for bitter taste receptors in CRS. A search strategy (see Supporting Information section) was created with support from a qualified medical information professional using keywords and controlled vocabulary related to “sinusitis,” “taste receptor,” and “chronic.” This search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, Web of Science, and Scopus on December 9, 2020.

Articles were independently assessed by 2 reviewers (J.C. and C.S.), first by title and abstract, to include only English language human studies. Studies with nonoriginal data (eg, review articles and book chapters) and case series with <5 subjects were excluded. Full-text review was then performed to include only articles studying CRS and TR genotype or phenotype. Special populations of CRS patients with other chronic conditions, such as cystic fibrosis (CF) and primary ciliary dyskinesia, were also included. Conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer (N.H.) and the senior author (N.R.R.).

Data regarding patient inclusion criteria, TR genotype or phenotype assessment methods, comparisons between patients and controls, and correlations with disease severity were collected. A complete list of included study demographics is included in Tables 1, 2, and 3. Level of evidence (LOE) was assigned based on criteria from the 2011 Oxford Level of Evidence tool for prognostic studies.13 The included articles were then assessed with either the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for case–control studies14 or the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for cohort studies.15

A meta-analysis comparing TR genotype or phenotype with CRS disease metrics was planned but could not be performed due to inconsistencies between studies in the TR genes evaluated, patient inclusion criteria, and taste-sensitivity measures.

RESULTS

2 |

Search

2.1 |

Our search yielded 542 initial results. After removal of duplicates and application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 22 unique articles were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). There were 11 case-control studies, 8 cross-sectional studies, 2 retrospective cohort studies, and 1 prospective cohort study (Tables 1, 2, and 3). Of the included group of 22 articles, 15 specified patients as CRSsNP or CRSwNP; 1 study included only CRSwNP patients; 1 did not specify between CRSsNP and CRSwNP patients; and 1 included CRSsNP, CRSwNP, and aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD). Four of the articles studied special populations, 3 studies assessed patients with CF, and 1 study included patients with primary ciliary dyskinesia (Table 3).

Genotype

2.2 |

Bitter TR genotype frequency in patients with CRS was studied in 12 of the included articles (Table 1). Overall, data regarding the association between TR genotype and CRS were mixed. The largest studies found no association between TAS2R38 diplotypes and CRS, but analyses of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) did report an association between genotype and disease.

Nine of the studies investigated the TAS2R38 bitter TR gene. Four studies, with a total of 899 patients, specifically compared the frequency of TAS2R38 haplotypes in CRS patients with controls. These studies categorized patients with the 3 most common diplotypes: PAV/PAV, PAV/AVI, and AVI/AVI. One study, encompassing 70 patients, found a decreased representation of the PAV/PAV diplotype in CRS patients requiring endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS).16 Another study of 124 patients reported an increased representation of the AVI/AVI diplotype specifically in CRSwNP patients compared with controls.17 Two studies, with a total of 705 patients, found no significant difference in the distribution of diplotypes between CRS patients and controls.18,19

Two studies investigated individual SNPs within the TAS2R38 gene in CRS populations. One study of 74 patients reported an association between the rs713598 SNP and the presence of CRS.20 Another study of 614 patients from 2 Canadian CRS cohorts described a similar relationship between the rs10246939 SNP and CRS with a reported allele frequency difference of 11.1% to 15.1%.21 Both rs713598 and rs10246939 are part of a group of common variants that make up the PAV and AVI haplotypes.22

Several articles investigated possible differences in CRS patient populations based on TAS2R38 diplotypes. In a sample of 25 CRS patients, the PAV/PAV diplotype was protective against Gram-negatives and associated with a decreased likelihood (0%) of pathogen colonization within the upper respiratory tract compared with CRS patients with the AVI/AVI (42%) or PAV/AVI (58%) diplotypes.23 Another study of 53 CRS patients found no difference in the frequency of sinonasal bacterial infections between the 3 common TAS2R38 diplotypes.19 In a study of 207 patients, PAV/PAV patients with CRSsNP reported postsurgical improvement of 38 points on the 22-item Sino-Nasal Outcome Test (SNOT-22) questionnaire, a measure of quality of life (QoL), compared with other diplotypes, which had an average improvement of 12 points. This association was not seen when also considering CRSwNP patients.12

Phenotype

2.3 |

Taste phenotype assessed with taste tests utilizing different compounds was reported in 9 included studies (Table 2). Taste compounds varied across studies, including denatonium benzoate, quinine, propylthiouracil (PROP), PTC, sucrose, citric acid, and sodium chloride (NaCl). The association between bitter taste phenotype and CRS was difficult to interpret due to the various taste compounds used and differences in outcome reporting. The results of the studies on sweet tasting ability in CRS were similarly mixed.

Five studies compared bitter tasting between CRS patients and controls. Three studies (n = 328, n = 652, and n = 67) found decreased bitter sensitivity within CRSwNP subgroups18,24,25 using a variety of compounds (denatonium, quinine, and PTC). Workman et al and Lin et al both hypothesized that quinine and denatonium sensitivity are indicative of 2 separate T2R functions differentially impaired in CRSwNP and CRSsNP, with decreased sensitivity to quinine only in CRSwNP.18,24

Two studies reporting on denatonium rather than quinine sensitivity described significantly decreased denatonium sensitivity compared with controls only in the CRSsNP group. In 1 of these studies, Workman et al (n = 328) assessed the same population that reported decreased quinine sensitivity in CRSwNP patients.26 The other study (n = 51) only reported on denatonium sensitivity.27 Lin et al, however, reported decreased sensitivity to denatonium in both CRS subgroups when compared with controls.18 Alternatively, Wolf et al (n = 37) found decreased taste sensitivity to bitter, sweet, sour, and salty compounds in CRS patients compared with controls but found no difference between CRSsNP and CRSwNP subgroups.28 One study of 123 patients reported an association between improved bitter (PTC) taste scores in CRSsNP patients with increased sinonasal QoL being reported after ESS.12

Finally, sweet tasting ability via T1R taste receptors was assessed in 3 studies: 2 of them, including 677 patients, reported increased sucrose sensitivity specifically in CRSwNP patients,18,26 and the other reported decreased sucrose tasting ability in 37 CRS patients, with no subgroup difference.28

Special populations

2.4 |

Also included in this review were studies involving special populations of patients with CRS: 3 studies of patients with CF and 1 study of patients with primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) (Table 3).

All 3 studies of CF patients (n = 1393) assessed the TAS2R38 bitter TR gene. In 1 study, the PAV/PAV diplotype was associated with improved sinonasal QoL in patients with homozygous F508 deletions in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane receptor (CFTR) gene.29 In an Italian population of patients with CFTR mutations and concurrent CRS, the PAV allele was associated with a decreased need for ESS.30 The third study reported no correlation between the TAS2R38 diplotype and incidence of CRS or need for ESS.31

In the study of 35 patients with PCD, the PAV/PAV diplotype was associated with less frequent respiratory exacerbations compared to patients with the PAV/AVI or AVI/AVI diplotypes.32

Quality assessment

2.5 |

In total, 4 studies were evaluated as a level 3, and 18 were assigned a level 4. The case-control, cross-sectional, and retrospective cohort studies were evaluated with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale to assess risk of bias (Table 4). Based on this scale (rating of 9, indicating the lowest risk of bias), the included studies ranged from 3 of 9 to 9 of 9, representing an overall moderate risk of bias. There was 1 prospective cohort study that assessed with the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool, was evaluated to have a moderate risk of bias (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

3 |

In this systematic review we have assessed 22 unique studies investigating the emerging role of bitter and sweet TRs in the pathophysiology of CRS. The findings suggest that there may be an association between T2Rs and CRS, but the exact mechanism is still unclear and mixed results in the literature make it difficult to fully decipher their role. Our review also addressed the potential relationships between T2Rs and CRSwNP, CRSsNP, and special populations (eg, CF). Sweet TRs have shown interesting relevance in CRS patients compared with controls, but additional studies are required for better understanding.

Genotype studies present the most specific method for isolating T2Rs, and thus may be most effective in determining the role of specific bitter TR genes in CRS. For TAS2R38, the most well-studied bitter TR gene, the 3 most common polymorphisms occur at amino acid 49 (proline or valine), 262 (alanine or valine), and 296 (valine or isoleucine), giving rise to PAV and AVI haplotypes.33 The homozygous PAV haplotype (functional) is defined as a bitter “supertaster,” heterozygous haplotype as a bitter “taster” or “medium-taster,” and homozygous AVI haplotype (nonfunctional) as a bitter “nontaster.”10,11 “Supertaster” CRS patients with the functional homozygous PAV haplotype are shown to have greater potential for a sinonasal protective advantage compared with their “non-taster” counterparts.11

Phenotype studies have potential to promote rapid identification of a patient’s “tasting status” and present implications for understanding pathophysiology and guiding treatment. Although genetic makeup is critically important, the use of novel phenotypic markers such as brief taste tests does allow for rapid identification of “tasting status” and, more importantly, receptor functionality.12 These compounds are prepared at varying concentrations and administered orally, rinsing between solutions. The subjects then identify the quality (eg, sweet, salty, sour, or bitter) and rate the perceived intensity.34,35 Several studies demonstrated that the use of bitter compounds (eg, PTC, denatonium, quinine) can stimulate sinonasal bitter T2Rs and stratify tasting status in CRS patients based on their perceived intensity rating.24–28

Bitter TRs

3.1 |

Although T2R bitter receptors have demonstrated an association in the disease state of CRS generally, CRSsNP and CRSwNP have different underlying mechanisms of pathogenesis. Some evidence suggests a potential role of TRs in both clinical phenotypes, but T2Rs may be more important and related to CRSsNP. Although there is mixed evidence in the literature, some of the included investigations of patients with CRSsNP suggested higher frequencies of phenotypic “nontasters” compared with controls.25–27 One study found that CRSsNP had significantly more nuclear TAS2R38 immunohistochemistry staining than controls after lipopolysaccharide treatment,36 suggesting that TAS2R38 can translocate to the nucleus upon stimulation of inflammation signals, particularly in CRSsNP patients; however, the role of T2Rs in the nucleus is still unclear, necessitating replication by other groups and in other cell types.

The relationship of TR status in CRSwNP is less clear. Despite 1 study reporting that patients with CRSwNP had a higher prevalence of the nontaster (AVI/AVI) haplotypes compared with controls,17 multiple investigations failed to demonstrate a difference between bitter phenotype taste scores of patients with CRSwNP when compared with controls.17,26,27 PTC taste ratings were also inversely correlated with biofilm mass in CRSwNP, suggesting that CRSwNP patients with functional T2Rs may have a greater immune response.37

When CRSsNP and CRSwNP groups were compared, there was generally no difference in TAS2R38 gene expression levels, taste scores, or pathogen colonization distribution.28,35,36,38 These findings suggest that, although TAS2R38 is more likely to be associated with CRS patients compared with controls, it is less likely to be involved in differences between CRSsNP and CRSwNP phenotypes. Further study is necessary to better understand mechanistic implications of TRs in CRS.

Sweet TRs

3.2 |

T1Rs have been shown to be associated with CRS18,26,28 and have almost exclusively been investigated using phenotype studies.18,26,28 Patients with CRSsNP were more likely to have a higher perceived intensity of sucrose compared with controls, suggesting greater sensitivity to sweetness, and associated increased T1R functionality.18,26 Similarly, CRSwNP patients reported higher sweet intensity ratings compared with controls but not when compared with CRSsNP patients.18,26 However, it is also possible that these perceived intensities are not driven by T1R function and may be a general effect of CRS on TR cell physiology or associated sensory pathways. The antagonistic function of T1Rs on T2Rs may play a role in the pathogenesis of CRS, but the difference between CRSsNP and CRSwNP is unclear.28 Given the paucity of studies on this topic, future investigations will be needed to evaluate differences in CRS subtypes as well as specific genotypes of T1Rs.

TRs and special CRS patient populations

3.3 |

Understanding the relationship between bitter T2Rs and CRS can also provide insight into potential T2R targets in special patient subpopulations. CF, AERD, and PCD often clinically manifest with high incidences of CRS. Given the inherent differences in the pathophysiology of disease in these special populations, the role of T2Rs in such patients has been investigated separately. Overall, although there may be some association between T2Rs and sinonasal disease status in these unique patients, evidence is still scarce.

A unique phenotypic study of patients with AERD demonstrated hypersensitivity to the bitter substance denatonium in AERD patients when compared with controls.27 Moreover, the epithelium-derived cytokine, interleukin-25, was elevated after denatonium administration, suggesting some mechanistic relevance.

Meanwhile, the prognostic benefit of TAS2R38 gene haplotypes is inconclusive for patients with CF. In 1 study, the absence of the PAV allele in CF patients was associated with CRS, requiring ESS and pulmonary P aeruginosa colonization,30 whereas another study found no difference in TAS2R38 haplotypes in diagnosis of sinus disease or need for ESS.31 However, the supertaster haplotype (PAV/PAV) for TAS2R38 correlated with an improved QoL, showing significantly lower SNOT-22 scores.29 For patients with PCD, a genotypic study showed a lower percentage of supertasters (PAV/PAV) with frequent respiratory exacerbations compared with tasters (PAV/AVI) and nontasters (AVI/AVI).32 In addition, there were no P aeruginosa infections with the supertaster (PAV/PAV) haplotype. Nonetheless, given the inherent complexity of these patient populations and limited number of studies, it is difficult to draw conclusions within these special populations.

Implications for understanding patient outcomes in ESS

3.4 |

In addition to better understanding the pathophysiology of CRS, extraoral taste receptors have potential implications on patient outcomes after ESS. T2Rs appear to have a more substantial clinical relevance in patients with CRSsNP, and have generally shown improved disease prognosis in the presence of functional T2R taste receptors. In 1 study of patients with CRSsNP, the PAV/PAV (“supertaster”) haplotype had greater 6-month improvements in sinonasal QoL, afterESS compared with non-PAV/PAV haplotypes.12 Notably, this study grouped PAV/AVI and AVI/AVI into one group given the supposition of increased immune response only in PAV/PAV. Conversely, however, patients with CRSwNP PAV/PAV did not differ in post-operative sinonasal QoL compared with patients with a non-PAV/PAV genotype.12 Also contradictorily, in patients with CRSwNP, those with higher denatonium responsiveness were more likely to undergo ESS.39 The literature suggests a possible association between haplotype and ESS outcomes, but the mixed conclusions warrant additional research.

Limitations

3.5 |

Multiple limitations in the current literature make it difficult to evaluate the exact role of extraoral TRs in CRS. Most of the current literature is classified as low-level evidence, with 18 studies having an LOE rating of 4. The study populations were primarily white, with all 12 studies reporting >75% to 85% white patients.12,16,18,19,21,24–27,29,31,37 A more diverse patient population is needed to understand whether the role of extraoral TRs in CRS is consistent among patients of different racial backgrounds. In addition, methodologic differences in phenotype and genotype studies introduce limitations that make it difficult to fully assess the aggregate effect of bitter TRs on CRS.

There is a lack of continuity of both the included phenotypic and genotypic studies. In phenotype studies, a wide range of phenotype compounds was used, with denatonium in 3 studies,26,27,39 PTC in 2,12,25 quinine in 2,24,28 PROP in 1,17 and combinations in 1.18 The inconsistent usage of phenotype compounds and concentrations makes it difficult to compare specific intensity scales across studies. In genotype studies, because only 4 of 17 genotype studies looked at non-TAS2R38 genotypes, investigation of additional T2R genotypes (eg, TAS2R13, TAS2R48, TAS2R49) may strengthen these current findings in CRS.20,21,38,40

Finally, there is a lack of institutional and geographic diversity in the included studies. Fourteen studies were done in the United States (Philadelphia [9],12,16,18,24,26,27,29,37,39 South Carolina [1],25 West Virginia [1],20 Colorado [2],38,40 and unspecified [1]31), 5 in Italy,17,19,28,30,32 1 in Canada,21 1 in Poland, and 1 in Australia.23 Despite the leading role of the University of Pennsylvania in pioneering this field and conducting 9 of 14 of the included US studies, increased genetic and geographic diversity of patients will be important given inherent differences in the distribution genetic of TR genes.

Future work

3.6 |

Based on this review of the current literature, we outline potential directions for future studies to improve the evidence base. First, although the literature suggests that TAS2R38 genotype is associated with CRS in limited populations, future studies should corroborate these relationships with more racially and geographically diverse samples. Second, the phenotype results showed similar results to the genotype studies but the lack of standardization in the quantitative intensity scores makes it difficult to draw conclusions. Thus, future studies should establish a standard scale to rank taste intensity, allowing for more robust findings. With these changes in mind, a more routine inclusion of tasting phenotypes in prospective CRS-focused studies would help to elucidate the clinical relevance of these unique receptors. Adherence to similar reporting would be helpful in studies examining response to viral infections (eg, SARS-CoV-2), and will be critical when evaluating therapeutic-focused outcomes.

CONCLUSION

4 |

In this review we have evaluated both genotype and phenotype assessments of bitter and sweet TRs in patients with CRS. The limitations in the literature lead to mixed conclusions about extraoral taste receptors in CRS: Although there are certainly some associations between T2R and T1R status with CRS, there is not sufficient evidence to suggest a causal role of TRs in CRS. Nevertheless, with multiple studies identifying increased TAS2R38 PAV/PAV haplotype frequency in CRS patients, TRs still represent a novel way to potentially assess patients with CRS. Given the preliminary and sometimes contradictory evidence, derived mostly by a small number of investigators, we encourage additional study of diverse populations at multiple levels. Moreover, sweet TRs may also play a role in CRS pathophysiology, but it is difficult to draw robust conclusions due to the limited number of studies on these receptors. With these encouraging findings, the role of TRs affords an opportunity for an enhanced understanding of the CRS pathogenesis and offers a clinically relevant biomarker.
