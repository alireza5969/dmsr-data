Abstract

Background:

Significant disparities exist for timely access to cancer care for children, with the highest disparities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). This study aims to conduct a systematic review that identifies the factors contributing to delayed care of childhood cancers in LMICs.

Methods:

We will conduct a systematic review with search strings compliant with the PICO framework: (1) the Population—children (aged 0–18 years) from LMICs; (2) the Exposure—factors contributing to timely childhood cancer care; (3) the Outcome— delays in childhood cancer care.

Discussion:

Our study is an essential step to guide strategic interventions to assess the myriad of factors that prevent children from accessing timely cancer care in LMICs. The results will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal and shared with institutions related to the field.

INTRODUCTION

1 |

Childhood cancer remains a leading cause of childhood mortality and disability around the world.1 Approximately 397 000 new childhood cancer cases are expected each year worldwide, and an estimated 85% of these cases occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).2–4 Cancer survival rates among children living in LMICs are estimated to be 30%, compared with 80% among children living in high-income countries (HICs).5–7 Likewise, only 43% of the expected burden of childhood cancer cases are diagnosed in LMICs, compared with 97% in HICs. The actual burden of childhood cancer might be underestimated because of challenges such as the lack of cancer registries and vital registration systems.4,8–10 In this context, the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer has aimed to reach at least a 60% survival rate worldwide by 2030, with particular emphasis in LMICs.11

Childhood cancer care is fundamentally different from adult cancer care.12,13 Early diagnosis and treatment are critical to attaining optimal survival for most childhood cancers.14–17 However, children are often neglected in cancer control planning efforts in resource-poor settings, with as few as 30% of children in LMICs receiving early cancer diagnosis and treatment.18–20 Therefore, identifying the factors contributing to delayed cancer care is an essential step in guiding strategic interventions to improve the care of childhood cancer along the entire continuum of care in low-resource settings.

Few reviews have been conducted to assess the factors associated with delayed cancer care for children in LMICs.21–24 Existing reviews either lack representation of LMICs or fail to account for the fundamental differences between childhood cancers and adult cancer care. Furthermore, most of these reviews often exclude qualitative studies and gray literature. Therefore, a global, comprehensive, and childspecific review of the factors of delayed cancer care for children is needed to guide policy and care for children with cancer. Our aim is to systematically review existing literature to identify the factors contributing to delayed childhood cancer care in LMICs. We expect our result will better characterize the barriers and limitations that prevent children from accessing timely cancer care in low-resource settings.

METHODS AND ANALYSIS

2 |

We will use the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines for all processes for this study protocol (Supporting Information Material S1).25 This protocol has been registered on the PROSPERO international prospective registry of systematic reviews with number CRD42021256128.26 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines will inform the reporting of the results of this study.27 Any amendments made to the protocol will be documented and published alongside the systematic review results.

Aim

2.1 |

This study aims to systematically review the literature to identify and evaluate the factors contributing to delayed childhood cancer care in LMICs.

Objectives

2.2 |

  1. To identify the factors contributing to delays across the entire childhood cancer continuum of care in LMICs.

  2. To quantify the delays to access to childhood cancer care in LMICs.

  3. To quantify health system capacity (workforce, infrastructure, and health financing) for childhood cancer care in LMICs.

  4. To critically appraise the quality of the included articles.

  5. To provide an evidence-based summary of the limitations and barriers experienced by children across the cancer continuum of care in LMICs.

Search strategy

2.3 |

We will search a total of 10 electronic databases, including Academic Search Complete (EBSCO), CINAHL (EBSCO), EMBASE, ERIC (EBSCO), Global Health (EBSCO), PubMed, SCOPUS, Web of Science Core Collection, Web of Science MEDLINE, and the WHO Global Index Medicus for all regions, African Index Medicus (AIM), Index Medicus for the Eastern Mediterranean Region (IMEMR), Index Medicus for the South-East Asia Region (IMSEAR), Latin American and the Caribbean Literature on Health Sciences (LILACS), and Western Pacific Region Index Medicus (WPRO), for evidence-based research peer-reviewed studies and gray literature from inception. Gray literature will be searched from the mentioned databases. Additionally, we will search international agencies such as the World Bank Group, Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), and the WHO International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). Search strings will be constructed in compliance with the PICO framework,28 including (1) the Population—children (aged 0–18 years) from LMICs (based on the World Bank classification), (2) the Exposure—factors contributing to timely childhood cancer care, and (3) the Outcome—delays in childhood cancer care (Table 1). The search strategy was developed in PubMed by one investigator (C-CC) under the guidance of a librarian (KC) specialized in public health, medicine, and child studies, and a specialist in childhood cancer in LMICs (KS). We will increase the sensitivity of the search strategy by including a combination of truncations and wildcards as key search terms and through an iterative process of evaluation and revision. No language filters will be applied. We performed a pilot study and applied our search strategy to a single database (PubMed) to evaluate the feasibility of this review. Then, the search strategy was translated to other databases using Polyglot Search Translator29 and revised by the mentioned librarian (Supporting Information Material S2).

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

2.4 |

All studies reporting factors of delays in childhood cancer care in any LMIC (in accordance with the World Bank classification) will be eligible for inclusion. Inclusion criteria will be determined by compliance with all the constructs in the PICO statement. No restrictions regarding language, publication date, outcome effect measure, or quality will be applied. Evidence-based research studies, including observational studies, qualitative studies, abstracts, conference papers, reports, and theses or dissertations, will be eligible for inclusion. We will include gray literature to help mitigate the risks of publication bias. All types of review studies will be excluded. However, their reference lists will be examined for additional studies suitable for inclusion.

Exposures

2.4.1 |

Studies will be eligible for inclusion if they report factors contributing to delays in childhood cancer care. Childhood cancer will be defined as all-inclusive cancers within this age category according to the International Classification of Childhood Cancer 3rd edition (ICCC-3).30 Pediatric cancer care was defined as any step across the entire childhood cancer continuum of care. From our pilot study, we found that traditional medicine is usually considered as a factor that contributes to delays in childhood cancer care. Thus, we will include traditional medicine as an exposure.31,32 Furthermore, we expect to find health system capacity variables (workforce, infrastructure, and health financing) as significant factors of delay.

Setting and participants

2.4.2 |

Childhood cancer is often defined by the literature as cancer occurring in children younger than 15 years old.12 However, based on our pilot search, we will define children as persons up to 18 years old in this review. LMICs were defined according to the World Bank country classification by income level updated to June 2020.33 Studies will be excluded if they have a sample population older than 18 years, mix LMICs and HICs, or mix adults and children in the analysis and a separate analysis for all these cases is not possible.

Outcome measures

2.4.3 |

Our primary outcome will be delays in care, defined as any interruption of time in the access to diagnosis and/or treatment of childhood cancer. However, because of the broad nature of our study, delays in care will not be defined by following an established timeframe specific to a single childhood cancer type. Instead, studies will be eligible for inclusion if they explicitly report delayed access to care, including reports of specific types of cancer or a general approach for all types of childhood cancers. Delays in care will be measured in time and reported in weeks.

Study screening

2.5 |

The studies identified through the electronic databases will be deduplicated, screened, and assessed for eligibility in EPPI reviewer (version 4.12.0.0).34 Two groups of reviewers will independently screen in duplicate the titles and abstracts and assess full-text studies against the eligibility criteria. Articles in languages different than English will be either assessed by a reviewer fluent in that language or translated with Google Translator and verified by an external person fluent in that language. The group will discuss and resolve by consensus any issues that arise during the screening and eligibility processes. If the discrepancies persist, the final decision will be made by another coauthor.

Data extraction

2.6 |

Data from the included studies will be collected in an excel spreadsheet. Data on health system capacity (workforce, infrastructure, and health financing) will be collected quantitatively and with as much detail as possible. The retrieved information will include title, authors, study design, sample size, age, location, year of publication, outcome, outcome measure effects, exposures, study timeframe, measure of delays in receiving care, number of childhood cancer workforce, availability of infrastructure, out-of-pocket expenditure, catastrophic expenditure, and impoverishing expenditure. Qualitative data will be extracted by citation. If possible, categorical variables will be retrieved from these studies. Two reviewers will extract the data from studies that meet inclusion criteria. A third reviewer will independently assess accuracy on a random subsample of 15% of the total number of studies.

Data synthesis

2.7 |

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize the findings and will be generated by using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA) and ArcMap 10.3 (ESRI, Redlands, CA). Qualitative studies will be summarized using a narrative approach. During the analysis, we will collapse the type of cancer into 12 categories according to the ICCC-3 and including leukemias, myeloproliferative diseases, and myelodysplastic diseases; lymphomas and reticuloendothelial neoplasms; CNS and miscellaneous intracranial and intraspinal neoplasms; neuroblastoma and other peripheral nervous cell tumors; retinoblastoma; renal tumors; hepatic tumors; malignant bone tumors; softtissue and other extraosseous sarcomas; germ cell tumors, trophoblastic tumors, and neoplasms of gonads; other malignant epithelial neoplasms and malignant melanomas; and other and unspecified malignant neoplasms. If available, delays in care will be reported in weeks. Studies that report different measures of delay will be converted to weeks.

Methodological quality appraisal and bias assessment

2.8 |

Two independent groups will perform the quality assessment of the studies included in this systematic review. The assessment of quantitative studies will be performed by using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies.35,36 The Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) checklist will be used to assess qualitative studies.37 The Authority, Accuracy, Coverage, Objectivity, Date, Significance checklist (AACODS) will be used to evaluate the gray literature.38 Any issues that arise during the quality assessment process will be discussed by the group and resolved by consensus. If the discrepancies persist, the final decision will be made by another coauthor. The overall strength of evidence will be assessed according to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) approach.

Patient and public involvement

2.9 |

Patient and public involvement (PPI) is not feasible because of the nature of this review that assesses public and secondary data, and therefore PPI will not be included in this study. No individually identifiable health information (IIHI) will be assessed.

DISCUSSION

3 |

Early diagnosis and treatment are essential to improve survival rates for childhood cancers.14–17 Significant barriers and limitations to timely cancer care for children remain in many LMICs and other low-resource settings compared with HICs.2–4 Our study will be the most comprehensive systematic literature review on factors contributing to delays in cancer care for children in LMICs. Our search strategy is unique because it incorporates peer-reviewed articles, gray literature, and quantitative and qualitative studies. Furthermore, including all types of childhood cancers facilitates a complete depiction of the landscape of barriers and limitations across the childhood cancer continuum of care. Publication bias is a risk in systematic review studies. However, we mitigate this risk by including gray literature. We expect this study will serve as the first step to guide international and regional collaborations to assess the factors that impact the access to timely cancer care in LMICs and ultimately improve survival rates in this population.

There are several limitations to our study. Although the search strategy has been designed to be optimally inclusive, there is a possibility of missing articles. For instance, the search strategy was developed in English, and therefore, some studies without an English translation of the title or abstract could be missed. Furthermore, we might miss gray literature from countries where private organizations run national cancer care programs and publish their own data outside the databases and websites included in this review. Another limitation is our definition of childhood in this review. Our pilot search revealed a lack of standardization in the definition of age for this population. Therefore, we defined children as persons up to 18 years old in this review, although the literature often defines childhood cancer as cancer occurring in children younger than 15 years.12

We predict considerable clinical heterogeneity between the studies included in this review. Despite the emphasis on protocolized care for children with cancer in high-resource settings, cancer care in low-resource settings is often based on specific institution or clinician practices. Thus, there is no “one-size-fits-all approach.” Another source of heterogeneity will come from the inclusion of all cancer types. We will try to mitigate heterogeneity through subgroup analysis, but there may be insufficient data available for some outcome assessments. The subgroup analysis will also intend to provide specific results to inform more targeted interventions aiming to reduce the identified delays in the future.

We recognize that restricting our analysis to LMICs as defined by World Bank, which is based on gross national income per capita, does not recognize that the capacity to provide adequate healthcare within any country is dependent on a myriad of factors.39 However, the use of proxy measures of low-resource settings such as LMICs is common practice in global health research. The use of other human development metrics may be more reflective of the underlying complexities that impact access to cancer care within any country, although it remains challenging to account for all social determinants that may impact delays in cancer care, such as proximity to healthcare infrastructure (i.e., rurality), civil conflicts, macro- and microeconomic resources, and nutritional status, among others. We will collect these variables whenever possible and incorporate these into our analysis, but there may not be sufficient data to adjust for all confounding factors.

Ethics statement and dissemination

3.1 |

This systematic review will evaluate public and secondary data, and therefore does not require ethical approval. Our findings will be submitted for open access publication in a peer-reviewed journal. The publication will be disseminated among the members of the Global Initiative for Children’s Surgery, which includes professionals from a wide range of disciplines that advocate and work in all aspects of surgical care for children, including cancer care. We will also disseminate our results among other international organizations, policymakers, representatives from the WHO, and local health champions who are part of our research team in Tanzania, Somaliland, and Ghana. Social media will be used to disseminate our study results to a broader audience. Findings will be presented at international conferences focusing on global health, pediatrics, and cancer care.

We expect that our study will contribute to the momentum created by the Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer. Our results are expected to be used as foundational metrics to national and international stakeholders interested in improving cancer care systems for children in these countries. Next steps based on our findings could include the creation of policy decision tools that serve as frameworks and evaluation instruments as we make progress toward a timely, affordable, and safe cancer care for all children.
