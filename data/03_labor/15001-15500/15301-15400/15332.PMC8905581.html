Chorioamnionitis and Risk for Maternal and Neonatal Sepsis: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

Abstract

Objective:

To estimate the risk of maternal and neonatal sepsis associated with chorioamnionitis.

Data sources:

PubMed, BIOSIS, and clinicaltrials.gov databases were systematically searched for full-text articles in English from inception until May 11, 2020.

Methods of study selection:

We screened 1,251 studies. Randomized controlled trials, case-control, or cohort studies quantifying a relationship between chorioamnionitis and sepsis in mothers (postpartum) or infants born >22 weeks gestation were eligible. Studies were grouped for meta-analyses according to exposures of histological or clinical chorioamnionitis and outcomes of maternal or neonatal sepsis ().

Tabulation, Integration, and Results:

One hundred three studies were included, and 55 met criteria for meta-analysis (39 studies of preterm infants, 10 studies of general populations of preterm and term infants, and 6 studies of late preterm and term infants). Study details and quantitative data were abstracted. Random-effects models were used to generate pooled ORs; most studies only reported unadjusted results. Histological chorioamnionitis was associated with confirmed and any early-onset neonatal sepsis (unadjusted pooled ORs 4.42 (95% CI, 2.68–7.29) and 5.88 (95% CI, 3.68–9.41), respectively). Clinical chorioamnionitis was also associated with confirmed and any early-onset neonatal sepsis (unadjusted pooled ORs 6.82 (95% CI, 4.93–9.45) and 3.90 (95% CI, 2.74–5.55), respectively). Additionally, histological and clinical chorioamnionitis were each associated with higher odds of late-onset sepsis in preterm infants. Confirmed sepsis incidence was 7% (early-onset) and 22% (late-onset) for histological and 6% (early-onset) and 26% (late-onset) for clinical chorioamnionitis exposed infants. Three studies evaluated chorioamnionitis and maternal sepsis and were inconclusive.

Conclusion:

Both histological and clinical chorioamnionitis were associated with early- and late-onset sepsis in neonates. Overall, our findings support current guidelines for preventative neonatal care. There was insufficient evidence to determine the association between chorioamnionitis and maternal sepsis.

Systematic Review Registration:

PROSPERO, CRD42020156812.

Précis

Chorioamnionitis is associated with early-onset and late-onset neonatal sepsis, with limited evidence for term infants or maternal sepsis.

Introduction

Acute chorioamnionitis is a relatively common histologic finding affecting 1–4% of births in the United States.1 Chorioamnionitis, also called intraamniotic infection, can be diagnosed clinically during pregnancy with maternal fever alone (> 39.0°C), or with a temperature of 38.0–38.9°C that persists after 30 minutes and one additional clinical sign of infection such as maternal leukocytosis, purulent cervical discharge or fetal tachycardia.2 The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) advises consideration of antibiotics for suspected intraamniotic infection, and for fever alone if another source of fever cannot be identified. After delivery, histological chorioamnionitis can be diagnosed through microscopic examination of the placenta.1,3 Criteria for diagnosing both clinical2–4 and histological3 chorioamnionitis have evolved, and manifestations of each are not always present simultaneously.1,5

Maternal and neonatal sepsis are a leading cause of death worldwide. During the time period from 2003–2009, sepsis accounted for 10.7% of global maternal deaths.6 While maternal death from sepsis occurs more frequently in developing countries, it is an increasing problem in the United States.6,7 Neonatal sepsis, classified as either early-onset (≤72 hours after birth) or late-onset (>72 hours after birth), remains a significant cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality, particularly in preterm infants and globally.8–12

Chorioamnionitis is currently used as a primary risk factor for identifying infants at risk of early-onset neonatal sepsis (EONS).13,14 However, overexposure of newborns to broad-spectrum antibiotics pending exclusion of EONS, or for “presumed” EONS in the absence of a definitive diagnosis, has potential short and long-term adverse effects such as higher risks of necrotizing enterocolitis and mortality.15,16 Late-onset neonatal sepsis (LONS) is challenging to diagnose due to nonspecific clinical signs, and there are no risk stratification tools to guide clinicians toward a threshold for when to send diagnostic tests or start antibiotics. There is a need to quantify the risk of both early- and late-onset neonatal sepsis following exposure to chorioamnionitis to assist in clinical decision-making.

Our objective was to estimate the risk of maternal and neonatal sepsis following exposure to chorioamnionitis (clinical or histological), including postpartum mothers and preterm and term infants, through a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis.

Methods

We conducted a search of original, peer-reviewed studies reporting an association between chorioamnionitis and sepsis. The review was preregistered using PROSPERO (registration: CRD42020156812). Our study was deemed exempt from institutional review board (IRB) approval by the Pennsylvania State University IRB. We followed Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.18 PubMed, BIOSIS, and clinicaltrials.gov databases were systematically searched from inception until May 11, 2020, using search terms for chorioamnionitis, sepsis, and similar terms including intra-amniotic infection, maternal inflammatory response, or blood poisoning (Appendix 1, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294). Language was restricted to articles in English. We manually searched references of included studies to identify studies missed in database searches.

Four reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full-text articles in pairs so that at least two reviewers screened every search result. In cases of disagreement, discussions took place with an independent third reviewer until an agreement between all three parties was met. Clinical experts were consulted when needed (KG, JG, LM). The web application Rayyan QCRI19 was used to manage articles during screening phases. Studies were included in the systematic review if they met all of the following criteria: 1) study design: randomized controlled trial, case-control, cohort, or cross-sectional; 2) exposure: studies that specified chorioamnionitis as an exposure; 3) outcome: studies that specified sepsis in the mother or newborn as an outcome; and 4) included a quantified measure of risk between chorioamnionitis and sepsis. Articles were excluded if they were conducted in non-human animals or if they included pregnancies that terminated before 22 weeks (due to non-viability). Studies were then included in the meta-analysis if they met the following additional criteria: 1) chorioamnionitis was specified as clinical or histological, and a definition was provided that met prevailing guidelines at the time of original publication; 2) the sepsis outcome was specified as confirmed or presumed; 3) neonatal sepsis was described as early- or late-onset (or the exact timing was reported); and 4) an odds ratio (or relative risk) or the numbers necessary to calculate an odds ratio were provided. Studies were not included in meta-analyses if no sepsis events were reported in both the exposed and non-exposed groups, according to best practice guidelines.20

Three reviewers independently extracted data for included studies (one-third each). Following data extraction, one of the three reviewers acted as a second reviewer by comparing information gathered with information in the original study to verify accuracy. A Microsoft Excel sheet was used to curate the following information for each study: citation details, study design, sample size, participant characteristics, chorioamnionitis and sepsis definitions, and main findings. We abstracted quantitative details including odds ratios or the number of cases and controls with and without chorioamnionitis necessary to calculate an odds ratio If clarification was needed, attempts were made to contact corresponding authors to provide the necessary information. We contacted 24 authors7,21–43 and received clarifying information from six.21,25,29,31,36,43 Any discrepancies in abstracted data between reviewers were discussed until reviewers were in agreement.

Risk of bias was assessed for studies included in the meta-analyses using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale,44 which assesses the following areas: selection (4 items), comparability (1 item), and outcome (3 items). One star is given for each of the criteria met, except for the comparability domain, which may receive up to two stars. The overall score is the total number of stars tabulated, with a maximum score of 9. A higher score indicates a lower risk of bias. All questions in the bias assessment were answered as they related specifically to the chorioamnionitis exposure and sepsis outcome, including studies for which assessment of the relationship between chorioamnionitis and sepsis was not the main research objective. Prior to assessment, the first author and another reviewer discussed criteria, scoring, and application of the tool. The two reviewers then independently assessed bias for three randomly chosen studies (92% agreement; kappa=0.79, z=4.60, p<001), following which discussions took place to identify and resolve points of disagreement. The reviewers subsequently assessed bias for four randomly selected additional studies, resulting in 91% agreement and a kappa coefficient of 0.79 (z=5.72, p<0.001), following which additional discussions took place. Based on the consistency in scoring and kappa scores indicating high interrater agreement,45 the first author assessed bias for the remaining studies.

Due to the considerable heterogeneity across studies, as well as the large number of studies, our preliminary step was to organize studies into subgroups for analysis based on exposure and outcome definitions. We conducted a pooled analysis and created a forest plot for each subgroup for which there were at least three studies with the same outcomes and exposures. Studies that did not meet criteria for meta-analyses are summarized in Appendices 3 and 4.

Histological and clinical chorioamnionitis were analyzed as separate exposures. For purposes of establishing a uniform exposure, histological chorioamnionitis was defined for the meta-analyses as the infiltration of polymorphonuclear leukocytes into the fibrous chorion or amnion.46 We frequently encountered other definitions, including isolated fetal inflammatory response (i.e. funisitis) without concurrent maternal inflammatory response, combined maternal and fetal inflammatory response (i.e. histological chorioamnionitis with funisitis), and subchorionitis. Where studies reported multiple exposures, we used the exposure consistent with our definition. For studies of clinical chorioamnionitis, studies were included in meta-analyses if clinical chorioamnionitis definitions were in accordance with accepted guidelines including maternal fever plus the presence of clinical symptoms.2,4,47 Studies that did not specify if chorioamnionitis was histologically or clinically diagnosed, or for which histological and clinical chorioamnionitis were combined as one exposure, were not included in meta-analyses.

Both neonatal and maternal sepsis were defined based on a positive blood culture (“confirmed”) or on the presence of clinical signs or symptoms (“presumed”). If individual studies used this definition of presumed, but called it “suspected”, we call it presumed for the purposes of this analysis. Studies that used a composite definition of all confirmed or presumed cases were combined with studies reporting all presumed cases for meta-analysis and reported as “any” sepsis. Early-onset and late-onset neonatal sepsis were analyzed as separate outcomes, with early-onset defined as occurring within 72 hours of delivery,13,14 and late-onset occurring >72 hours following delivery.48 Studies that used different time frames, or that did not specify a time frame for sepsis onset, were excluded from neonatal sepsis meta-analyses. Maternal sepsis was defined as occurring postpartum.

Studies were classified according to gestational age groups of the infant populations included in the studies. We used standard definitions: term (≥37 weeks), preterm (<37 weeks), late preterm (≥34 weeks but <37 weeks), very preterm (<32 weeks), and extremely preterm (<28 weeks).49 Studies that included only low birth weight infants <1500 grams, but that did not report gestational age at delivery, were considered to be preterm.50

For publications that did not provide an unadjusted odds ratio, we calculated it using raw numbers for chorioamnionitis and sepsis as reported in the individual manuscripts. We used random-effects DerSimonian and Laird models to generate pooled odds ratios (OR) for the association between chorioamnionitis and sepsis, to create forest plots, and to generate pooled incidence estimates for each exposure and outcome. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. An I2 statistic of 30–60% was considered to reflect moderate heterogeneity between studies, and I2>60% was interpreted to reflect high heterogeneity.51

We restricted neonatal studies to those in preterm only infants to compare results to the main findings that included studies of general populations of combined preterm and term infants, as well as infant populations comprised of late preterm or term only infants. The number of term only infant studies was too small to conduct restricted analysis.

Publication bias was assessed through inspection and interpretation of a contour-enhanced funnel plot of standard errors with the log odds ratio.52 We used symmetry of effect sizes upon visual inspection as an indication that publication bias was unlikely. Eggers regression was used to test for funnel plot asymmetry when there were at least 10 studies included in a meta-analysis.53,54

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our findings to the influence of individual studies. We removed studies one at a time and reestimated the pooled ORs. We also used fixed effects models to estimate pooled ORs for studies with an I2 value <30%.

Stata version 16 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX) was used to conduct the meta-analyses.

Results

Figure 1 illustrates the study selection process. 1,251 articles were identified from the database and manual reference searches, following duplicate removal. Following title and abstract screening and full article review, 103 articles were included in the qualitative synthesis. Fifty-five articles met criteria for inclusion in meta-analyses. Multiple studies included more than one outcome or exposure and were therefore included in more than one of the subgroup meta-analyses.

Appendix 2 (available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294) describes characteristics for the 55 studies included in the meta-analysis, representing 214,194 infants in 24 countries. Four case-control, 28 prospective cohort, 21 retrospective cohort, and two cohort studies as secondary analyses of randomized controlled trials were included. Fourteen investigations were multi-site studies. The years during which studies were conducted ranged from 1980–2017 (five studies did not specify the years for which their studies were conducted). Sample size, representing the number of infants in the studies for whom chorioamnionitis and sepsis data were available, ranged from 28 to 145,065.

Thirty-nine studies included only preterm infants (<37 weeks gestation), 10 included general populations of preterm and term infants, and six studies included a combination of late preterm and term infants. The range of reported mean gestational age and birth weight for the studies was 26.9–39.3 weeks and 930–3325 grams, respectively. Data on maternal characteristics were not reported in most publications.

Appendices 3 and 4 (available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294) describe characteristics of neonatal and maternal sepsis studies that were included in the systematic review, but that did not meet criteria for inclusion in the meta-analysis. Neonatal sepsis studies included 31 studies of preterm only infants, eight studies of combined preterm and term infant populations, and five studies of late preterm or term infants; studies were conducted in 24 different countries. Maternal sepsis studies were comprised of two small studies (conducted in the United States55 and Nigeria,36 respectively) and a retrospective case-control study using a large, population-based database consisting of 5 million births in the United States.7

Study quality criteria and scores for studies included in the meta-analysis are displayed in Appendices 5 and 6 (available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294). The average score was six with scores ranging from four to nine. One of the main sources of bias was in the representativeness of the exposed cohort or cases. Most studies included infants who were preterm only, and within a narrowly specified gestational age range. Only 18% of the studies assessed chorioamnionitis and sepsis in infants of all gestational ages greater than 22 weeks.56–65 Studies of histological chorioamnionitis were subject to selection bias, as study cohorts were selected based on the availability of placental pathology assessed during routine clinical care, representing a sub-population of infants for whom placental pathology was available. An additional source of bias was in lack of adequacy of follow-up for cohort studies, or non-response rate for case-control studies. Of the cohort studies, 67% either had inadequate or no description of follow-up of infants to assess sepsis. For case-control studies, 75% had an insufficient or unreported response rate for assessment of chorioamnionitis exposure. Finally, lack of comparability of cohorts or cases provided a source of bias in approximately half of the studies. Only 49% of the studies included matching in their study design or assessment of potential confounders in their analyses.

Histological chorioamnionitis increased odds of confirmed EONS by 4.42 (95% CI, 2.68–7.29) and any confirmed or presumed EONS by 5.88 (95% CI, 3.68–9.41) in pooled analyses of unadjusted odds ratios from 27 studies (Figure 2). Heterogeneity between studies was lower for confirmed EONS (I2=39%) compared to any EONS (I2=77%). Sample size ranged from 36 to 1,296 for confirmed sepsis studies, and from 28 to 3,417 for studies of any confirmed or presumed sepsis.

Two studies of histological chorioamnionitis and confirmed EONS,65,66 and four studies of histological chorioamnionitis and any confirmed or presumed EONS,28,67–69 adjusted for potential confounders in multivariable models (Appendix 7, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294). Pietrasanta et al. and Lau et al. reported higher adjusted odds of confirmed EONS among infants born to mothers with concurrent histological chorioamnionitis and fetal inflammation.65,66 All four studies of histological chorioamnionitis and any EONS reported higher adjusted odds of EONS following exposure to histological chorioamnionitis.28,67–69

Eleven studies of confirmed EONS and histological chorioamnionitis were in preterm infants, 5 of which were conducted in very preterm infants;68,71–74 one study included term and preterm.65 For presumed EONS, one of the studies was in term infants only,70 and the remaining 13 were in preterm infants, four of which were very preterm.68,75–77 Odds ratios for studies for the two individual studies that included term infants were similar to pooled odds ratios (Figure 2). The overall pooled incidence of confirmed EONS among all infants with histological chorioamnionitis was 7% (95% CI, 5–9%), but the incidence in the study including term infants was 3% (95% CI, 2–6%). The overall pooled incidence of any EONS among all infants with histological chorioamnionitis was 44% (95% CI, 30–59%). While the incidence in the term infant study was 11% (95% CI, 8–14%), cases of EONS occurred exclusively in infants sent to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), and incidence among these term infants in the NICU with histological chorioamnionitis was 40%.70

Twenty-two studies evaluated the association between clinical chorioamnionitis and confirmed EONS (Figure 3). Eleven included preterm infants only, seven included a general population of all deliveries (preterm and term infants), and four included only infants that were late preterm or term (≥34 weeks). The overall unadjusted pooled odds ratio was 6.82 (95% CI, 4.93–9.45). Unadjusted pooled odds ratios for general infant populations and populations of only late preterm or term infants were similar in magnitude. For all infant groups combined, the pooled unadjusted incidence of confirmed EONS in infants exposed to clinical chorioamnionitis was 6% (95% CI, 5–8%); in subgroup analyses, the incidence was 11% (95% CI, 7–14%) in preterm infants, 3% (95% CI, 1–5%) in the general infant population, and 0.8% (95% CI, 0–2%) in late preterm and term infant groups.

High heterogeneity was found between all studies (I2=75%), with the highest variation between studies of general populations of preterm and term infants (I2=79%). Sample size ranged from 252 to 15,839 for preterm infant only studies, from 36 to 145,065 for general populations of preterm and term infants, and from 812 to 31,112 for late preterm and term only infant studies.

Nine studies (6 preterm only) used multivariable regression models to assess the relationship between clinical chorioamnionitis and confirmed EONS independent of confounders (Appendix 8, http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294). Alam et al., who studied an infant population of preterm and term infants, reported an odds ratio for EONS that was no longer statistically different from 1.0 following adjustment for potential confounders.64 While most (but not all) point estimates for the other eight studies were slightly attenuated after adjustments, all reported statistically significant adjusted odds ratios.29,40,57,63,78–81

Fifteen studies examined the association between clinical chorioamnionitis and any confirmed or presumed EONS (Figure 4). Eight of the studies included only preterm infants,62,67,82–87 four studies included preterm and term infants,31,57,59,60 and three studies included late preterm and term infants only.88–90 The overall pooled odds ratio indicated 3.90 (95% CI, 2.74–5.55) times the odds of developing any sepsis. The pooled incidence of any EONS among infants exposed to clinical chorioamnionitis was 38% (95% CI, 22–55%) among preterm, 26% (95% CI, 11–40%) among preterm and term, and 14% (95% CI, 0–32%) among late preterm and term groups. Variation between studies of preterm infants was low (I2=20%) compared to studies of general populations of preterm and term infants (I2=76%) and late preterm and term infant studies (I2=79%). Sample size ranged from 28 to 2,192 for studies of preterm only infants, from 317 to 9,391 in general infant population studies, and from 399 to 1,385 in late preterm and term infant studies.

Six studies considered additional confounding factors in multivariable models assessing the relationship between presumed EONS in relation to clinical chorioamnionitis (Appendix 8, http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294).42,57,59,67,84,85 After adjustment for potential confounders, all but one67 found significantly higher odds of EONS following exposure to clinical chorioamnionitis.

Nine studies evaluated histological chorioamnionitis in relation to confirmed LONS (Figure 5). All studies of confirmed LONS were in preterm infants, with an unadjusted pooled odds ratio of 1.75 (95% CI, 1.14–2.68).66,92,93 The unadjusted pooled incidence of confirmed LONS across studies was 22% (95% CI, 16–29%) among infants exposed to histological chorioamnionitis and 16% (95% CI, 9–23%) among non-exposed infants. High heterogeneity existed between studies (I2=69%). Sample size ranged from 90 to 838, representing a total of 3,087 infants across studies. Multivariable models were used in two studies, both of preterm infants, to assess the relationship between histological chorioamnionitis and confirmed LONS (Appendix 9, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294). Mu et al. reported an odds ratio of 3.36 (95% CI, 1.28–8.83) after accounting for gestational age.92 In contrast, Pietrasanta et al. found no association between histological chorioamnionitis and LONS after adjusting for gestational age and six additional factors.66 For presumed LONS, there was an insufficient number of studies for pooled analysis.31,91

Six studies evaluated clinical chorioamnionitis in relation to confirmed LONS (Figure 6); all were in preterm infants. Unadjusted pooled odds ratios indicated that preterm infants exposed to clinical chorioamnionitis had 1.35 (95% CI, 1.11–1.64) times the odds of developing confirmed LONS compared to infants not exposed to clinical chorioamnionitis. The overall unadjusted incidence across studies was 26% (95% CI, 17–35%) in the infant group exposed to clinical chorioamnionitis, compared to 22% (95% CI, 14–30%) in the infant group not exposed to clinical chorioamnionitis. High heterogeneity (I2=68%) was found between studies. Sample size ranged from 251 to 8,330, representing a total of 20,546 preterm infants in pooled analyses. One study used multivariable modeling to adjust for potential confounders, and reported no association between histological chorioamnionitis and confirmed LONS.79 Only two studies examined this exposure with presumed LONS, therefore these results were not pooled.31,57

Results of neonatal sepsis studies that did not meet criteria for meta-analyses are shown in Appendix 3 (http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294).

Three studies assessed chorioamnionitis in relation to maternal sepsis s (Appendix 4, http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294)7,36,55 An et al. followed up with 107 women for 6 weeks following delivery to ascertain the development of maternal sepsis in relation to histological chorioamnionitis. Women reported to the hospital for sepsis evaluation if they experienced symptoms (criteria for sepsis diagnosis were not described). Researchers found no association between maternal sepsis and histological chorioamnionitis (unadjusted OR 0.56 (95% CI, 0.08–4.15).36 Garite et al., who conducted a study of 251 women with premature rupture of membranes, reported no association between clinical chorioamnionitis and maternal sepsis, though diagnostic criteria and time frames for sepsis diagnosis were not described (unadjusted OR 2.20 (95% CI, 0.19–24.73).55 Al-Ostad et al. conducted a retrospective case-control study using a large, population-based database consisting of 5 million births in the United States. Sepsis and chorioamnionitis were identified using ICD-9 codes. Women with sepsis were reported to have higher odds of having had chorioamnionitis, compared to women without sepsis (unadjusted OR 12.60 (95% CI, 11.08–14.32)), even after adjusting for demographics and pregnancy and labor outcomes (adjusted OR 11.53 (95% CI, 10.11–13.14)).

Visual inspection of contour-enhanced funnel plots suggested a potential for publication bias for studies of histological chorioamnionitis and confirmed EONS and for studies of histological chorioamnionitis and any EONS (Appendix 10, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/C294), which was supported by significant Egger’s regression tests (p=0.004 and 0.007, respectively). Visual inspection of funnel plots suggested a low risk for publication bias for remaining studies, supported by non-significant Egger’s regression tests. When we evaluated the effects of individual studies by removing each study from analysis one by one and recalculating pooled ORs, we found similar results. Re-calculation of pooled effect estimates using fixed effects models also resulted in similar estimates.

Discussion

Exposure to histological chorioamnionitis or clinical chorioamnionitis during pregnancy was associated with 3–6 fold increased odds of EONS. Subgroup analyses showed that the odds of EONS following exposure to clinical chorioamnionitis were similarly increased in preterm, combined preterm and term, and late preterm and term infant populations. However absolute risk for sepsis in exposed groups was 11% among preterm infants, compared to 3% in preterm and term, and 0.8% in late preterm and term infants. Exposure to clinical chorioamnionitis or histological chorioamnionitis was associated with 1.35–1.75 times the odds of LONS among preterm infants. Overall, studies including term infants were few. Evidence for a relationship between chorioamnionitis and maternal sepsis was limited and inconclusive.

Chorioamnionitis has previously been linked to a higher risk of neonatal sepsis in preterm infants17 as well as several other adverse outcomes (e.g. patent ductus arteriosis,123 necrotizing enterocolitis,124 and intraventricular hemorrhage125). Our findings regarding the relationship between histological chorioamnionitis, clinical chorioamnionitis, and confirmed EONS from preterm infant studies were similar in magnitude to those from a meta-analysis conducted by Villamor-Martinez et al.17 However, our findings of an association between histological chorioamnionitis and any EONS were in contrast to non-significant findings for histological chorioamnionitis and presumed sepsis from the other meta-analysis. Reasons for a difference in findings could include that most of our studies reported a composite definition of any confirmed or presumed sepsis. In addition, five of the eight studies included in the other meta-analysis were excluded in ours due including infants <22 weeks gestation,126 or using histological chorioamnionitis100,127 or sepsis112,113 definitions that did not meet our criteria. We also included 11 additional studies.62,67,69,70,76,77,83,86,128–130

The strengths of our study include delineation of histological chorioamnionitis from clinical chorioamnionitis and EONS from LONS to provide more specific, focused results. Further, we performed sub-analyses to parse out effects of confirmed only versus presumed neonatal sepsis, and effects for preterm only versus combined preterm and term infant populations. We demonstrated robustness of findings through sensitivity analyses.

Our study had several limitations. The majority of studies only provided results for unadjusted ORs without the consideration of confounding factors. For studies of histological chorioamnionitis, selection bias was inherent due to the fact that only infants who had placental pathology available were included. This may have resulted in cohorts of infants who were predisposed to develop sepsis following premature delivery or adverse labor and delivery events, and therefore results may over-estimate neonatal sepsis rates. Though a large number of studies evaluating neonatal sepsis were conducted, most were lacking maternal characteristics so we could not determine if risk is altered based on baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. The relationship between chorioamnionitis and maternal sepsis could not be evaluated given the lack of available publications. Future research in this area is particularly important with the high rates of maternal mortality in the US compared to other developed countries.7

Historically, there has been variable practice in the diagnosis of clinical chorioamnionitis, unknown specific risk of EONS following chorioamnionitis, and awareness of potential overtreatment of infants born to mothers with chorioamnionitis with antibiotics.13,14 In light of these clinical uncertainties, this meta-analysis provides evidence of a significantly increased risk of EONS in the setting of clinical or histologic chorioamnionitis, particularly in preterm infants. For preterm infants born to mothers with clinical chorioamnionitis, our findings support the recommendation to evaluate for infection and initiate empiric antibiotic therapy until culture results are available. Current obstetric guidelines recommend intrapartum antibiotics for suspected or confirmed chorioamnionitis.2 While analysis of intrapartum antibiotic administration including timing and duration was beyond the scope of this study, one identified study reported a decreased risk of maternal death from sepsis when chorioamnionitis was diagnosed.7 The authors postulated that this result was likely related to initiation of antibiotic therapy.

Histological chorioamnionitis was also found to be associated with EONS. However, the clinical utility of this finding is limited by lack of immediate availability of pathology results. Furthermore, placental pathology is not uniformly available across hospital settings. Our group is working toward development of a rapid point of care proxy for histological chorioamnionitis that could be used on all placentas immediately after delivery to inform clinical management.131,132

While there is significant overlap in the diagnosis of clinical and histological chorioamnionitis, it is not always concurrent. This may be due to the presence of subclinical chorioamnionitis (which would be identified upon placental examination but remain undiagnosed in the clinical setting) or due to the use of non-specific clinical signs in the diagnosis of clinical chorioamnionitis.1 The overlap between clinical and histological chorioamnionitis and the risk for sepsis was beyond the scope of this study and could be investigated in future research.

We found extensive evidence that histological and clinical chorioamnionitis are associated with early-onset neonatal sepsis. Our findings also suggest a small association between chorioamnionitis and late-onset neonatal sepsis. Studies of maternal sepsis were few and inconclusive. Overall, findings support current guidelines for preventative neonatal care.
