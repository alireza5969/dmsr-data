Effect of Peripheral Neuropathy on Bone Mineral Density in Adults with Diabetes: A Systematic Review of the Literature and Meta-analysis

Mahshid Mohseni[1]

Abstract

Introduction:

Peripheral neuropathy occurs in two thirds of patients with diabetes mellitus (DM). It can lead to severe pathological changes in the feet, and it increases the risk of fracture more than any other diabetic complication. The objective of this review is to analyze available literature on the effect of peripheral neuropathy on BMD of the foot, spine, or hip. We hypothesize that the presence of diabetic neuropathy leads to lower BMD in adults with diabetes.

Methods:

Original studies investigating the effects of diabetic neuropathy on bone density were searched for inclusion in this systematic review. Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: 1) participants included adults with either Type 1 DM or Type 2 DM; 2) Method used for the diagnosis of neuropathy described in the manuscript 3) DXA scan, ultrasound, or CT scan was used to measure proximal femur, spine, or foot bone mineral density were reported, and 4) bone parameters were analyzed based on the presence and absence of neuropathy.

Results:

Among the 5 studies that met eligibility criteria, 4 did not find a significant effect of neuropathy on BMD. One study showed a significant negative impact of neuropathy on calcaneal BMD in patients with type 1 diabetes. The meta-analysis did not show a significant effect of peripheral neuropathy on BMDs of proximal femur, spine, and calcaneus in diabetic adults.

Conclusion:

Our study shows no evidence that peripheral neuropathy affects bone density or bone turnover in DM. However, this conclusion should be taken with caution since only a very limited number of studies were available for inclusion in the analysis and included both type 1 and type 2 DM patients. Improved measures of peripheral neuropathy and more advanced imaging technologies are needed to better assess the effect of diabetes on bone health.

Introduction

1.

Peripheral neuropathy is reported in two thirds of patients with diabetes mellitus (DM). Diabetic peripheral neuropathy is the main cause of Charcot osteoarthropathy, a disorder associated with progressive destruction of bones and joints in the feet. It is well known that bone and nervous system interact; neurotransmitter receptors are present on bone cells and afferent sensory fibers directly innervate them (1-4). Both type 1 DM (T1DM) and type 2 DM (T2DM) increase the risk of fragility fractures (5, 6), but the mechanisms of such negative effect of DM remains largely unknown. In a recent large-scale analysis of US Veterans records a significant portion of the 22% higher fracture risk among subjects with DM was mediated by DM-related complications, particularly peripheral neuropathy (7). Consistent with these findings, experimentally induced peripheral neuropathy reduces bone mass in rats (4). Nonetheless, the question remains as to whether peripheral neuropathy affects bone mineral density (BMD) and/or bone turnover. Understanding the effect of this common diabetes complication on bone health is crucial, and it could inform future therapies for reducing fragility fractures in patients with DM ultimately improving clinical outcomes.

The objective of this meta-analysis was to systematically review available literature to test the hypothesis that the presence of diabetic neuropathy is associated with lower BMD in adults with DM. Results do not support such association, but studies meeting the inclusion criteria for analysis are very limited, and results should not be considered conclusive.

Materials and Methods

2.

Study Eligibility:

2.1.

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: 1) participants included adults (> 18 years of age) with either T1DM or T2DM; 2) Method used for the diagnosis of neuropathy described in the manuscript 3) Measurement of bone density proximal femur, lumbar spine, or calcaneous by DXA, ultrasound, or CT-based methods, and 4) BMD and when available bone markers were analyzed based on the presence and absence of neuropathy.

Search Protocol

2.2.

Original studies investigating the effects of diabetic neuropathy on bone density were searched for inclusion in this systematic review. Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, we performed a detailed systematic biomedical literature search in PubMed/MEDLINE (1946-present), EMBASE (1947-preseent),SCOPUS (1823-present), Web of Science (WOS, 1900-present), The Cochrane Library and Clinicaltrials.gov from inception through to March 24, 2020.The search strategy was conducted using both the controlled vocabulary of each database and plain language keywords for the terms: Bone Density, Diabetes Mellitus, Diabetes Mellitus, Type 1, Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2, "Latent Autoimmune Diabetes in Adults", "Diabetic Neuropathies" and "Neuropathy" limited to English language and to age eighteen and older. Titles and abstracts of retrieved publications were reviewed for eligibility by two investigators independently (MM and PH). Full-text versions of potentially eligible publications were obtained and reviewed for inclusion. All studies of human participants of any study type were considered. Review articles, case studies, theses, commentaries, and unpublished meeting abstracts were excluded.

Data Collection

2.3.

Data extraction was performed using a standard form including author, year, publication title, location of the study, participant characteristics (age, sex, diabetes type, and BMI), measures of diabetes control, sample size, measure used to establish neuropathy, technique and location of bone density testing, bone markers if evaluated, statistical analysis performed and outcomes. Main variables were bone density, bone markers and the neuropathy status.

Data Analysis

2.4.

Meta-analysis was performed to assess the strength of the relationship between bone density and bone markers with diabetic neuropathy. Differences in bone density were reported as standardized mean differences and expressed as g/cm2, T-score, and Z-score. Differences in bone markers were reported as standardized mean differences in the unit reported. A random effects model was used for the meta-analysis to account for the study designs. Heterogeneity was examined using I2 statistic and publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot. STATA software (Stata Corp, College Station, TX) was used for statistical analysis.

We used Critical Appraisal Skills Program tool for case control studies (casp-uk.net/casp-tools-checklists) to assess the quality of the studies in our meta-analysis which includes 11 items related to definition of study population, likelihood of bias, accuracy of outcome measures and appropriateness of statistical analysis. We also used to the 8 question tool to assess risk of bias in cohort studies by the Clarity Group at McMaster University. No minimum quality standard was required for inclusion in this systematic review.

Results

3.

The results of the search strategy are summarized in the flow diagram in Fig. 1, following PRISMA guidelines. Our broad search strategy resulted in 6,688 abstracts that were reviewed by two authors separately for their eligibility. Of those, 6,570 were excluded because the studies did not meet the eligibility criteria. The remaining 118 abstracts were further reviewed, and case reports, review articles, unpublished meeting abstracts, and manuscripts not in English language were excluded. Ultimately, 55 manuscripts were fully reviewed and 6 studies met all the criteria for our systematic review. Among these 6, one study was further excluded from data analysis because it was the only one using CT-based bone density measurement: the remaining 5 studies were used for the meta-analysis, and the demographic data are shown in Table 1.

Study Outcomes

3.1.

Among the 5 studies that met all eligibility criteria, 4 did not find a significant effect of neuropathy on BMD; one showed a significant negative impact on calcaneal BMD in patients with T1DM. Three studies reported bone turnover markers in diabetic patients with and without neuropathy; in 2 of those studies, bone turnover markers were higher in the group with neuropathy relative to the group without neuropathy.

Greenhagen et al. (8) measured BMD of the spine and calcaneus in diabetic patients with and without Charcot arthropathy (and a small group of non-diabetic patients) expressed as T-score, and found no difference between the two groups. However, this study did not report measures of diabetes control, type of diabetes, or other associated comorbidities.

In a cross-sectional study, Piaggesi et al. (9) measured BMD by DXA and bone turnover markers in 66 patients including a group with both T1DM and T2DM and a control group. They used Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instruments (MNSI) for diagnosis of neuropathy and used HbA1C as a measure of diabetes control. There was no difference between BMD of the lumbar spine, proximal femur, distal forearm, or calcaneus between diabetic patients with and without neuropathy. Serum deoxypyridinoline cross-links, a bone resorption marker, was higher in the group with peripheral neuropathy, but other bone turnover markers were not different between the two groups.

Rasul et al. (10) studied hip and spine BMD and bone turnover markers in 120 subjects with T1DM or T2DM, with and without neuropathy. They used monofilament test and nerve conduction velocity for the diagnosis of peripheral neuropathy and reported HbA1C as a measure of diabetes control. Again, no difference in BMD between subjects with or without neuropathy was found. However, procollagen type I N-terminus propeptides (P1NP) and type I collagen telopeptide cross-links (CTx), markers of bone formation and resorption, respectively, were higher in the neuropathic group relative to the group without peripheral neuropathy.

In a cross-sectional study of 42 adults with type 1 diabetes (21 with neuropathy and 21 without neuropathy), Rix et al. (11) used clinical history, physical examination, and biothesometry (to determine vibration perception threshold) for diagnosis of neuropathy and reported HbA1C as a measure of diabetes control. The authors found lower BMD (lumbar spine and proximal femur) in the group with neuropathy, though the difference did not reach statistical significance; whereas calcaneal BMD (by ultrasound) was significantly lower in the neuropathy group. Finally, Christensen et al. (12) found no differences in BMD of proximal femur, spine, or calcaneus between subjects with and without Charcot foot arthropathy in a cross-sectional study of 20 patients with T1DM and T2DM.

Quality Assessment

3.2.

Answers to the Critical Appraisal Skills Program form for all studies are provided in Table 2. Answers to the tool to assess risk of bias in cohort studies by Clarity Group at McMaster University is reported in Table 3. Sample sizes ranged from 20 to 120, with power analysis reported in only one study (11). In 3 studies, participants were recruited from local health services and diabetes clinics, and their databases (9-11). The method used to diagnose diabetes was not reported in any of the studies. Criteria used to define neuropathy varied widely among the studies, most used a combination of tests. Accuracy and reproducibility of the neuropathy measurements were not described in any study, and none reported blinding of assessors with respect to outcomes of interest. Diabetes duration and measures of diabetes control were reported in three studies (9-11). BMD reporting varied, with some studies presenting mean and SD of areal bone density (in g/cm2), others only showing T- or Z-scores. Bone turnover markers reported also varied among studies. Only two studies controlled for potential confounding variables, such as BMI and comorbidities (9, 10). The five studies included in the meta-analysis had a total sample size 271 patients.

Meta-analysis Results

3.3.

We found no significant effect of diabetic neuropathy on total hip bone mineral density [effect size −0.25 (−1.83, 1 .33); Figure 2). The I2 (96.97%) showed significant heterogeneity between the studies; and there was no apparent asymmetry in the Funnel plot (Figure 3), indicating lack of significant publication bias in our meta-analysis. Furthermore, we found no significant effect of neuropathy on lumbar spine BMD in the diabetic population [effect size 0.15 (−1.31, 1.01); Figure 4]; and no effect of neuropathy on calcaneal bone mineral density [effect size −0.32 (−1.86, 1.22); Figure 5]. Likewise, there were no differences in bone turnover markers between patients with and without diabetic neuropathy [effect size −0.08 (−0.36, 0.21); Figure 6].

Discussion

4.

Our meta-analysis of 5 studies, which included a total of 271 subjects demonstrated no effect of peripheral neuropathy on BMD or bone turnover markers in adults with diabetes. The results are limited by the high I2 indicating poor consistency among studies included in this meta-analysis, which is likely caused by different criteria used for neuropathy diagnosis and heterogeneity in BMD reporting. Inability to determine the independent effect of neuropathy may also represent a potential source of bias in this study. Age, sex, ethnicity, body weight, renal function, menopausal status, smoking, physical activity, duration and control of diabetes and co-morbid conditions are all possible contributors to BMD. In two of the studies included in our analysis, these confounding variables were managed by using participant matching or statistical analysis (9, 10). Of the five studies in our meta-analysis, only one study analyzed the effect of weight (10). Weight has the potential to mask the bone loss associated with neuropathy since higher weight is associated both with higher BMD (13) and the presence of neuropathy (14). Our results are consistent with a published meta-analysis that also reported no effect of neuropathy on foot BMD (15). However, that analysis was limited to the effect of neuropathy on foot BMD, and included studies that categorized BMD as normal, osteopenia, and osteoporosis, whereas we selected studies where quantitative BMD data were available.

Increased skeletal fragility is seen in both types of diabetes, and neuropathy has been linked to increased fracture risk (16, 17). However, while BMD is typically reduced in T1DM, it is normal or even increased in T2DM (17), suggesting that other factors account for the increased skeletal fragility in T2DM. Compromised bone material quality or increased cortical bone porosity have been proposed as potential mechanisms. As T1DM and type 2DM are associated with different effects on BMD a potential effect of peripheral neuropathy on BMD may not emerge in studies where patients with both types of diabetes are included, as in most of the studies we analyzed. Independent of BMD, other factors such as impaired balance and muscle wasting (sarcopenia) could also contribute to the increased risk of fractures in patients with diabetes and diabetic complications.

Limitations in the imaging techniques could also limit our understanding of the effect of neuropathy on BMD. In most studies, quantitative ultrasound techniques or DXA have been used, but data are reported as either areal bone density (in gr/cm2) or T- or Z-scores, thus adding another source of variability. Furthermore, DXA does not assess bone quality or strength, while diabetes mainly affects bone microarchitecture or bone material properties rather than bone density. Therefore, DXA may underestimate the effect of neuropathy on bone quality and strength in the diabetic population. Advanced imaging techniques, such as high resolution peripheral computed tomography, may aid in better assessment of the effect of neuropathy on bone geometry and strength in this population.

In summary, this meta-analysis did not reveal any effect of peripheral neuropathy on BMD or bone turnover in diabetes. However, this result should be taken with caution since the studies available for inclusion in the analysis were very limited and included both T1DM and T2DM. Future studies should use validated measures to define neuropathy and more advanced imaging technologies to better capture the effect of diabetes on bone health, ideally in a prospective fashion and in subjects with T1DM and T2DM independently.
