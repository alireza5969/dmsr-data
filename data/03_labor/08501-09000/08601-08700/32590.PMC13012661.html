Abstract

Objective:

IgG4-Related Disease (IgG4-RD) is an inflammatory process that uncommonly can present in the skull base and calvarium and mimic a tumor but the nature of this condition is not well summarized in the neurosurgical literature.

Methods:

A review of 2 cases of IgG4-RD in the skull base highlighting the diagnostic challenges with assessment of these skull base lesions was performed, and a systematic review of relevant literature summarized.

Results:

A systematic review of literature conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines identified 113 publications with 184 cases of IgG4-RD in the skull base or calvarium. The most commonly affected locations include the meninges, cavernous sinus, base of the posterior fossa, clivus, and mastoid bone. Headache, visual and auditory disturbances, cranial nerve dysfunction, and seizures were the most common presenting symptoms. Medical treatment was highly successful, and most commonly consisted of corticosteroids co-administered with immunosuppressive agents such as rituximab. Prevalence appeared to be equal between sexes, and serum IgG4 levels were elevated in 61% of cases. Delayed diagnosis and a need for multiple biopsies was reported in numerous cases.

Two cases of skull base IgG4-RD from the authors institution demonstrate the variable presentations of this disease. More invasive surgical biopsies were required in both cases, and corticosteroid treatment led to significant clinical improvement.

Conclusion:

IgG4-RD is an uncommon condition with a growing body of reported cases that can affect the skull base and calvarium and should be in the differential diagnosis as delay in diagnosis and treatment may be common.

INTRODUCTION

IgG4-Related Disease (IgG4-RD) was first described in 2001 associated with autoimmune pancreatitis1. Recent investigations have identified IgG4-RD in nearly every anatomical region of the body including the skull base, calvarium, and meninges2–5. IgG4-RD is most prevalent in older males. However, recent data suggests that manifestations in the head and neck show a similar prevalence between males and females4,6. This disease primarily affects adults, but pediatric cases have been reported7–10.

IgG4-RD has been documented in many skull base locations including meninges, sphenoid bone, temporal bone, sphenoid and maxillary sinuses, clivus, intracranial blood vessels, internal auditory canal, trigeminal nerve, brain parenchyma, cavernous sinus, infratemporal fossa, pterygopalatine fossa, and suprasellar region. Lesions are often confined to the skull base and calvarium; however, concurrent spinal or systemic involvement has been reported9,11–16

Many inflammatory disorders in the head and neck including Küttner tumor, Mikulicz’s disease, Riedel thyroiditis, and Hashimoto thyroiditis have been recognized as manifestations of IgG4-RD3,4,6,17–19. IgG4-RD has also been implicated as a cause of inflammatory pseudotumor and hypertrophic pachymeningitis2,3,20–26. Given their similar presentations, various central nervous system tumors are frequently in the differential diagnosis of skull base IgG4-RD27.

Original diagnostic criteria for IgG4-RD centered around the histological features of IgG4+ lymphocytic infiltrate, obliterative phlebitis, and storiform fibrosis18. Various blood markers such as serum IgG4 can aid in a diagnosis, but are not sensitive nor specific for IgG4-RD2,18 and the exact role of IgG4 levels in the disease pathophysiology remain unclear. Guidelines have from the American College of Rheumatology shifted to a point-based system in which a diagnosis of IgG4-RD is based on a spectrum of classic features and the absence of other plausible diagnoses (Table 1)28. In developing these criteria, however, skull base manifestations of IgG4-RD were underrepresented compared to other head and neck manifestations, likely owing to the scarcity of consolidated knowledge of skull base IgG4-RD. As a result of these ambiguities and the uncommon nature of the disease, diagnosis of IgG4-RD in the skull base or calvarium can be a challenge and requires biopsy of the affected tissues.

The nature of IgG4-RD in the skull base and calvarium is not well summarized in the current literature. The objective of this review is to create a consolidated account of skull base IgG4-RD and increase awareness of its clinical presentations, treatment modalities, and streamline the often-prolonged clinical course of its affected patients.

METHODS

Data regarding two patients IgG4-RD in the skull base from a single center was extracted from an institutional review board (IRB) approved multicenter database, the IMRIS Muliticenter intraoperative MRI Neurosurgery Database (I-MiND), that has been used to study outcomes for patients undergoing neurosurgical procedures for brain tumors and other conditions29–38. Data were collected and managed via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap)39 servers. Patient consent was not obtained as all data was de-identified. A systematic review of relevant literature on IgG4-RD of the skull-base and calvarium was performed.

Literature Review Protocol and Inclusion Criteria

A detailed literature search was performed with the assistance of a medical librarian (LHY) including the terms of IgG4 and skull, skull base, and cranial. A search was created using a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary in Embase.com 1947-, Ovid Medline 1946-, Scopus 1823-, The Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and clinicaltrials.gov. All search strategies were completed July 13, 2020 with no added database supplied filters or limits to find a total of 1,830 results. 478 duplicate records were deleted after using the de-duplication processes described in “De-duplication of database search results for systematic reviews in EndNote,”40 resulting in a total of 1,352 unique citations included in the project library. Fully reproducible search strategies for each database can be found in the appendix.

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines including adherence to the 27 item PRISMA checklist for systematic reviews41.

Of 1352 initial sources, 995 were excluded due to content irrelevant to IgG4-RD in the skull base or calvarium. 92 summary articles were excluded due to a lack of primary data on specific cases of IgG4-RD. 144 sources described IgG4-RD in the head and neck, but did not involve the skull base or calvarium and were excluded. Cases that were limited to the eye and orbit and those that were exclusively IgG4-related hypophysitis were excluded from this analysis provided that they did not have further involvement into the skull base. 8 sources published in a language other than English were excluded. 113 sources which described IgG4-RD in the skull base and calvarium were analyzed (Figure 1).

RESULTS

A systematic review of literature revealed 184 cases of IgG4-RD in the skull base and calvarium. Results are presented exactly as reported in original publications without solicitation of additional information from their authors.

Six locations had 10 or greater cases of IgG4-RD in the literature. Among these, the most commonly affected location was the dura and meninges, comprising 60 cases (32%). The cavernous sinus accounted for 39 cases (20.9%) followed by 15 cases (8%) in the posterior fossa skull base. Other involved locations include 11 cases in the clivus (5.9%), 10 in the mastoid (5.3%), and 10 in the pterygopalatine fossa (5.3%). Additional involved locations include the maxillary sinus, temporal bone, suprasellar region, and middle fossa skull base (Table 2). An index of affected locations is shown in Table 3. Involvement of multiple locations was common, and some cases also demonstrated soft tissue involvement from skull base originating lesions. In cases of concurrent spinal involvement, biopsy of affected spinal dura was used for diagnosis, particularly in cases that demonstrated similar leptomeningeal thickening between intracranial and spinal locations12. Cases with spinal involvement commonly presented with focal neurological signs such as altered reflexes, weakness, and paresthesia9,12,42,43.

Treatment information was reported for 145 cases (Figure 2). Most common was corticosteroid treatment, utilized in 119 cases. Immunosuppressive agents such as rituximab, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, and mycophenolate mofetil were also used. Multi-drug regimens such as a corticosteroid combined with rituximab or other immunosuppressive agents were used in 73 patients. In more recent cases, corticosteroids with rituximab were the most common combination therapy; corticosteroids with other immunosuppressives or cytotoxic agents was more common in earlier reported cases. Collectively, rituximab was used in 51 patients, a number of which had steroid-resistant or recurrent disease. In rare cases, intrathecal administration of rituximab led to a rapid resolution of symptoms in previously drug resistant disease. Surgical resection was utilized in 34 patients, often in tandem with pharmacologic therapies. The decision between biopsy or surgical resection was highly variable. Common trends in patients who underwent surgical resection included significant involvement of local bone, rapid growth, significant mass effect, and heightened concern for malignancy. Of note, presumed diagnoses of meningioma or lymphoma were noted in several cases that utilized surgical resection. Topical steroid use was reported as effective in a small number of sino-nasal manifestations. Radiation therapy was rare, but reported effective in a small number of cases44–47. A small number of cases described less symptomatic patients opted for observation only6,48,49

Prevalence of IgG4-RD in the skull base and calvarium was comparable amongst males and females. Sex was reported in 161 cases and included 78 females (48.4%) and 83 males (51.6%). Elevated serum IgG4 levels were found in 63/103 cases (61%) and values ranged from 8 to 3410 mg/dL (normal < 135 mg/dL18). Patients frequently followed an extended clinical course; time to diagnosis was reported to be greater than 1 year in 32 patients (Table 2, Figure 2). Diagnosis was delayed by the need for multiple biopsies in 23 cases. Multiple biopsies were needed due to inadequate sampling of tissues, often revealing non-specific inflammatory infiltrate, failure to examine for features of IgG4-RD at initial histopathological analysis, or due to disease recurrence and lack of diagnosis at initial presentation.

On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the majority of IgG4-RD lesions appear as well defined, homogenous, T1 and T2 isointense or hypointense lesions5,19,20. IgG4-RD lesions are also well visualized on computed tomography (CT) imaging, particularly in cases with bone involvement19. On post-contrast imaging studies, IgG4-RD lesions are typically homogenously enhancing20.

Two cases from the authors’ institution are presented which highlight the varying disease presentations that may be encountered with IgG4-RD of the skull base.

Case Reports

Case 1:

A 52-year-old female presented with ear pain originally diagnosed as otitis media and treated with myringotomy tube placement. This progressed to pain and numbness in the maxillary and mandibular nerve regions. MRI imaging (Figure 3) revealed an enhancing lesion in the right infratemporal fossa near the foramen ovale and masticator space which was concerning for malignancy. Diplopia from a right abducens nerve palsy developed the following week. Consecutive endoscopic endonasal biopsies were performed and revealed non-specific inflammatory changes. The severity of symptoms and concern for malignancy prompted a third biopsy. A right middle fossa craniotomy was performed to provide extradural access for biopsy of the trigeminal ganglion and Meckel’s Cave region. Histopathological examination showed a dense chronic inflammatory process with significant intraneural and perineural destruction (Figure 4). Immunostains for IgG and IgG4 highlighted the plasma cells and demonstrated a focally elevated IgG4/IgG ratio of 41.8% (Figure 4). Significant fibrosis was noted, but not of a storiform appearance. Obliterative phlebitis was not observed. A diagnosis of “inflammatory pseudotumor, likely IgG4-related disease” was rendered. A tapering course of prednisone with two intravenous doses of rituximab lead to a significant improvement in visual symptoms and radiological regression of the lesion (Figure 3). Residual pain in the trigeminal region was managed with carbamazepine.

Case 2:

A 53-year-old female developed a mandibular infection that was diagnosed as osteomyelitis at another institution in 2009. Following surgical debridement and antibiotics, the patient recovered well and remained stable until 2017 when she developed blurred vision. MRI revealed a clival lesion. Two biopsies at outside institutions suggested an inflammatory process, and a diagnosis of osteomyelitis was made in both cases. She was treated with antibiotics until she presented to the authors institution in 2020 with worsening blurred vision. MRI and CT studies suggested disease progression in the clivus (Figure 5). Additional MRI and CT studies suggested a differential diagnosis of bacterial infection, granulomatous disease, malignancy, or IgG4-RD. An endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal biopsy of the clival region mass was performed, including removal and debridement of much of the clivus, and reconstruction with a vascularized naso-septal flap as apparent on postoperative imaging (Figure 5). Histopathological examination demonstrated a dense lymphocytic infiltrate involving the local bone and dura with storiform fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis (Figure 6). Immunostains for IgG and IgG4 revealed an IgG4/IgG ratio of 57%. Ultimately a diagnosis of IgG4-RD was made. A tapering course of prednisone with mycophenolate mofetil was given and the patient experienced remission of their visual symptoms and headaches.

Discussion

IgG4-RD is an uncommon entity, but a growing number of cases in the skull base and calvarium have been reported. The 2 cases reports highlight the need for thorough histopathological evaluation of inflammatory lesions in the skull base. In both cases, three biopsies were required to arrive at a proper diagnosis and treatment. Delay in diagnosis and misdiagnosis are common with this disorder. Although conservative surgical techniques should be favored when possible, it is important to note the difficulties of confirming a diagnosis of IgG4-RD in the skull base when selecting biopsy methods. This is reinforced by the large number of cases in the literature which reported multiple biopsies to arrive at a diagnosis of IgG4-RD. The 1st case demonstrated that the process can be heterogenous and portions of the lesion can be nondiagnostic and lack the defining features of IgG4-RD. Even with common diagnostic criteria, the clinical significance of features such as storiform fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis in skull and skull base lesions is unclear. Reporting of histological features was inconsistent in the literature, and often a lymphocytic infiltrate with elevated IgG4 levels was the chief finding for diagnosis. Heterogeneity in histological findings is emphasized by these 2 cases as the first lacked storiform fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis while the second displayed the full array of classic histopathological findings. Consistent with other reports, 39% of IgG4-RD had normal serum IgG4 levels18, further confirming that serum IgG4 levels are not a reliable marker of IgG4-RD. With the wide range of observed values, a graded system as proposed by Wallace et. al28 represents the best method to interpret serum IgG4 levels.

The 2 presented cases emphasize the varied presentation for IgG4-RD in the skull base, ranging from incidental findings50 to headaches48, visual impairment51–53, orbital pain10, hearing loss and other otologic symptoms54–57, intracranial hypertension, vasculopathy58–63, facial pain64, seizures9,45,65–67, hemiparesis68, cranial nerve deficits46,56,69–72, or other generalized neurological symptoms44,73. As confirmation of the diagnosis of IgG4-RD can only be made on histopathological examination, skull base manifestations often require more invasive surgical methods and the diagnosis can be overlooked on preliminary biopsies74,75. In some cases of systemic involvement, diagnosis of skull base IgG4-RD was rendered from histopathologic examination of peripheral tissues12,15,75,76; however, the validity of this approach is unclear based on more recent diagnostic guidelines. IgG4-RD has been diagnosed concurrently with lymphoma, mastoiditis, and ANCA-positive vasculitis, so attributing all lesions to IgG4-RD without direct examination of all affected tissues may overlook co-existing pathologies47,57,77–79. Failure to examine for features of IgG4-RD on histopathological examination can lead to greatly prolonged clinical courses and increased anxiety for patients. Skull base IgG4-RD has been mistaken for pathologies such as malignancy7,22,62,63,80,81, mastoiditis54,57,78, or osteomyelitis13 based on imaging and clinical symptoms alone.

The main treatment of IgG4-RD described to date has been corticosteroids; a tapering course of prednisone commonly yields a rapid response and is suggested as a diagnostic factor for IgG4-RD28,82,83. In systemic disease, glucocorticoid treatment may be associated with a greater risk of relapse, but this trend is unknown in the skull base and calvarium84. Adjuvant immunosuppressives such as azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or rituximab have been used, but over time, there was a trend towards the use of corticosteroids with rituximab over other combinations. Given its greater effectiveness in systemic IgG4-RD, the use of rituximab in particular is warranted in those with severe or recurrent disease, or for patients with a poor tolerance to steroids. Intrathecal administration of rituximab can be considered and has been successful in resistant cases85–87.

Both cases described in this report followed a similar recovery course upon treatment with steroids and immunosuppressive agents. The effectiveness of steroids and immunosuppressive agents reinforces the need for prompt tissue diagnosis so as to avoid prolonged clinical courses. Although less common, the second case highlights the utility of surgical debridement, particularly when there is significant mass effect or infiltration into local bone. Several cases reported concern for malignancy as a factor in their decision for surgical resection, further emphasizing the need for consideration of IgG4-RD as a possible etiology for skull base lesions. Corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents may exacerbate similar appearing lesions such as osteomyelitis or alter the diagnostic yield of a biopsy, and should not be started without a suspected diagnosis of IgG4-RD88.

This is the first systematic review that has examined IgG4-RD in the skull base and calvarium. Compiling the known locations where IgG4-RD has been observed in the skull can help elucidate when IgG4-RD should be considered in the differential diagnosis of skull lesions. The 184 cases discovered in this review highlight the varied presentations of IgG4-RD.

Despite a thorough systematic review of literature, there are limitations to this study. Early cases of the disease may have been overlooked due to lack of widespread awareness of its variable manifestations and inconsistent diagnostic criteria. Conversely, some early cases of IgG4-RD may not fit the more stringent diagnostic criteria established in recent years28. Nonetheless, this review serves to consolidate the current body of knowledge and create a foundation to identify the varied presentations and optimum management of IgG4-RD in the skull base and calvarium.

Conclusion

A systematic review revealed 184 known cases of IgG4-RD in the skull base and calvarium. Lesions involved many locations in the skull and skull base, most commonly the meninges and cavernous sinus. IgG4-RD responds well to corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents such as rituximab. If IgG4-RD is suspected, more invasive biopsy methods may be needed to ensure a proper histopathological examination. This index of known cases provides a platform to better understand IgG4-RD in the skull base and calvarium.

Table 1:

Diagnostic criteria of IgG4-RD, adapted from the 2019 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism guidelines28

Entry criteria:
 Clinical or radiologic involvement of a typical organ
 Pathologic evidence of an inflammatory lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate accompanied of unknown etiology in a typical organ
 Typical organs: pancreas, salivary glands, bile ducts, orbits, kidney, lung, aorta, retroperitoneum, pachymeninges, or thyroid gland
Exclusion criteria:
 Clinical: fever, response to glucocorticoids
 Serologic: Leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, peripheral eosinophilia, disease specific antibodies (e.g. SSA/Ro), cryoglobulinemia
 Radiologic: Radiologic findings suspicious for malignancy or infection that have not been investigated, rapid progression, Erdheim-Chester disease, splenomegaly
 Pathologic: Cellular infiltrates suggesting malignancy that have not been sufficiently evaluated. markers of inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, neutrophilic or granulomatous inflammation, necrotizing vasculitis, features of a macrophage disorder
 Known diagnosis of the following: Multicentric Castleman’s disease, Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis (if only pancreatobiliary disease is present), Hashimoto thyroiditis (if only the thyroid is affected)
Inclusion criteria:
 Histopathology 0–13, max score with lymphocytic infiltrate, storiform fibrosis ± obliterative phlebitis
 IgG4 Immunostaining 0–16, max score when IgG4/IgG >71%
 Serum IgG4 concentration 0–11, max score when 5x upper limit of normal
 Salivary Gland Involvement 0–14, max score for 2+ sets of glands involved bilaterally
 Chest 0–10, max for band-like soft tissue changes in thorax
 Pancreas and biliary tree 0–19, max score with pancreatic enlargement and biliary tree involvement
 Kidney 0–10, max score with bilateral low-density areas in renal cortex
 Retroperitoneum 0–8, max score with circumferential involvement of aorta or iliac arteries
Case is classified as IgG4 RD if entry criteria are met, no exclusion criteria are present, and total points ≥20.
