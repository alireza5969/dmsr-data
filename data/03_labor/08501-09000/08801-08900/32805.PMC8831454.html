Changes in Alcohol Use during COVID-19 and Associations with Contextual and Individual Difference Variables: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Abstract

Objectives:

The current study meta-analyzed studies examining changes in alcohol consumption during the COVID-19 pandemic and systematically reviewed contextual and individual difference factors related to these changes.

Method:

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) protocol, studies were gathered via PsycINFO, PubMed/MEDLINE, and preprint databases (published 29 April 2021) that examined individual-level changes in consumption during the initial COVID-19 mitigation measures (before October 2020). Next, sample proportion increases and decreases in consumption, in addition to mean change in consumption variables from pre- to during-COVID, were meta-analyzed, and contextual and individual difference variables related to consumption changes during the pandemic were summarized.

Results:

One hundred and twenty-eight studies provided data from 58 countries (mean n = 3,876; median n = 1,092; aggregate sample n = 492,235). The average mean change in alcohol consumption was nonsignificant (Cohen’s d = −0.01, p = .68); however, meta-analysis revealed that 23% of participants reported increases in consumption and 23% reported decreases. These changes were moderated by per capita gross domestic product and country. Narrative synthesis revealed multiple predictors of increased drinking, including contextual changes (e.g., children at home, income loss, working remotely, low social support), individual difference variables (being female, a young-to-middle aged adult, or Black), and mental health/alcohol-related risk factors (e.g., depression).

Conclusions:

The identified factors associated with increased alcohol consumption should be considered in planning behavioral health services during future crisis events that abruptly alter everyday environments in ways that increase stress and decrease access to naturally occurring rewards.

In early 2020, governments around the world instituted varying degrees of mitigation strategies in response to the emerging COVID-19 pandemic. Prior to the widespread dissemination of vaccines, mitigation strategies such as closing schools, businesses, and recreational facilities stay at home orders; mask-wearing; and physical distancing (also known as social distancing) were the most effective preventive measure for ameliorating the impact of the virus (Hatchett et al., 2007). Although critical to lessening the public health impact of the virus, these measures decreased access to key sources of reward, including in-person social interactions, work, school, religious activity, and recreational/leisure activities, which had predictable negative effects on psychological functioning and subjective wellbeing.

Mitigation measures were accompanied by a proliferation of warnings regarding the possibility of elevations in alcohol consumption (Friedman et al., 2020; Leatherby & Gelles, 2020) because of the financial, social, occupational, and behavioral impact of the orders, in addition to the uncertainty and stress associated with the pandemic. These predictions were guided both by research demonstrating that substance use is more likely under conditions of reward deprivation, uncertainty, and scarcity (Acuff et al., 2019; Snider et al., 2020; Vanderveldt et al., 2015) and by previous observations that substance use increases after natural disasters and emergencies (Flory et al., 2009; North et al., 2011).

Scientists answered the call-to-action and conducted numerous empirical studies in the Summer and Fall of 2020 that investigated drinking practices since the start of the widespread impact of the pandemic (roughly March 2020) and associated mitigation measures. A subset also assessed contextual factors (e.g., living situation, loss of job, type and context of drinking) and individual difference characteristics (e.g., sex and age) that predicted changes in consumption. The physical distancing interventions and economic consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in abrupt contextual changes that would be expected to increase harmful alcohol use, at least among a subset of persons with environmental and personal constraints and vulnerabilities that limited their ability and resources to adapt to the post-COVID environment. Although it is important to understand population-level changes in alcohol consumption as a result of the pandemic, it is also important to understand person-level predictors of changes to explain heterogeneity. One recent systematic review attempted to summarize these findings (Schmidt et al., 2021); however, the review missed a substantial amount of the literature, misrepresented some of the findings included in the tables, and did not meta-analytically aggregate the findings. Thus, a more rigorous and complete summary of the literature is warranted. The current study systematically reviewed studies that examined changes in alcohol consumption related to the onset and first six to eight months of the COVID-19 pandemic when some degree of mitigation measures were imposed worldwide with few exceptions. The goals of the review are to: (1) aggregate available quantitative data to determine the degree and nature of changes in alcohol consumption across studies, and (2) qualitatively review contextual and individual difference factors across studies that were related to changes in alcohol consumption during this initial pandemic period when mitigation measures abruptly altered the natural environment. In many places, the COVID-19 crisis is lessening due to vaccine distribution and mitigation measures (although this may change due to variants of the COVID-19 virus). However, the COVID-19 pandemic had a drastic impact on our lives on micro to macro levels that may influence drinking and other health related behaviors. Understanding these trends can potentially teach us about the impact of abrupt environmental changes like this on alcohol consumption and related behaviors.

Method

Study Selection

Empirical studies were identified through searches of PsycINFO, PubMed/MEDLINE, and preprint (bioRxiv, PsyArXiv, OSF Preprints) databases for peer-reviewed and pre-print journal articles published as of April 29, 2021. Search terms included the following keywords: (alcohol OR drinking) AND (COVID OR coronavirus OR COVID-19 OR sars-cov-2). The primary inclusion criteria were: (1) human research participants ages 12 and up, (2) examination of individually reported changes in alcohol use during the COVID-19 pandemic (cross-sectional or longitudinal assessment), (3) English language, (4) published (or posted for preprints) in January 2020 forward; and (5) data collection occurring before October 2020 (to capture changes in drinking during the first wave mitigation measures). Eligible alcohol variables included self-reported changes in alcohol consumption (“Did your consumption increase, decrease, or stay the same?”), self-report measures of alcohol consumption (quantity and frequency), and measures of alcohol problems or severity (e.g., Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test; Young Adult Alcohol Consequences Questionnaire). Studies that examined biological measures of alcohol consumption were not eligible because they typically measure acute alcohol intoxication but do not represent overall patterns of drinking. Figure 1 provides an overview of the study selection process and flow. This was a systematic review and meta-analysis of existing literature, and therefore was not considered human subjects research. This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42020227150).

Coding Procedures and Computation of Effect Sizes

One author (J.S.) performed all searches and, after removing duplicates, screened titles and abstracts of all articles returned in the database searches for clearly eligible or ineligible studies and compared results. Next, one author (S.A.) reviewed the full manuscript of each study and coded relevant data, including sample size, age, sex/gender, study design, and effect sizes. Ten randomly selected studies were coded by another author (J.S.) in order to calculate interrater reliability (IRR). IRR was .91. Designs included subjective cross-sectional (S-CS) studies that queried whether individuals’ subjectively thought that their alcohol consumption had increased, decreased, or remained the same since before COVID-19 mitigation measures; objective cross-sectional (O-CS) studies in which participants reported quantitative levels of alcohol consumption, related problems, and/or severity before (retrospectively) and after mitigation measures began; longitudinal (L) studies that assessed consumption prospectively over two or more time points; and cohort comparison (CC) studies that compared post-COVID cross-sectional data from one cohort to pre-COVID data from another cohort, or to a region not yet affected by COVID-19. Available effects sizes were generated for the (1) proportion of participants who reported an increase in alcohol use, (2) proportion of participants who reported a decrease in alcohol use, and (3) numerical changes in alcohol consumption from before to after enactment of mitigation measures. Proportions were summarized as the percentage of people who reported either increases or decreases in alcohol consumption (reported in 101 and 72 studies, respectively). Numerical changes were summarized as Cohen’s dz (n = 28 studies; k = 72 effect size estimates). Proportion effect sizes were transformed to log odds for the analysis and retransformed back to proportions for interpretation.

Rating of Evidence Quality

Quality of evidence was assessed using the National Institutes of Health quality assessment tools (https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health-topics/study-quality-assessment-tools). Specifically, we used the quality assessment tool for Before-After (Pre-Post) Studies with No Control Group. Each study was rated based on whether they met criteria for nine questions related to the quality of the evidence for contributing to the current review. We used proportion agreement to determine interrater reliability between coders completing a random sample of five studies and found an average of .99 across all five studies, suggesting excellent interrater reliability for the remaining studies. We then created a proportion of criterion met to total criterion assessed in order to use in quantitative analyses controlling for quality of the study. This was used as a moderator in meta-analytic models. We also gave a qualitative rating to each study in its ability to draw associative conclusions about the effects of the COVID-19 on changes in alcohol consumption. As suggested by the authors of the NIH assessment tools, we used the evidence rating as a loose guide for determining a qualitative summary of “poor”, “fair”, or “good” for the quality of evidence to contribute to our study question. In addition to the NIH assessment tool questions, our qualitative rating took into account other factors such as the sample (whether the sample was general or specific to a population that may reasonably not have the same results as the general populations, e.g., people with a history of myocardial infarctions) and the quality of reporting of the analyses and results.

Moderator Variables for Meta-Analyses

Four moderator variables were included in the meta-analyses: (1) Age and Sex/Gender were treated as continuous moderators defined as the average age and percentage of females reported in the sample. These data were available for a subset of studies (age n = 76 studies; sex/gender n = 118). (2) Whether the sample was comprised exclusively of pre-COVID drinkers or included both pre-COVID drinkers and abstainers was treated as a dichotomous moderator. Cohen’s d meta-analysis also reported whether studies with college samples had different effect sizes than other samples. (3) Country Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita in 2019 was extracted for each country from the World Bank national accounts databank (World Bank, 2021) and reflects a country’s economic output relative to its population and provides an index of the economic and environmental scarcity or plenty across the country. (4) Individual country COVID-19 response was rated using the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (CGRT). This database rates government responses to the pandemic on each date since January 1, 2020 (Hale et al., 2020). Trained raters used publicly available data to score each day on 19 indicators, which were aggregated into 4 indices scored from 1 to 100 to reflect (1) government lockdown stringency, (2) containment and health measures (e.g., testing, contact tracing), (3) economic support (e.g., income support), and (4) an overall government response index combining all indicators in the database. Higher numbers reflected greater levels of government response. We used the peak index from the time of the first critical intervention in a given country to the end of data collection for each study.

Meta-Analytic Methods

Three sets of meta-analyses were conducted. The first and second sets evaluated the proportion of participants who reported increasing and decreasing alcohol use during the early days of the pandemic, respectively. A random-effects framework was used with separate meta-analyses for each effect size. The third set of meta-analyses evaluated the Cohen’s dz reflecting changes in alcohol use from before to during COVID-19 mitigation measures. These sets of analyses used the robust variance estimation (RVE) meta-regression method to calculate omnibus effect size estimates (Hedges et al., 2010; Tipton, 2015). The RVE method allowed for the incorporation of dependent effect size measures without violation of independence assumptions and allowed use of multiple effect size measures from the same participants (Hedges et al., 2010; Tipton, 2015). We used a small-sample size adjustment form of the RVE method to improve the protection of intended Type I error rates (Tipton, 2015). Heterogeneity for proportion effect sizes was tested using τ (Hedges et al., 2010) and I2 (Higgins & Thompson, 2002), two indices of the between study variance. Moderator analyses were planned for each set of meta-analyses. Estimates of publication bias were generated using Egger’s plot for funnel asymmetry, and influential cases were identified using a jackknife approach. All tests were conducted in R statistical language with RVE models evaluated using the robumeta package (Fisher & Tipton, 2015), and traditional meta-analysis models were tested using the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010). Type I error rates were set at .05, and all tests were two-tailed. Discrepancies in the number of studies with available effects sizes reflected the reported data (e.g., some studies may have reported the percentage of participants who increased consumption without providing information on the percentage who decreased).

Results

Meta-analytic Sample Characteristics

The search (summarized in Figure 1) returned 1,682 articles. After removing duplicates and screening full text articles for eligibility, 128 articles remained that could contribute an effect size to one of the meta-analyses. Table 1 presents individual study characteristics. Studies had an average of 3,876 participants (median n = 1,092; aggregate sample n = 492,235). On average, samples were 37.09 years old (range = 16.7 to 71.0), and 61% female (range = 0% to 100%). Studies included data from at least 58 countries. Study designs included subjective cross-sectional (k = 97), objective cross-sectional (k = 25); longitudinal (k = 10, and cohort comparison (k = 2).1 Qualitative ratings of study quality are included in Table 1. Overall, 46 studies were rated as “poor”, 53 studies were rated as “fair”, and 29 studies were rated as “good”.

Meta-analytic Outcomes

Meta-analysis revealed a small, nonsignificant decrease in alcohol use across studies (k = 60, Cohen’s dz = −0.016, 95% Confidence Intervals [CIs] = −0.09 to 0.06, p = .68). The results also suggested some heterogeneity (τ = .15). Because of the lack of studies, we were unable to examine country and continent as moderators. However, the results of the remaining moderator results for the Cohen’s d effect size are provided as supplemental materials. No other moderator was significant.

Meta-analytic results for studies that reported a percentage change in alcohol consumption can be found in Table 2. Across studies, 23% of participants reported increases, and 23% reported decreases. There was significant heterogeneity across studies for both increase and decrease effect sizes (Table 2). Studies conducted in countries with higher GDPs had higher percentages who reported increases and lower percentages who reported decreases in consumption. Both country and continent emerged as predictors of decreases in consumption. Whereas Europe and Australia/New Zealand hovered close to the global percentage decrease (~24), North America had much lower percentage decreases (16%), and Asia had much higher percentage decreases (34%). The country-level moderation reflected this as well, with both the United States and Canada demonstrating lower decreases than all other countries. Although the continent moderation effects for increases in alcohol consumption were not significant, the country moderator revealed an approximately 10% lower increase in alcohol consumption in China compared to the omnibus model, while the U.S. and a number of European countries had approximately >5% higher increase across studies. No other moderators were significant.

Importantly, neither study quality ratings, sample size, nor study design (longitudinal versus cross-sectional) were significant moderators of any effect size. These results can be found in Table 2, Supplemental Table 1, and Supplemental Table 2. Further, one study removed analyses suggested there were no study outliers disproportionately influencing study results.

Review of Studies Examining Predictors of Changes in Alcohol Consumption

The following section provides a brief overview of predictors of changes in drinking associated with COVID-19 mitigation measures. The key findings are synthesized in Table 3, which provides a summarizing sentence with the most common outcome across studies, along with a percentage of how many studies reported a significant effect for a given outcome. The summaries given should only be considered as the authors best estimation of what the data suggests based on our review of these literatures. Contextual Factors

Multiple factors associated with individuals’ employment and living circumstances, including where and how they drank, predicted changes in drinking during the pandemic. Persons with stable unemployment both before and after mitigation measures were imposed were less likely to increase consumption and more likely to decrease consumption compared to those with unstable employment that occurred initially during the pandemic period (Biddle et al., 2020; Rossinot et al., 2020; Szajnoga et al., 2020). Further, persons working remotely were more likely to increase consumption compared to those working in-person (Rolland et al., 2020; Szajnoga et al., 2020). Those who lost their job during COVID-19 appeared to be more likely to increase drinking (Biddle et al., 2020; Vanderbruggen et al., 2020), even when compared to those who were working remotely and those with stable pre-COVID unemployment (Vanderbruggen et al., 2020). Pandemic-related income loss was uniquely associated with greater likelihood of increases in alcohol consumption (Wardell et al., 2020; Zajacova et al., 2020) and seemed to account for the most variance when included in a multivariate model with job status and job change (Wardell et al., 2020). Students appeared less likely to increase drinking compared to those who were employed (Oksanen et al., 2020; Rolland et al., 2020; Vanderbruggen et al., 2020). Essential workers, in particular, appeared to be at unique risk for increased consumption (Sallie et al., 2020) and a greater likelihood of binge drinking (Weerakoon et al., 2020). More highly educated individuals were more likely to report increases in drinking. With one exception (Hanafi et al., 2021), all studies supporting this finding were in Western countries. Only two studies reported the opposite effect (Koopmann et al., 2020, 2021). Another study found that the association with higher education was significant only among women (Biddle et al., 2020).

Two studies found that the proportion of individuals engaging in solitary drinking increased during the pandemic (Panagiotidis et al., 2020; Wardell et al., 2020), while another study found a decrease in the frequency of solitary drinking and a simultaneous increase in the frequency of virtual drinking (McPhee et al., 2020). Another study found that drinking with intimate partners increased, and drinking with friends decreased (Panagiotidis et al., 2020). Further, increased drinking in the home varied across sex/gender and age (Callinan, Mojica-Perez, et al., 2020); i.e., in 2019, 18-to-29 year old males drank more than half of their alcohol outside of the home, whereas during the pandemic, they increased home drinking but not enough to compensate for pre-COVID levels, resulting in overall reductions. In contrast, 18-to-29 year old females increased home drinking to match pre-COVID levels (Callinan, Mojica-Perez, et al., 2020). All other age groups also compensated for reduced drinking in other contexts by increasing home drinking.

Three studies found that each additional person in the household was associated with greater increases in drinking (Kim et al., 2020; Pabst, Bollen, Creupelandt, Fontesse, & Maurage, 2021; Rossinot et al., 2020), and living with children also was associated with a greater likelihood of increasing consumption. The association between social living conditions and changes in drinking also appeared to depend partially on a relative change from pre-COVID living situations; e.g., college students who moved home with parents tended to drink less compared to those who continued living in their pre-COVID residences (White et al., 2020). Living alone, the size of the dwelling, and the type of the dwelling (apartment, house, etc.) were not reliably associated with changes in drinking during the pandemic, although one study found that living alone was associated with greater increases in solitary drinking, but not overall consumption (Wardell et al., 2020). Population density (i.e., rural versus urban) also was not associated with changes in alcohol consumption. Less than half of the studies examining the effect of relationship status on changes in drinking found that having a significant other was associated with increases in drinking, with the remaining studies finding no effect. The majority of studies observed no relationship between social support and changes in drinking.

Although the duration of quarantine or stay-at-home order appeared to have no effect on changes in drinking (Knell et al., 2020; Weerakoon et al., 2020), studies examining the self-reported intensity of the stay-at-home order appeared to consistently suggest that greater intensity was associated with increases in consumption (Ahmed et al., 2020; Huckle et al., 2020).

Individual Difference Factors

Demographic characteristics including age, sex/gender, and race showed variable associations with drinking during the pandemic. Results generally showed a non-linear relationship between age and changes in consumption. Consumption increases were most likely among those aged approximately 25-45 compared to those aged 25 and under, and the likelihood of increased alcohol consumption diminished with each decade over age 40. Most studies reported decreases in alcohol consumption among emerging adults (roughly ages 18 to 25), an effect that was particularly pronounced among college students who went home to live with parents compared to those who stayed in their pre-COVID living situations (White et al., 2020). One study found the proportion of emerging adults drinking five or more drinks on one occasion dropped from 31.9% (in 2017-2019) to 8.5% post-lockdown in the U.K. (Niedzwiedz et al., 2020).

There were no consistent sex/gender differences in pandemic-related changes in alcohol consumption. Nevertheless, some studies found that women were more likely to report increases in drinking frequency, whereas men were more likely to report increases in risky drinking (e.g., binge drinking). Several studies identified sex/gender differences in drinking contexts. For example, adolescent girls reported greater increases in drinking overall and were more likely to drink with parents, whereas adolescent boys were more likely to report both solitary drinking and in-person drinking with peers (Dumas et al., 2020). Another study found that solitary drinking increased among adult men, although overall consumption levels generally did not differ by sex/gender (Wardell et al., 2020).

Changes in consumption as a function of race seemed to differ across countries. A U.K. COVID-focused survey conducted in late April 2020 found that White participants were more likely to report increased frequency of alcohol consumption and binge drinking compared to data collected between 2017 and 2019 (Niedzwiedz et al., 2020). A U.S. study also found greater increases in drinking frequency among White participants (McPhee et al., 2020), but this same study and others in the U.S. otherwise consistently found that Black or non-White participants were more likely to report greater increases in quantity of alcohol consumption compared to White participants (Barbosa et al., 2020; Grossman et al., 2020; McPhee et al., 2020; Pollard et al., 2020). Further, non-White participants generally reported proportionately higher increases exceeding binge drinking limits compared to White participants (Barbosa et al., 2020; Bonar et al., 2021; Grossman et al., 2020), and a Canadian study found a greater increased likelihood of solitary drinking among Black than White participants (Wardell et al., 2020). In contrast, two additional studies suggested decreases in consumption among those who identified as Hispanic (Boschuetz et al., 2020; Pollard et al., 2020).

Mental Health and Alcohol-related Risk

Mental health status had a consistent association with changes in alcohol use during the pandemic. Twelve studies found a positive relationship between levels of depression and alcohol consumption in both males and females. Further, older adults (aged 50+) who indicated they were depressed were at least three times more likely to report increases in consumption compared to those who indicated they were not depressed (Tran et al., 2020). Fifteen studies found that greater anxiety was associated with a greater likelihood of increased consumption, and anxiety was a stronger predictor of increased consumption among men than women (Tran et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). Twenty studies found that positive associations between increased alcohol consumption and various measures of psychological distress, such as psychological health, post-traumatic stress disorder, and perceived stress. Five studies also found a positive relation between changes in drinking and COVID-specific distress, and one suggested that coping motives explained this relationship (McPhee et al., 2020). Six studies found that drinking to cope was associated with greater increases in alcohol consumption (Chodkiewicz et al., 2020; McPhee et al., 2020; Wardell et al., 2020) and alcohol problems (Wardell et al., 2020).

Among studies that examined pre-COVID alcohol use or severity as a predictor of changes in consumption, almost half found a positive relationship between increases in consumption and pre-COVID alcohol use severity. This relationship was observed across multiple indices, including the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (Chodkiewicz et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2020; Oksanen et al., 2020), drinking frequency (Biddle et al., 2020; Dumas et al., 2020), history of substance use (Boschuetz et al., 2020), among those who identified as “addicts” (Sidor & Rzymski, 2020), and the recency of last drinking episode among patients in AUD treatment (Balhara et al., 2020). Finally, a greater likelihood of increased alcohol consumption was associated with an unhealthy diet (Ingram et al., 2020), sleep problems (Pérez-Carbonell et al., 2020), and less exercise (Ingram et al., 2020).

Discussion

The meta-analytic results suggest that there was a relatively normal distribution of changes in drinking during the pandemic. At a general level, the findings should be interpreted relative to pre-COVID population-level trends in drinking (Jager & Keyes, 2021), which appear to differ by region. In recent decades, alcohol use prevalence rates have decreased in Europe (Rehm et al., 2019), increased in the U.S. (Grucza et al., 2018) and Western Pacific (World Health Organisation, 2018), and remained relatively stable in Southeast Asia (World Health Organisation, 2018). Although speculative, the meta-analytic results tentatively suggest that the pandemic may have interrupted decreasing population-level drinking trends in some countries and escalated increasing population-level drinking trends in others (e.g., U.S.). However, it is unclear why countries differed greatly in increases and decreases in drinking. Countries differed with respect to whether alcohol was considered an essential item during the pandemic (e.g., U.S., U.K., Canada, and France) or was considered nonessential or was banned (e.g., Greenland, India, South Africa), which likely influenced changes in alcohol use. However, attitudes toward alcohol are also different across these countries. In some countries, reductions in social drinking outside of the home may have been more likely to be offset by increases in drinking at home, either solitary drinking or drinking with an intimate partner (Panagiotidis et al., 2020). The GDP per capita findings are consistent with research indicating that individuals in higher income brackets drank similar or greater amounts of alcohol compared to those in lower income brackets (Collins, 2016) and may also explain some of the country-level findings.

The results suggested that, although population drinking levels may have remained roughly the same, roughly one fourth increased drinking, while another one fourth decreased drinking. The review identified contextual and individual difference factors associated primarily with increased drinking during the pandemic and highlighted risks for persons with environmental and personal constraints and vulnerabilities that limit their ability and resources to adapt to the post-COVID environment. These findings have implications for future pandemics or other disasters that abruptly change our collective natural environment, and the pattern of associations for contextual variables is broadly consistent with behavioral economic theory and research (Acuff, Tucker, et al., 2020). Specifically, behavioral economics indicates that alcohol consumption is (1) an inverse function of the costs (or constraints) of alcohol, and (2) a direct function of the costs (or value) of alternative rewards.

Thus, while alcohol is a robust immediate and certain reinforcer, its reinforcing properties are relative to contextual factors that can increase or decrease its value. Contexts with relatively few alcohol-free reinforcers are associated with increased alcohol use, and contexts enriched with alcohol-free reinforcers are associated with decreased use (Acuff, Amlung, et al., 2020). Scarcity, uncertainty, and negative affect/stress were common conditions during the pandemic, and the review found multiple variables reflecting these conditions to have associations with increased alcohol use, including depressive symptoms, increased stress, having children in the house (likely without access to school/childcare), and job loss. Consistent with behavioral economic theory and research, these variables appeared to increase the value of alcohol (Amlung & MacKillop, 2014; Vanderveldt et al., 2015). Further, several studies demonstrated that self-reported intensity of stay-at-home orders were also associated with greater increases in binge drinking. Other factors associated with increased drinking involved increased opportunity for drinking with diminished external responsibilities (i.e., working remotely) and even social drinking, due to living with a greater number of people. Other variables appeared to be associated with constraints on access to alcohol-free rewards (e.g., reduced social interaction and support) and a decrease in constraints on access to alcohol through online alcohol purchasing. Finally, one study demonstrated that reduced reward in the environment during the pandemic was associated with increased drinking (McPhee et al., 2020). It should be noted that the contextual variables described in this paragraph lie on a continuum of mutability, and while for some these may change easily (i.e., some people switch jobs easily or move from one living situation to another), for others these may be more immutable, and changes may best occur in other domains.

Other results suggested that females, those between ages 25 and 45, and those who identify as Black were more likely to increase alcohol consumption. Adults ages 25 to 45, especially females, appeared to have increased drinking virtually or in the home, which offset reductions in drinking outside the home associated with mitigation measures like restaurant and bar closures. Although emerging adults typically report the highest levels of alcohol consumption in the population, drinking in this age range is more likely to occur outside the home at bars or restaurants (Callinan et al., 2016), which the pandemic mitigations made more difficult and risky. In addition, increased drinking among Black participants appeared most consistent in studies conducted within the U.S., which may reflect the added effects of racial stress and historic health and social disparities related to systemic racism, all of which contributed to the disproportionate impact of the pandemic on people of color in terms of morbidity, mortality, and economic impact. Although women were generally more likely to increase drinking frequency, men may have been more likely to increase binge drinking.

The current review has several limitations. First, the review only reported on individual level changes in alcohol use and did not examine other factors associated with drinking that may also have changed during the pandemic. For example, only a few studies reported on initiation of alcohol use, with varying results (Boehnke et al., 2020; Koopmann et al., 2021). The current review also did not focus on reductions in treatment seeking during the pandemic (Jackson et al., 2020), which is of concern given that the pandemic reduced access to behavioral health services and necessitated a shift to electronic platforms for service delivery. Further, several studies examined purchasing behaviors that were not discussed, although these are a pertinent alcohol-related behavior to understand (Balhara et al., 2020; Callinan, Mojica-Perez, et al., 2020; Huckle et al., 2020). Second, although COVID-19 may have affected other drug use in similar ways, pharmacological and societal factors related to alcohol may make some of these results unique, and results may not translate to other substances. Third, due to the emergent nature of the pandemic, authors were not contacted directly, studies were screened by only a single author, only studies published in English were included in the review, grey literature was not reviewed (although pre-prints were included), and the search criteria were relatively simple and may have missed some studies that examined change in alcohol use but did not include alcohol in the title.

Finally, the review was primarily based on studies that relied on retrospective data collection, and more longitudinal data are needed. However, our results found that there was no difference between cross-sectional and longitudinal studies in the size of effect, and that study quality did not moderate effect size. Further, recent research has demonstrated robust correspondence between subjective self-report measures and longitudinal quantitative self-report methods (Minhas et al., 2021). These points assuage concerns regarding our reliance on longitudinal data. The more critical point is the lack of studies using three or more timepoints before COVID. These data are useful in examining trajectories of changes after the onset of COVID mitigation measures after consideration of deviations from pre-COVID trajectories. These changes should be studied in the context of pre-COVID population-level drinking trajectories (Jager & Keyes, 2021), which no study has done to date.

Conclusion

Due to the emergence of mutant COVID-19 strains and limitations in worldwide vaccine production, distribution, and uptake, it is difficult to predict when the pandemic will end or become more manageable. Further, ongoing trends reflecting increases in human travel, populations, and environmental impact will continue to increase the risk of future pandemic-like events (Morens & Fauci, 2020). Our results suggest that while the pandemic did not increase alcohol consumption across the board, there are contextual and person-level risk factors for drinking increases that can inform alcohol prevention and control strategies during the current pandemic and may also inform risk and prevention strategies in future pandemics.

Public Health Significance

Throughout the early months of the first wave of COVID-19, about one-fourth of study samples reported increases in alcohol consumption, and one-fourth reported decreases in consumption. The review suggested that the pandemic differentially affected drinking based on multiple individual and contextual variables. In light of the emergence of mutant strains and the prediction of similar future pandemics, these results provide an empirical basis for a behavioral health pandemic preparedness plan that could reduce the likelihood of pandemic-related drinking increases.
