Systematic review of digital and non-digital non-pharmacological interventions that target quality of life and psychological outcomes in adults with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

Summary

Background.

Patients with systemic lupus erythematous (SLE) experience psychological comorbidities and impaired quality of life (QOL). We conducted a systematic review to examine the efficacy of non-pharmacological interventions for improving psychological outcomes and/or QOL in patients with SLE. To expand on a previous systematic review in this area and enhance our understanding of efficacious interventions for this population, our search included quasi-experimental and experimental studies of interventions delivered or supported by remote methods (including digitally) or in person.

Methods.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted with a research librarian using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was registered before data extraction on the international prospective register of systematic reviews PROSPERO Web site (CRD42020154962). The search included controlled-vocabulary and title/abstract terms related to non-pharmacological interventions for SLE published through October 2019 in MEDLINE (Ovid), Cochrane Library databases (Wiley), Embase (Elsevier), CINAHL (EBSCO), PsycINFO (EBSCO), Web of Science (Clarivate), ACM Digital (Association of Computer Machinery), and IEEE Xplore. Studies were synthesized using a systematic narrative synthesis framework. Risk of bias was assessed.

Results.

Twenty-three studies were included: 21 randomized controlled trials and two quasi-experimental studies. Non-pharmacological diet, physical activity, psychological, and course-based interventions improved QOL and psychological outcomes, and were delivered in traditional settings (e.g., hospital) or remotely. No studies assessing digital non-pharmacological interventions were identified in our search. Quality assessments showed serious risk of bias for the two quasi-experimental studies, and high risk of bias in a subset of experimental studies.

Conclusions.

Non-pharmacological interventions benefit patients with SLE. Future research should include more representative samples in rigorous evaluations and consider ways to incorporate digital technologies to increase accessibility.

Context:

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease that affects multiple organs.1 Individuals with SLE experience a myriad of physical and psychological comorbidities.2 Limitations in physical functioning most commonly emerge as limited or impaired mobility (e.g., in the lower limb area), fatigue, and pain.3-5 As a result, patients with SLE experience broader impacts in their day-to-day activities relevant to work (i.e., productivity,6 ability to maintain employment2, 7), daily functioning (i.e., social and household activities),8 and standard of living (i.e., mood, sleep).4, 7, 9-11 These limitations can have serious impacts on patients’ psychological well-being and quality of life (QOL).

Interventions are needed to support patients in managing these complications. Non-pharmacological interventions are often used as an adjunct to usual medical care for SLE (i.e., transplants, dialysis, medications) to address patients’ psychological needs, functioning, and QOL.12 Most non-pharmacological interventions have focused on improving health-related outcomes such as physical activity or exercise (e.g., via aerobic training), diet and nutrition (e.g., via fish oil, supplementary vitamins), psychological problems (e.g., via cognitive behavioral therapy; mindfulness-based cognitive therapy), and self-management of symptoms (e.g., via a SLE self-help course). Self-management interventions can be particularly useful for patients with SLE as an adjunct to routine care; despite recommendations for patients to be in regular contact with their primary doctor or rheumatologist, visits can be hindered by barriers such as difficulty in seeking or accessing healthcare services2 that result from limitations in physical functioning.5

In 2019, a systematic review was conducted on non-pharmacological interventions for patients with SLE for addressing fatigue, depression, anxiety, and QOL.12 The search yielded 15 published randomized controlled trials and showed that physical exercise and psychological interventions as adjuncts to traditional medical therapy improved fatigue, depression, pain and QOL. However, this review was limited in four ways. First, studies only included randomized controlled trials, which limits the scope of potentially relevant results. Assessing quasi-experimental studies that also examine the efficacy of interventions for patients with SLE could lead to an enhanced understanding of beneficial interventions for this population. Second, the review did not include a search for remotely delivered interventions, including digital interventions, for patients with SLE. Although some of the cited interventions were delivered remotely (i.e., study visits occurred in participants’ homes, sessions were conducted by telephone instead of in-person), the lack of explicit search criteria for remotely-delivered interventions may have limited the search results. Given complications of mobility associated with SLE, interventions that are delivered remotely or leverage remote tools to support it may be beneficial for this population by lessening the burden on the individual. Third, the review only reported on the psychological outcomes of depression and anxiety. Assessing for psychological outcomes beyond only depression and anxiety is important because patients with SLE commonly experience other psychosocial problems (e.g., psychological dysfunction, psychological distress, coping with socioeconomic impact, distressed mood, disturbed sleep) in addition to depression and anxiety.13-15 Lastly, risk of bias was not assessed, which is an important assessment that allows for a deeper understanding of the quality of studies.

For these reasons, we conducted an updated systematic review to examine the efficacy of non-pharmacological interventions for SLE. Our search included quasi-experimental and experimental studies of interventions delivered or supported by remote methods (including digitally) or in-person, that aimed to improve psychological outcomes and/or QOL. We also conducted quality assessments to evaluate risk of bias.

Methods:

Search Strategy:

A comprehensive literature search was conducted with a research librarian (ABW) in the following databases: MEDLINE (Ovid), Cochrane Library databases (Wiley), Embase (Elsevier), CINAHL (EBSCO), PsycINFO (EBSCO), Web of Science (Clarivate), ACM Digital (Association of Computer Machinery), and IEEE Xplore. All databases were searched from inception through 10/14/2019 without language or date limits. The search incorporated a combination of controlled-vocabulary and title/abstract terms related to (1) non-pharmacological interventions for (2) systematic lupus erythematosus. Database specific logic was applied. Complete search strategies can be found in Multimedia Appendix 1. The review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines16 and was registered before data extraction on the international prospective register of systematic reviews PROSPERO Web site (CRD42020154962). Following the approach in the 2019 systematic review,12 we did not perform a meta-analysis due to the heterogeneity between studies and the interventions examined.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria:

Only trials with experimental or quasi-experimental designs that were published in academic journals were included; grey literature was excluded (e.g., thesis, conference proceeding, clinical trial registration). We defined experimental studies as having randomization to an intervention and a comparator condition (i.e., inactive control, active control, treatment as usual, or unspecified control group). We included pre-test post-test designs where the sample was randomly selected, but participants were not randomly allocated to either intervention, and were followed up at baseline and post-intervention.

Accordingly, the following inclusion criteria were followed: Studies that 1) included participants who have SLE; 2) included adults who were 18 years or older; 3) delivered a non-pharmacological intervention; 4) were written and published in English; 5) examined a study that was experimental (i.e., randomized controlled trial) or quasi-experimental (i.e., non-randomized trial, one-group or two-group pre-post design); and 6) evaluated outcomes of QOL and/or psychological impact. Studies that did not meet these criteria were excluded.

Study Selection:

Results were screened using Rayyan, a Web-based software program that allows reviewers to collaborate during the study selection process.17 Two researchers (AC and NWW) first independently conducted a pilot screen with 100 randomly selected articles and discussed discrepancies to ensure consistency of inclusion and exclusion criteria. Then they conducted an independent screening to review titles and abstracts.

Data Extraction:

Two researchers (AC and NWW) extracted data independently using a Web-based extraction form (i.e., Google Form). Data extraction included: publication date, authors, study title, study location(s), study design, sample number, sample description (age, ethnicity, gender), type of prevention/treatment, intervention name, type of intervention, comparator name, type of comparator, presence of support such as guidance or monitoring, intervention setting, length of intervention, main outcomes, feasibility outcomes, and metrics on intervention use. Discrepancies were discussed, and a third reviewer (AKG or EGL) assisted in reaching consensus when needed. For any study that evaluated an intervention separately among patients with SLE and other diseases, only data on the SLE group were extracted.

Main Outcomes:

This review aimed to search for studies that evaluated the efficacy of the delivery of an intervention on QOL and/or psychological outcomes. All studies that evaluated at least one of these outcomes were included.

Quality Assessment:

For this review, we assessed risk of bias for randomized and nonrandomized studies using the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing risk of bias in randomized trials18 and the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing risk in nonrandomized studies of interventions.19 In the randomized trials, we evaluated for selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, and reporting bias, defined as a low, high, or unclear risk of bias. In the nonrandomized trials, we evaluated for bias due to confounding, bias in the selection of participants into the study, bias in classification of interventions, bias because of deviations from intended interventions, bias because of missing data, bias in measurement of outcomes, and bias in selection of the reported outcomes.

Results:

Included Studies:

A total of 6,395 records were identified. A total of 4,160 article titles and abstracts were reviewed. After title/abstract screening, a total of 2,948 articles were excluded based on title/abstract, and 212 remaining articles were assessed for full text eligibility. A total of 23 articles were included in the final review for qualitative synthesis. Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flow diagram.

Study characteristics:

Table 1 presents a summary of the study characteristics with a focus on the QOL and psychological outcomes for this systematic review; Multimedia Appendix 2 presents a summary of the full study characteristics and results. Of the 23 articles included in this review, 21 studies were randomized controlled trials and two studies were quasi-experimental studies (i.e., randomly selected sample divided into an intervention group and comparator group;20 randomly selected sample where the control group was recruited first21). Nine studies were conducted in the United States, and 14 studies were conducted outside of the U.S.: Brazil (n=3); Canada (n=2); Taiwan (n=1); Korea (n=1); Iran (n=2); Serbia (n=1); Sweden (n=1); United Kingdom (n=1); and Spain (n=2).

Participant characteristics:

A total of 1,273 participants were included in this review across all 23 studies. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 75 years old. Across the studies, 98% (n=1,246) of participants identified as women. Among the studies that reported participants’ race and ethnicity, 30% (n=382) of participants identified as White or Caucasian, 9.03% (n=115) as Black or African American, 0.07% (n=1) as Asian, 7.78% (n=99) as “other” or “non-white,” 2.67% (n=34) as Hispanic/Latino, and 0.86% (n=11) as unknown.

Intervention setting/mode of delivery:

Of the 23 studies, eleven studies (48%) assessed interventions that were delivered through remote methods or leveraged the support of remote tools to deliver a face-to-face intervention. No studies that assessed digital non-pharmacological interventions were identified. Remotely delivered interventions included: two telephone-based counseling interventions;22, 23 one self-administered diet intervention;24 one self-administered and minimally supervised exercise intervention;25 and one supervised in-home exercise intervention.26 Interventions also leveraged the use of telephone to support an intervention delivered in the participants’ homes;27 and to provide encouragement, support and progress monitoring,26, 28-32 or post-intervention booster sessions.28, 32 For simplicity, we subsequently refer to these eleven interventions as “remotely delivered interventions.”

Eleven additional studies (48%) tested face-to-face interventions. Studies that tested face-to-face interventions were either delivered in a hospital,25, 33-35 clinic,20, 21, 36, 37 or unspecified but authors indicated participants “attended sessions.”38-40

One study (4%) did not specify how or where the intervention was delivered.41

Intervention and comparator group characteristics:

The majority of studies (57%) were of psychological interventions (n=12).20, 22, 23, 27, 30, 34-40 Eight of the studies (35%) were of physical activity interventions.25, 26, 28, 29, 31, 33, 41, 42 Two of the studies (9%) were of diet-based interventions.24, 32 One study (4%) was a self-management course for SLE.21

Of the 23 total studies, 20 included only one comparator group (i.e., five active controls, four inactive controls, nine treatment as usual, one waitlist control, one unspecified control group). Three studies had two comparator groups.33, 37, 41 Of these latter three studies, one study compared two telephone counseling interventions and usual care;22 one study compared two exercise interventions and usual care;28 and one study compared an exercise intervention with relaxation therapy and no intervention.41

Outcomes:

Overall, 21 studies found improvement in QOL and psychological outcomes; 17 studies found significant differences between groups.

The face-to-face physical activity interventions (n=2)25, 33 and psychological interventions (n=8)20, 21, 34, 35, 37-40 showed significant improvement in helping patients manage QOL or psychological outcomes. One study testing a face-to-face psychological intervention (i.e., supportive expressive group psychotherapy) did not find significant improvement in QOL.41

The remotely delivered physical activity interventions (n=4),28, 29, 31, 42 psychological interventions (n=4)22, 23, 30, 42, or diet-based interventions (n=2)24, 32 showed significant improvement in QOL or psychological outcomes. One study that tested a physical activity intervention, delivered in the participants’ home with minimal supervision through weekly telephone monitoring and support, showed improvements in psychological outcomes, but the improvements were not statistically significant.26

The study of a physical activity intervention, whose mode of delivery was unspecified, showed significant improvement in QOL (i.e., physical and vitality subscales) over the course of the intervention.41

Quality of Life:

Studies demonstrated that diet, physical activity, and psychological interventions may benefit patients with SLE in improving QOL. Of the 15 studies that assessed for changes in QOL, 13 showed significant improvement over time in QOL: two diet-based interventions,24, 32 four psychological interventions,27, 35, 39, 40 six physical activity interventions,25, 28, 29, 33, 41, 42 and one self-management course.21 One psychological intervention demonstrated improvement in QOL from baseline to end of intervention, but this change was not statistically significant.20

QOL was assessed by the SLE-specific Quality of Life questionnaire (SLEQoL),43 LupusPro™,44 or the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36).45 Across the studies that used these measures, three studies showed significant improvement in all the subscales of QOL,27, 32, 33 and five studies showed significant improvement in the majority of the subscales of QOL.25, 30, 35, 39, 40 The most common QOL subscales that showed improvement as attributed to the intervention were: emotional health or well-being,24 mental health or status,29, 30, 35, 40, 42 energy/fatigue and vitality,24, 25, 35, 41, 42 and couples communication.30

Psychological Outcomes:

The majority of studies (n=20) assessed for changes in psychological outcomes. Studies found significant improvement over time in depression,21, 25, 33, 35, 39 anxiety,20, 35, 39 stress-related coping skills,21, 39 self-efficacy,21, 22, 30, 37 perceptions of body image,20 quality of sleep,42 affect,22 and overall psychological well-being (i.e., psychological symptoms, psychological distress, psychological status, psychological dysfunction).23, 27, 34, 37 Perceptions of helplessness26 and psychological status23 were outcomes that demonstrated improvement over time but were not found to be statistically significant. Outcomes were measured by a variety of different standardized instruments across the studies (see Multimedia Appendix 2).

Use and Acceptability Outcomes:

Only six studies reported outcomes in terms of use and acceptability of the intervention. Among this subset of studies, the majority reported that the outcomes were favorable. Three studies assessed patients’ satisfaction with or acceptability of the intervention.20, 32, 37 Two studies reported participants’ perceptions of improvements in their health as a result of the intervention.31, 41 Three studies, including two overlapping with the studies above, reported participants’ concerns with and critiques of the intervention.20, 21, 41 Studies that assessed satisfaction and acceptability did so through open-ended questionnaires, and results showed favorable responses. One study reported majority ratings of the intervention as “highly acceptable and culturally sensitive,”32 one study reported an average attendance rate of 86%,20 and another study reported an average intervention credibility rating as 5.11 based on a Likert scale ranging from “0 (illogical, not confident)” to “6 (very logical, very confident).”37 Regarding patients’ perceptions of improvements in their health, one study examining a physical activity intervention showed 49% of the participants rated themselves as ‘much’ or ‘very much’ better compared with 28% in the relaxation group and 16% in the control group.41 Another study revealed that 70% of the participants in the intervention group felt that they had “more energy” and “improved psychological well-being after exercise,” and everyone felt that the benefits outweighed the drawbacks in continuing to exercise.31 In the three studies reporting participants’ concerns with the intervention, participants expressed concerns about burdens such as busyness, lack of motivation to remain in the study, finding time to exercise, short-term symptoms (i.e., knee pain), troubles with memory and concentration (i.e., due to pain, fatigue, SLE-related symptoms or medications), and dislike of having family members present at group sessions.20, 21, 41

Study adherence:

In four studies, all participants completed the study (i.e., both the intervention and research assessments).27, 32-34 Of these studies, two interventions were delivered in-person,33, 34 one intervention was delivered in participants’ homes,27 and one intervention was a self-administered diet-based intervention.32 In thirteen additional studies, the authors reported study completion rates, which ranged between 64% to 97%. Reasons for dropout were reported in 11 of these 13 studies (84%). Reasons for dropout were disease-related (e.g., worsened disease flare, pain, fatigue, changes in treatment),21, 41 transportation-related,21 personal (e.g., family affairs, moved away),21, 23, 41 or death.22, 36 Six studies did not report this metric.20, 26, 31, 35, 38, 40

Risk of Bias:

Risk of Bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2.0 for randomized studies (Table 2) and ROBINS for non-randomized studies (Table 3). Of the 21 randomized studies, six studies were labeled as “high risk’ and 15 studies were labeled as “low risk.” Bias for randomized studies were most commonly due to detection bias, attrition bias, and reporting bias. Of the two non-randomized studies, both were labeled as “serious risk.” Bias because of missing data and selection of data were most common.20, 21 Full risk of bias results can be found in Multimedia Appendix 3.

Discussion:

This paper presents a systematic review of studies that tested the efficacy of non-pharmacological interventions on improvements in QOL and psychological outcomes for adults with SLE. The search revealed 23 experimental and quasi-experimental studies of remote and face-to-face interventions, almost all of which (n=21) demonstrated improvement over time for QOL and psychological outcomes. These effects were observed across physical activity, psychological, diet, and self-management interventions.

Our review extends the current review literature on non-pharmacological interventions for SLE in several key ways. First, we searched for studies of remotely delivered interventions, including digital interventions. This is because, to our knowledge, no published reviews have specifically focused on remotely delivered interventions for patients with SLE. Additionally, our review included both experimental and quasi-experimental studies. This is an important extension of a recent systematic review that only included experimental trials of non-pharmacological interventions for patients with SLE.12 Using our expanded criteria, we obtained a larger number of studies examining non-pharmacological interventions for patients with SLE. This allowed for a more comprehensive assessment of published literature in this area. We also assessed for risk of bias to evaluate the quality of the studies and determine potential improvements for future research in this area. Given findings showing bias most commonly due to missing data and selection bias, future research should be attentive in reporting all outcomes assessed.

We did not find any studies of digital non-pharmacological interventions that met our search criteria. This is unfortunate given the potential for scale and increasing access to care that digital interventions offer.46 Given the relatively novel field of digital interventions for SLE, it is possible such trials are underway but were left out of our search given our exclusion of protocols and grey literature and inclusion criteria of quasi-experimental and experimental studies that specifically target improvement of quality of life and psychological outcomes.47 However, we did find a substantial number of interventions that were delivered remotely. The efficacy of remotely delivered interventions for improving patients’ QOL and psychological outcomes is noteworthy for this population given the number of physiological comorbidities that interfere with mobility.

One concern that emerged from this review is that the demographic characteristics of samples included in these studies are not representative of those with SLE. Among the subset of studies in this review that provided information on the demographic sample selected, most participants identified as White or Caucasian and 98% identified as women. Although SLE is more common in women than men, 10% of men are affected by SLE,48 highlighting the underrepresentation of males in studies captured in this review. Further, the incident rates for SLE is three to four times higher in Black or African-American women than White or Caucasian women, and there is an increased risk of developing SLE in racial and ethnic minorities, including Asian American or Pacific Islander, Native American, and Hispanic women.49 Yet, individuals who identify as these races or ethnicities were underrepresented compared to individuals who identify as White. Moving forward, future research is needed to evaluate non-pharmacological interventions among larger, more representative samples of the population affected by this disease and determine whether culturally sensitive tailoring is needed for these interventions. Additionally, only a small proportion of the studies included in this review actually assessed acceptability and satisfaction, so it is important for future researchers to be attentive to the user experience, a mechanism by which there could be changes in clinical outcomes.50

Strengths and Limitations:

One strength of this study is that we obtained a wider perspective on the number of non-pharmacological interventions for patients with SLE compared to previous reviews. Unlike previous reviews, we did not limit the search to only randomized controlled trials; we expanded the search to include quasi-experimental studies as well as used a comprehensive search string for digital technology-based interventions. We also evaluated risk of bias for all studies.5 Consistent with best practices, two independent reviewers coded all studies. However, study limitations should be noted. Although we worked with a medical research librarian to conduct the search strategy, it is still possible that some relevant publications were missed in the search. By expanding our search to include quasi-experimental studies, we evaluated additional non-pharmacological interventions beyond only experimental studies, but with the limitation of a serious risk of bias. Additionally, a subset of the randomized experimental studies was considered high risk, which means our results could be biased due to some studies that have poor quality. These biases should be corrected in future randomized or non-randomized trials. Future research for higher quality randomized controlled trials is warranted.

Conclusion:

This systematic review evaluated non-pharmacological interventions for patients with SLE and found that interventions as an adjunct for usual medical care may be beneficial for improving patients’ QOL and psychological outcomes. These findings are important given the number of psychological comorbidities associated with the disease as well as impairments in QOL. Results were promising in terms of efficacy of remotely delivered and face-to-face non-pharmacological interventions. Moreover, patients’ perceptions of acceptability and satisfaction were generally positive among studies assessing these factors. Future research should include more representative samples in rigorous evaluations and consider ways to incorporate digital technologies to increase accessibility.
