Anorectal manometry to diagnose dyssynergic defecation: Systematic review and meta-analysis of diagnostic test accuracy

Abstract

Background:

Chronic constipation is a common condition, and dyssynergic defecation underlies up to 40% of cases. Anorectal manometry is recommended to assess for dyssynergic defecation among chronically constipated patients but remains poorly standardized. We aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry and determine optimal testing parameters.

Methods:

We performed a systematic review with meta-analysis of diagnostic test accuracy including cohort studies of chronically constipated patients and case–control studies of patients with dyssynergic defecation or healthy controls. Meta-analysis was performed to determine summary sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Key Results:

A total of 15 studies comprising 2140 patients were included. Including all studies (estimating optimal diagnostic accuracy), the AUC was 0.78 [95% CI 0.72–0.82], summary sensitivity was 79% [61%–90%], and summary specificity was 64% [44%–79%] to diagnose dyssynergic defecation. In cohort studies only (estimating real-world diagnostic accuracy), the AUC was 0.72 [0.66–0.77], summary sensitivity was 86% [64%–95%], and summary specificity was 49% [30%–68%]. Employing three consecutive simulated defecation attempts improved sensitivity to 94%. A fourth simulated defecation maneuver with air insufflation may improve accuracy. Measuring anorectal pressures to identify complex dyssynergic patterns did not improve real-world diagnostic accuracy estimates over anal pressure measurement alone. Choice of manometry system did not impact diagnostic accuracy.

Conclusions & Inferences:

Following the current iteration of the London consensus protocol (three simulated defecation attempts measuring anal relaxation), the role of anorectal manometry in evaluating dyssynergic defecation appears limited. Future iterations of this protocol may improve diagnostic accuracy.

INTRODUCTION

1 |

Chronic constipation is a common general health condition, accounting for eight million primary care visits, two million new gastroenterology referrals, and 700,000 emergency department visits annually in the United States.1–3 Chronic constipation is costly to the healthcare system, with over $1.3 billion spent in over-the-counter laxatives and an additional $8.1 billion spent on other direct healthcare costs each year.4 Chronic constipation is stratified into two basic types: transit-related disorders of the colon (slow and normal transit) and rectal evacuation disorders. The most common cause for rectal defecatory disorder is dyssynergic defecation, characterized by lack of appropriate relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles and/or lack of adequate rectal propulsive forces during attempted defecation.5 Unlike slow transit constipation which often responds to laxative therapies, dyssynergic defecation does not consistently improve with laxatives and is often optimally treated with biofeedback training and pelvic floor physical therapy.6 This important clinical distinction provides the rationale for clinical guidelines which recommend physiologic anorectal testing in constipated patients who do not improve with empiric laxative therapy.7 Offering anorectal testing enables gastroenterologists to break the cycle of ineffective dietary and laxative recommendations for up to 30%–40% of constipated patients who seek specialist advice.8

Anorectal manometry is a common anorectal test which uses pressure sensors to measure anal sphincter relaxation during defecation.9 Unfortunately, adoption of anorectal manometry and other anorectal tests in the broader clinical community remains poor.10 This is likely driven by two lingering clinical questions which are pivotal to acceptance of this testing by the broader clinical audience:

Is anorectal manometry actually useful in routine practice?

This question is pertinent across gastroenterology considering the two million referrals for chronic constipation annually and positioning of anorectal function testing as first-line testing in AGA practice guidelines.11,12 Rome IV criteria for dyssynergic defecation are neither practical nor feasible in a broader context, as they require patients to undergo a battery of anorectal tests to look for concordance among multiple test results which are largely unavailable to most practices.13

If anorectal manometry is useful, what protocol should I use to perform and interpret this test?

Among the relatively few centers that perform anorectal manometry (compared to burden of disease), no two centers had identical protocols for patient preparation, setup, study, or data interpretation, and no center fully complied with published guidelines in a recent international survey of all motility laboratories registered with the European Society of Neurogastroenterology & Motility (ESNM) and American Neurogastroenterology and Motility Society (ANMS).10,14 To address these setbacks, the International Anorectal Physiology Working Group was convened by the major international motility societies to develop the recently published London consensus protocol which provides expert guidance on key technical performance and interpretation parameters, similar to development of the Chicago Classification system for esophageal manometry which is now widely adopted in clinical practice.15,16 The London consensus protocol was developed using a combination of best available data and expert consensus; its utility in clinical practice has not been validated.

Toward clarifying the appropriate role and technique for anorectal manometry in evaluating such a common complaint as chronic constipation, we performed a systematic review with meta-analysis of diagnostic test accuracy of anorectal manometry in diagnosing dyssynergic defecation among chronically constipated patients, and to identify key technical performance and interpretation parameters which optimize sensitivity and specificity.

METHODS

2 |

Literature search

2.1 |

Our study conformed to standard methodologic recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Diagnostic Test Accuracy and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.17,18 We performed a systematic literature search of PubMED and EMBASE databases (1950–2019), Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and major conference abstracts from 2006 to December 2019 to identify studies performing anorectal manometry and at least one other anorectal test recognized in the Rome IV criteria as appropriate for assessing defecatory function (defined as balloon expulsion test, defecography, or electromyography) on patients with chronic constipation and/or healthy non-constipated adults at least 18 years of age.13 Our search strings were designed to capture a broad range of pelvic floor studies to screen for the appropriate data. Our PubMED search string was as follows: (‘anorectal manometry’ [All Fields] OR ‘Diagnostic Tests, Routine’ [Mesh]) AND (‘constipation’ [MeSH Terms] OR ‘constipation’ [All Fields] OR OR ‘Fecal Incontinence’ [Mesh] OR ‘fecal incontinence’ [All Fields] OR ‘rectal sensation’ [All Fields] OR ‘rectal hyposensitivity’ [All Fields] OR ‘rectal hypersensitivity’ [All Fields] OR ‘visceral hypersensitivity’ [All Fields]). Our EMBASE search string was as follows: (‘constipation’/exp OR ‘constipation’ OR ‘feces incontinence’/exp OR ‘feces incontinence’) AND anorectal AND manometry. Studies identified in prior systematic reviews evaluating anorectal manometry in chronically constipated patients were also considered. Studies were translated into English language when necessary. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in Table 1.

Studies were pooled into two groups to enable simultaneous analyses of (1) real-world diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry in evaluating chronic constipation, and (2) optimal diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry in diagnosing dyssynergic defecation. To assess real-world diagnostic accuracy, we pooled cohort studies of chronically constipated patients in whom the presence of dyssynergic defecation was not known, as well as clinical trials of chronically constipated patients with extractable baseline anorectal testing data. To estimate optimal diagnostic accuracy, case–control studies were added to these data which defined a case by either the presence of chronic constipation symptoms or by known dyssynergic defecation (as defined by study authors), in comparison with healthy non-constipated controls.

Data extraction

2.2 |

Data were systematically extracted into a spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel 365, Microsoft) to capture study characteristics (country of origin, enrollment criteria), patient demographics (age and sex distribution), presence of chronic constipation symptoms, and the reference test measuring anorectal function (balloon expulsion test, defecography, or electromyography). We extracted five key technical and interpretation parameters: (1) device manufacturer, model, and type (single sensor pull-through catheter, high-resolution catheter providing longitudinal data through the anal canal (2D), or 3-dimensional catheter providing cross-sectional data through the anal canal) (3D), (2) patient positioning during testing (lateral supine or seated upright), (3) manometric criteria for diagnosing dyssynergic defecation (impaired anal relaxation pressures alone, or assessing both rectal and anal pressures to classify patients on more complex pressure patterns19), (4) the number of simulated defecations during testing protocol without air insufflated into the rectal balloon, and (5) the number of simulated defecations with air insufflated into the rectal balloon to mimic stool. Data extraction was performed independently by two authors (AO and RR), and discrepancies were resolved by consensus among these study authors. Study quality and risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias QUADAS-2 tool.20

Definition of dyssynergic defecation

2.3 |

Rome IV criteria define dyssynergic defecation by the presence of chronic constipation symptoms and at least two concordant anorectal tests.13 As our study necessarily considered anorectal manometry to be investigational, applying the Rome IV criteria would require all patients to have undergone several anorectal tests, representing only a fraction of patients in practice and increasing the potential for bias in our study findings. Furthermore, there is insufficient evidence to evaluate the Rome IV definition in existing literature, representing a knowledge gap; no eligible studies provided sufficient data to assess the diagnostic performance of anorectal manometry against the Rome IV definition of dyssynergic defecation as the reference standard. Consistent with recommendations in Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guidelines on how to define the gold standard in this situation, in a manner which minimizes diagnostic bias, we modified the definition of dyssynergic defecation as the presence of chronic constipation symptoms plus at least one reference anorectal test (balloon expulsion test, defecography, or electromyography) showing abnormal anorectal function during simulated defecation. We extensively conducted sensitivity analyses around this definition to assess anchoring effects of varying reference tests (or combinations of reference tests) on the stability of diagnostic accuracy estimates.21

Study endpoints

2.4 |

The primary endpoint was area under the curve (AUC) for anorectal manometry in diagnosing dyssynergic defecation. AUC measures the discriminant ability of a diagnostic test to accurately stratify diseased patients from healthy patients and ranges from 0.50 (no diagnostic advantage over a coin flip) to 1.00 (perfect test).

We assessed the AUC of anorectal manometry in diagnosing dyssynergic defecation among patients presenting with chronic constipation to estimate ‘real-world diagnostic accuracy in broader practice’. We then assessed the AUC of ‘optimal diagnostic accuracy in enriched tertiary care populations’ by adding case–control studies enrolling cases with known dyssynergic defecation (per study authors) and healthy non-constipated controls. Summary operating sensitivity and specificity as well as diagnostic odds ratios (DOR) were evaluated in both analyses. Summary likelihood ratios (LR) of having dyssynergic defecation based on anorectal manometry results were also reported in ‘real-world diagnostic accuracy in broader practice’ analysis.

Statistical analysis

2.5 |

Binary data were reported using percentages and counts, and continuous data were reported using means. Meta-analysis was performed using a bivariate mixed-effects regression model.22,23 To determine summary diagnostic OR and LR, we applied a continuity correction factor of 0.5 to cells with zero events in each 2 × 2 table.24 Receiver operator curves were developed to evaluate the diagnostic ability of anorectal manometry by assessing trade-offs between varying estimates of sensitivity or specificity. 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for diagnostic performance estimates. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using an I2 statistic. Publication bias was assessed using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test. Meta-regression was performed to assess the effects of study and patient characteristics, technical parameters, and reference testing on summary sensitivity and specificity. Statistical analysis was carried out using STATA (version 15.1, STATACorp).

High levels of between-study heterogeneity precluded meta-regression to assess the effects of key technical factors on diagnostic test performance in pooled global studies. After excluding two well-conducted studies which differed primarily on recruitment strategy, meta-regression was possible in the sub-analysis of remaining studies. Importantly, all of the 15 eligible studies were included in the primary analyses of diagnostic test performance. Possible explanations for heterogeneity are discussed extensively in study limitations.

RESULTS

3 |

Of 3603 abstracts reviewed in our literature search, we included 15 eligible studies involving 2140 chronically constipated or non-constipated individuals (mean age 51.0 years, 78.6% female) undergoing anorectal testing to diagnose dyssynergic defecation (Figure 1). Study characteristics, patient demographics, and test parameters are detailed in Table 2. All studies evaluated anorectal manometry performed in left or right lateral decubitus positions. One trial reported anorectal manometry results on both electromyography and defecography as distinct reference tests which were analyzed separately in sensitivity analysis.25 The risk of bias and rated study quality was heterogenous, and one study presented a high risk of bias (Table S1). Interrater reliability between reviewers during data extraction was strong (κ statistic = 0.81).

Two eligible studies reported significant differences in observed diagnostic accuracy compared to other studies, so much so that meta-regression on key technical factors was not possible when these studies were included. These differences are likely explained by differences in recruitment strategy rather than study quality: One study enrolled older individuals compared to other studies while the other enrolled individuals with the lowest pre-test probability of having dyssynergic defecation (16%) compared to other studies.26,27 To optimize the integrity and transparency of the diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry based on all available evidence, these studies were included in meta-analysis but excluded from meta-regression.

Estimating real-world diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry in broader practice

3.1 |

Ten cohort studies reported 1663 anorectal testing assessments in patients presenting with chronic constipation symptoms.25–34 Summary diagnostic accuracy is described in Table 3. The AUC was 0.72 (95% CI 0.66–0.77) with 86% summary sensitivity (95% CI 64–95) and 49% summary specificity (95% CI 30–68) to diagnose dyssynergic defecation using anorectal manometry alone with a diagnostic OR of 6 (95% CI 2–15) (Figure 2A). The positive LR was 1.7 (95% CI 1.2–2.3), and the negative LR was 0.29 (95% CI 0.13–0.68). There was no evidence of publication bias (p = 0.22), but there was a high level of between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 99%).

Estimating optimal diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry in enriched tertiary care populations

3.2 |

In combining case–control studies with cohort studies, 15 studies enrolling 2140 patients were included.25–39 The AUC was 0.78 (0.72–0.82) with summary sensitivity of 79% (95% CI 61–90) and summary specificity of 64% (95% CI 44–79) in diagnosing dyssynergic defecation, with a diagnostic OR of 6 (95% CI 3–14) (Figure 2B). There was a high level of between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 99%) but no evidence of publication bias (p = 0.20).

Choice of manometry system

3.3 |

In joint analyses of pooled studies for both real-world and optimal diagnostic accuracy analysis cohorts, there was no evidence to suggest significant differences in diagnostic accuracy among pull-through, 2D high-resolution, or 3D high definition anorectal manometry catheter types (p > 0.10 in all analyses).

Optimizing the study protocol

3.4 |

Simulated defecation is the hallmark maneuver to evaluate anorectal function with anorectal manometry. In cohort studies (to estimate real-world diagnostic accuracy in broader practice), performing three attempts at simulated defecation,28,29,32,36,38,39 compared to only one attempt,30,31 increased the sensitivity of anorectal manometry to 94% (Table 4). Findings were similar when case–control studies were included to estimate optimal diagnostic accuracy in enriched tertiary care populations (data not shown).

Two studies performed an additional defecation maneuver with air insufflated into the rectal balloon (to mimic stool).31,32 Adding this maneuver improved estimates of summary sensitivity when case–control studies were included (94% with this maneuver vs. 77% without this maneuver, p = 0.02), but adding this maneuver did not impact diagnostic accuracy in cohort studies alone.

Optimizing the interpretation protocol

3.5 |

Dyssynergic defecation can be identified during simulated defecation by assessing for adequate anal relaxation alone (vs. inataquate anal relaxation suggestive of dyssynergic defecation), or by assessing for both adequate increase in intrarectal pressure with anal relaxation to produce a variety of complex dyssynergic defecation patterns and classifications on manometry.40 Based on available literature, there was no evidence that adding more complex anorectal pressure assessments to basic anal canal pressure measurements improved diagnostic accuracy (p > 0.10 in all analyses).

Estimating the utility of anorectal manometry as a standalone test in broader practice

3.6 |

The prevalence of dyssynergic defecation was 45.3% in pooled global studies of chronically constipated patients. Based on the observed likelihood ratios, an abnormal manometry predicts a 58% (57%–60%) chance of having dyssynergic defecation for which a trial of biofeedback therapy is warranted, while a normal manometry reduces the likelihood of having a biofeedback-responsive defecatory disorder to 19% (17%–22%) in pooled global cohort studies. If three consecutive simulated defecation attempts were used consistent with the London consensus protocol, a normal manometry appeared to reduce the likelihood of having a biofeedback-responsive defecatory disorder to 10% (95% CI 4–19) as a standalone test.

Additional sensitivity analyses

3.7 |

In meta-regression, diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry improved when electromyography was used as the reference test anchoring the modified definition of dyssynergic defecation, resulting in 94% (95% CI 70–100) sensitivity for ARM in diagnosing dyssynergic defecation in cohort studies and 95% (95% CI 83–100) sensitivity including case–control studies (p = 0.05 in both analyses). We did not detect statistically significant differences in diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry if defecography or balloon expulsion was used as reference tests (p > 0.10).

Age, gender, and year of study did not significantly affect diagnostic accuracy in meta-regression (p > 0.10 in all analyses).

DISCUSSION

4 |

We performed a systematic review with meta-analysis of diagnostic test accuracy involving 15 studies on 2140 patients to assess anorectal manometry in diagnosing dyssynergic defecation among chronically constipated patients. In estimating optimal diagnostic accuracy in enriched tertiary care populations, the AUC was 0.78; this compared to an AUC of 0.72 in estimating real-world diagnostic accuracy in broader practice. Following the London consensus protocol which serves as the initial consensus protocol for performing anorectal manometry more broadly, the sensitivity of anorectal manometry in detecting dyssynergic defecation approaches 90% but the specificity is only 49%. Given the high sensitivity in detecting dyssynergic defecation following the current iteration of the London consensus protocol, anorectal manometry may have a limited role in identifying patients who clearly do not have dyssynergic defecation (given its high sensitivity) in practices that otherwise routinely refer patients with laxative-refractory chronic constipation to biofeedback. Maneuvers that are not included in the current iteration of the London consensus protocol to perform anorectal manometry may yield greater diagnostic accuracy in detecting dyssynergic defecation, but these findings require further validation in prospective studies.

Anorectal testing has long remained poorly standardized, presenting a major barrier to uptake of anorectal testing in the gastroenterology community. Anorectal manometry may only be available in approximately 70 centers in the United States.10 The recent London consensus protocol published in January 2020 represents one of the first attempts to standardize the protocol for anorectal manometry, building on prior success of the Chicago testing protocol and classification system developed over the last two decades for esophageal motility testing.15 Yet, this protocol remains to be clinically validated. Furthermore, dyssynergic defecation remains a ‘minor disorder’ in this classification system, despite representing an important part of anorectal physiologic evaluation in practice (and a core reason to perform anorectal function testing at all, within the context of practice guidelines targeting a broad audience). The logical question that follows: ‘Is anorectal manometry actually appropriate to assess dyssynergic defecation?’ Recognizing the strong, entrenched, and divergent opinions in this field, but also the clinical need and concern among general gastroenterologists (who are not anorectal physiology experts) to understand the evidence, a rigorous appraisal of an extensive body of evidence by systematic review is justified in a manner which minimizes bias.41

In evaluating several key technical and interpretation parameters for anorectal manometry, we found that performing three sequential simulated defecation attempts (rather than only one or two attempts) optimized diagnostic accuracy to detect dyssynergic defecation. There was insufficient evidence to support differences in diagnostic accuracy among currently available anorectal manometry systems. There was insufficient evidence to support that diagnostic accuracy would improve by adding rectal pressure measurements to basic anal relaxation pressure measurements, or by adding a maneuver to simulate defecation with air insufflated into the rectal balloon. All studies had the patient lying in a left or right lateral decubitus position. Overall, these findings support the London consensus protocol (three simulated defecation attempts measuring anal relaxation) as the optimal protocol for performing anorectal manometry in clinical practice.

Our synthesis of anorectal manometry studies performed on over 2000 patients contrasts with the existing Rome IV framework, which conceptualizes how we define dyssynergic defecation using objective anorectal tests.42 The Rome IV diagnostic criteria require the presence of constipation symptoms and two anorectal tests to diagnose dyssynergic defecation, which may be burdensome in broader practice. We showed that the AUC for anorectal manometry is impacted by low specificity for ruling in dyssynergic defecation. For practices that refer patients for biofeedback based on the presence of dyssynergic defecation, balloon expulsion appears to be the more relevant test within the context of the current iteration of the London consensus protocol. However, anorectal manometry appears sufficient in ruling out dyssynergic defecation as a standalone test due to its high sensitivity. For the subset of practices that otherwise empirically refer patients with laxative-refractory chronic constipation to biofeedback guided by shared decision-making (and have sufficient expertise to support such a practice), anorectal manometry following the London consensus protocol may be useful in this specific setting to identify patients who clearly do not have dyssynergic defecation.

Contrasting our results with those of our prior systematic review evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of balloon expulsion testing for dyssynergic defecation, balloon expulsion testing may be the more clinically relevant anorectal test if a single test must be chosen. In fact, the diagnostic accuracy of balloon expulsion testing alone is superior to anorectal manometry in diagnosing dyssynergic defecation (AUC of 0.84 for balloon expulsion under optimal conditions, or 0.80 under real-world conditions of chronic constipation symptoms with no prior testing).43 This also recognizes the facility, capital, and personnel costs of developing the motility laboratory needed to support anorectal manometry.44 Our findings present a solution to the reality that most gastroenterology centers simply have no chance to comply with Rome IV, ESNM and AGA guidance which strongly advocate anorectal physiology evaluation—due to a lack of adequate diagnostic anorectal physiology equipment or expertise. When weighed against the sheer prevalence of chronic constipation accounting for over two million new patient gastroenterology consultations each year, balloon expulsion testing likely represents a cost-effective, point-of-care opportunity for general gastroenterologists to appropriately evaluate their patients (compared to a status quo of routinely performing no anorectal evaluation at all).11 Moreover, clinical trial data provide evidence that an inability to defecate a rectal balloon suggests 71% treatment response to biofeedback therapy as a safe and well-tolerated treatment measure (compared to 8% treatment response with normal baseline balloon expulsion testing).7 In contrast, data are unfortunately lacking to evaluate the value of baseline anorectal manometry as a standalone test to predict response to biofeedback therapy.15 Clearly, further appropriately designed clinical outcome trials, which link test result to treatment choice, are needed to inform clinicians of the best test(s) to conduct in clinical practice.

Outside of evaluating for dyssynergic defecation, anorectal manometry likely remains clinically relevant for several reasons. First, anorectal manometry provides additional unique physiology data including rectal sensation and tone to assess a broader range of possible anorectal physiology disorders (such as rectal areflexia, anal hypotension, or rectal hyper/hyposensitivity), which are already considered major disorders of anorectal physiology in the recent London consensus in contrast with the anorectal coordination data presented here.15 Second, the standard recommended course of biofeedback therapy provided by physical therapists involves a treat-to-target approach to (1) improve a patient’s ability to expel a balloon from the rectum, (2) improve anal sphincter relaxation during simulated defecation, and (3) normalize rectal hyper or hyposensitivity when present.45 Anorectal manometry is necessary to assess the latter two treatment targets, and simplified anorectal pressure sensors are used by physical therapists during treatment when the protocol is strictly followed. Third, anorectal manometry may represent an opportunity as a biomarker to assess treatment response. In a clinical trial by Rao et al.46 using the ANMS-ESNM biofeedback protocol on 77 patients diagnosed with Rome II dyssynergic defecation, repeating anorectal manometry following treatment resulted in 79% of patients having normal defecatory mechanics post-treatment.

Study strengths include our rigorous methodologic adherence to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Diagnostic Test Accuracy and PRISMA statement, reporting of QUADAS-2 assessments on study quality, and identification of additional eligible studies compared to prior systematic reviews.47,48 Importantly, the aims of prior studies were different than our own and these methodologic differences do not suggest differences on overall study quality. In fact, prior studies critically highlight variability in anorectal testing among gastrointestinal motility and anorectal physiology experts in extraordinary detail. Other prior studies assessed either healthy individuals49 or individuals with dyssynergic defecation50; study designs which are appropriate to define cutoffs for ‘abnormal’ but preclude construction of 2 × 2 cells (representing true/false positives and true/false negatives) to assess diagnostic accuracy. Our findings generally agree with a prior clinical trial adhering rigorously to STARD guidelines that also reported that diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry was poor.51 In contrast with our study, Grossi et al. did not include a physiologic test requirement in defining the gold-standard ‘disease’ and only stratified patients based on the presence and severity of chronic constipation symptoms (vs. no constipation/healthy). However, the poor diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry was driven by a high false-positive rate among non-constipated individuals leading to poor specificity, which we also found in our study. It is likely that the sensitivity is higher in our study due to advancements in the conduct of anorectal manometry since publication of that study: (1) the inclusion of a third simulated defecation maneuver in the London consensus protocol and (2) the possible utility of a fourth simulated defecation maneuver with air insufflation in the rectal balloon as found in our meta-regression. Interestingly, our meta-regression found that these added maneuvers did not improve specificity; thus, our findings still generally agree with Grossi et al. specifically on the disutility of anorectal manometry (in its current form) in making a positive diagnosis of dyssynergic defecation. Recently, a clinical trial by Sharma et al. has been reported suggesting that a seated position may improve diagnostic accuracy and improve specificity, noting that we could not explore the influence of this change in testing protocol on diagnostic accuracy due to differences in study design precluding inclusion of this study in our analysis.52 Overall, our findings suggest a need to further refine the testing protocol before advocating broader use of anorectal manometry in detecting dyssynergic defecation. Rather than abandoning the test completely, and learning from the iterative experience in refining esophageal manometry protocols, further prospective studies following the rigorous design of Grossi et al. may shed light on whether adding both a seated position and fourth attempt at simulated defecation with air insufflation could yield greater diagnostic accuracy—this may inform future iterations of the London consensus protocol.53

Expectedly, a high level of between-study heterogeneity and variation in study quality were evident. These findings may be related to study population differences in age, sex, and parity which likely arise from differences in recruitment strategy. These patient characteristics are risk factors not only for dyssynergic defecation, but also a wide range of structural evacuation disorders (such as rectal prolapse or rectocele) which can mimic dyssynergic defecation on anorectal manometry.54 Normal ranges for anorectal manometry measurements vary by these patient characteristics among asymptomatic healthy individuals.49,55 Subjective differences in test protocols are also important to consider. As an example, the wording used when asking patients to defecate can significantly impact test findings.

According to Rome IV, diagnosing dyssynergic defecation requires two abnormal anorectal function and/or imaging tests. Unfortunately, there were no published studies identified in our evidence synthesis to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry within the context of the current Rome IV definition of dyssynergic defecation advocated in our practice guidelines (i.e., knowledge gap, insufficient evidence). Furthermore, most studies presented an unclear risk of bias. While our study findings may come across as ‘negative’ at first glance, these findings do not diminish the importance of a systematic review of evidence which serves to summarize ‘that which we know and that which remains uncertain’, especially given the burden and economic impact of chronic constipation on our healthcare system and our patients. To the contrary, our evidence synthesis finds that balloon expulsion testing appears to be the preferred test, if a single test must be chosen in situations where adequate facilities to perform comprehensive anorectal physiology assessments are lacking.43 For centers with adequate facilities, we produced a synthesized evidence base supporting Rome IV, the AGA practice guidelines, and the London consensus protocol regarding the indication and conduct of anorectal manometry to evaluate dyssynergic defecation. We also identify a critical need for prospective studies tying diagnostic testing to the current consensus definition for this disease. Recognizing the lack of adequate evidence on anorectal manometry within the context of a gold standard for dyssynergic defecation defined by Rome IV (a knowledge gap recognized in our systematic review), we varied the reference test extensively in sensitivity analysis to assess stability of diagnostic accuracy estimates and performed risk-of-bias assessments.56 We recognize that similar situations of heterogeneity in disease definitions exist across the spectrum of motility disorders and disorders of brain–gut interaction and that heterogeneity in itself does not preclude the conduct of high-quality evidence syntheses.57–59 Anorectal manometry was most concordant with electromyography, which is rational as both anorectal manometry and electromyography directly measure sphincter muscle activity. Diagnostic performance otherwise did not depend on choice of reference test. There were not enough data to evaluate the diagnostic performance of anorectal manometry compared to two or three other reference tests in combination.

On balance, our systematic review with meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy suggests that anorectal manometry has high sensitivity but poor specificity in evaluating dyssynergic defecation using the current iteration of the London consensus protocol. Balloon expulsion appears to be the more relevant test if a single test is needed to evaluate for dyssynergic defecation in laxative-refractory, chronically constipated patients when the availability of physical therapy expertise is limited. The London consensus protocol does support a limited role for anorectal manometry in the context of ruling out dyssynergic defecation in the subset of practice settings in which patients with laxative-refractory chronic constipation are otherwise routinely referred to biofeedback. Learning from the iterative experience toward refining the conduct and appropriate use of esophageal manometry, our findings highlight specific avenues to improve the diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry in evaluating the chronically constipated patient in future clinical trials.

Key points

  • Anorectal manometry is recommended to assess for dyssynergic defecation among chronically constipated patients but remains poorly standardized in practice.

  • In our systematic review and meta-analysis of diagnostic test accuracy, we found that accuracy of anorectal manometry in detecting dyssynergic defecation was limited due to poor specificity.

  • Further iterations of the London consensus protocol may improve diagnostic accuracy.
