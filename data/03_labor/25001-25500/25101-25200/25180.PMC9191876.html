Non-pharmacologic therapies in treatment of childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE): a systematic review

Elizabeth Ross[1]

Khalid Abulaban[1][2]

Elizabeth Kessler[1][2]

Natoshia Cunningham[1][3]

[1] Michigan State University College of Human Medicine

[2] Helen DeVos Children’s Hospital (Rheumatology)

[3] Michigan State University Department of Family Medicine

SUMMARY

Background:

Childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE) is a complex multisystem autoimmune disease often associated with pain, fatigue, and mood-related disturbances. cSLE is associated with increased disease severity and higher rates of mortality as compared to adult onset SLE. Therefore, a multi-faceted approach to care, including the use of non-pharmacologic therapies, is essential to ensure optimal patient outcomes. The use of non-pharmacologic therapies as adjunctive treatments has been shown to be beneficial in adults with SLE, yet, their use and effect is less well understood in cSLE. This is the first systematic review to explore the use and quality of evidence of non-pharmacologic approaches to treat cSLE.

Methods:

A literature review was performed using PRISMA guidelines. Studies until March 2021 with participants diagnosed with cSLE were included. The quality of the evidence was graded via OCEBM levels of evidence guidelines and bias assessed using Cochrane guidelines. Completed clinical trials (via clinicaltrials.gov) were also searched to identify unpublished results.

Results:

Eleven published studies consisting of 1,152 patients met inclusion criteria for this review, as well as 3 additional studies with unpublished data on clinicaltrial.gov. Of the published trials, four studies used patient education/support, 3 studies used dietary supplementation, 3 used forms of psychotherapy (e.g., CBT), and 1 used aerobic exercise to target the following issues: treatment adherence (n=3), quality of life (n=3), fatigue (n=2), pain (n=2), depressive symptoms (n=1), anxiety (n=1), and health-related outcomes including disease severity (n=3), cardiovascular disease risk (CVD; n=3), and muscle function (n=1). Across investigations, the quality of the evidence based on study design was moderate/low. In terms of potential outcomes, dietary supplementation methods were successful in 2 of 3 studies and were associated with improvements in disease activity and fatigue. Aerobic exercise was effective in decreasing resting heart rate and increasing cardiorespiratory capacity. Patient education/support was related to significantly increased treatment adherence and decreased cardiovascular risk markers. Two of the three studies examining the impact of psychotherapy showed improvements (e.g., in treatment adherence, depression and fatigue).

Conclusion:

This review identifies several promising non-pharmacologic therapies to use as adjunctive treatments to traditional pharmacologic regimens in health and mental health- related outcomes in patients with cSLE. Future well controlled clinical trials would be beneficial to more rigorously evaluate the effects of non-pharmacologic therapies in pediatric populations.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic and complex autoimmune disease characterized by multisystem organ damage. When diagnosed prior to age 18, this condition is known as childhood-onset Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (cSLE) (1). Over 300,000 individuals are diagnosed with SLE (1), and 10-20% of new diagnoses occur in adolescence (2), accounting for a prevalence rate of 3.3 to 24 per 100,000 children (3). cSLE is associated with a more severe disease onset and disease burden. This necessitates frequent medical visits and a complex medical regimen (4), consisting of greater use of steroid and immunosuppressive therapies (5,6) and generally increased rates of health care utilization (7). cSLE is associated with a twenty-fold increased risk of mortality compared to those diagnosed with SLE in adulthood (8).

Youth with cSLE have complex medical and psychosocial needs. To combat disease progression, children with cSLE attend frequent medical visits and are placed on complex pharmacologic regimens that can be difficult to follow and have undesirable side effects. Further, one-third of youth with cSLE have a psychological disorder such as depression or anxiety, which is significantly higher than rates observed in community samples (9,10). Psychological disorders in SLE are associated with poor medication adherence and increased health care utilization in children (7) and adults (11-13), further compounding disease outcomes. A large portion of youth with cSLE experience fatigue (65%), depressive symptoms (30%), and elevated pain (40%) (14), leading to poorer coping long-term (15). Pharmacologic therapies that target disease activity do not universally address symptoms (e.g., depression, anxiety, fatigue, and pain) that are potentially modifiable with non-pharmacological therapies. Yet these symptoms characterize some of the main complaints of children with SLE. At present, there is a lack of accessible comprehensive and evidence-based treatment options to address the complex needs of patients with cSLE.

Some non-pharmacologic therapies such as lifestyle modifications (exercise, dietary supplementation) and psychotherapy have had promising results in combating mood symptoms and fatigue in healthy children and adults (16-18). Furthermore, a recent review by Fangtham et al characterized current approaches to non-pharmacologic therapies in adult SLE, identifying forms of psychotherapy and exercise as effective in reducing depression, anxiety, pain, and fatigue, as well as increasing self-reported quality of life (19). However, there have been no reviews to date examining the usefulness of non-pharmacologic therapies in treating cSLE, which is critically important given the increased risk for mortality and the unique developmental considerations (e.g. increased risk of poor treatment adherence and mood disorders) in this population. Therefore, this review aims to summarize current non-pharmacologic therapies, the quality of the evidence, and their potential effectiveness in treating the whole range of symptoms (disease- and mental health-related) experienced by cSLE patients.

METHODS

Articles for the study were identified by systematic search via Cochrane, EMBASE, PubMed, and the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL). Standardized search terms and key words were used across databases to identify articles related to ‘non-pharmacologic therapies’, ‘childhood- onset SLE’, and related treatment methods (‘exercise’, ‘behavior therapy’, ‘diet’, ‘complementary therapies’). The search encompassed all studies to March 2021.

Included articles were studies directly addressing different non-pharmacologic therapies in individuals diagnosed with cSLE. One investigator (E.A.R.) completed the search and ensured quality with the support of a medical librarian, with articles included in the review being confirmed by another investigator (N.R.C.). Any studies in question were included by consensus within the team. Information on the study design, population sampled, interventions, and outcomes was extracted.

Quality of studies were graded based on Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) levels of evidence (20), with a Grade 1 indicating the highest level of evidence corresponding to a systematic review of randomized trials. Grade 2 covers randomized control trials and Grade 3 includes non-randomized controlled studies. Grade 4 (case study/ case-control study) and 5 (mechanistic studies) were not utilized in this review. Risk of bias was also assessed using Cochrane review guidelines and charted with the robvis visualization tool (https://www.riskofbias.info/welcome/robvis-visualization-tool). Each study was labeled as low, high, or some risk of bias based on 5 categories: bias arising from randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended intervention, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result. Furthermore, reporting bias due to missing results (e.g., unpublished works) was investigated via a search of clinicaltrials.gov for completed protocols related to cSLE.

Published studies were qualitatively synthesized and then grouped into categories according to the non-pharmacologic therapy being utilized, including: diet supplementation, aerobic exercise, patient education/support, and psychotherapy. Scientific rationale for the approaches is briefly described. Targets of treatment were also examined, and multiple targets (fatigue, pain, mood symptoms, treatment adherence, cardiovascular disease risk, quality of life, and disease severity) were commonly assessed by each study, showing the wide range of effects of non-pharmacologic therapies. A meta-analysis was not performed due to heterogeneity of studies, interventions and targets.

RESULTS

Literature search

Figure 1 shows the flow of the published literature search following PRISMA guidelines. In total, 1,463 records were identified from four libraries meeting initial search criteria. Once duplicates were removed and title/abstract review was performed, 44 studies were fully analyzed. Eleven of those resources were assessed to meet the criteria to be included in this review. Thirty-three studies were excluded based on: lack of treatment (n=16), ongoing project/study design only (n=6), presentation abstracts (n=5), not cSLE specific (n=4), and case study design (n=2). Table 1 shows completed investigations registered in clinicaltrial.gov that were not included in the published literature (n=3 studies identified). None of these studies have published results to date, though one investigation has published their study protocol (21).

Participants

Eleven published studies with a total of 1,152 participants were included in this review (see Figure 2 and Table 2 for additional details). 1,075 of those participants were female (93%), with 5 studies including only female participants. Overall age range of participants was 7 to 24 years. Race data was not provided in 5 studies, but of the remaining 7 studies 433 of 1,026 patients self-identified as African American, Hispanic, or Asian American (42%). All patients studied were diagnosed with cSLE prior to age 18, and the diagnosis was confirmed using American College of Rheumatology (ACR) or International Classification of Disease-9/10 criteria. Disease activity was measured by either Systemic Lupus Activity Questionnaire (SLAQ) or Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) scores.

Interventions

Dietary supplementation

Three of the eleven studies included in this review assessed the effect of dietary supplementation on various disease targets (e.g., disease activity, fatigue, and muscle function).

Royal Jelly (22)

Royal jelly is a compound produced in the hypopharyngeal and mandibular glands of young nurse worker bees (Apis mellifera) that has previously demonstrated immunomodulatory properties in autoimmune mouse models (34, 35).

Zahran et al tested the use of royal jelly on disease severity in an open label trial over 12 weeks (3 months) in twenty youth with cSLE (ages 7-16). This was the first study to assess the use of royal jelly as an adjunctive treatment of cSLE. The quality of evidence is low (evidence grade of 3) due to the lack of randomization in the study design, and it has a high risk of bias based on Cochrane guidelines, given lack of randomization and outcome measurement (see Figure 2). Royal jelly caused minor symptoms including transient itching (1 patient), skin rash (1 patient), and stomach pain (2 patients), and all patients continued treatment. Findings indicated the royal jelly supplementation (2 grams, daily) was associated with statistically and clinically significantly decreased SLEDAI scores (SLEDAI scores decreased from 11.3 to 8.3, moving from high to moderate activity).

Vitamin D (23)

Vitamin D has been shown previously to have important immunologic effects, modulating dendritic cell (36-39), B cell, and T cell responses (40-42) and low levels being linked to development of autoimmunity and increased severity of established disease (43, 44). There is evidence that cSLE patients show higher rates of vitamin D deficiency than age-matched controls (45, 46).

Lima et al investigated the effect of 50,000 IU/week oral calciferol (vitamin D) tablets over 6 months on disease activity (SLEDAI) and fatigue in 40 female cSLE patients. This randomized control trial received an evidence grade of 2 due to its design, as well as a low risk of bias. Four patients given vitamin D (and 2 in the control group) reported abdominal pain, without discontinuation of therapy. Compared to the control group, who received an identical placebo tablet and follow-up, SLEDAI scores at 6 months compared to baseline were significantly improved in treatment group (from 4 to 3 SLEDAI score vs no change in control). This decrease in SLEDAI was statistically significant, but not necessarily clinically significant as these patients remained in the mild disease category. Fatigue was assessed using the Kids Fatigue Severity Scale (K-FSS). At the end of the study, cSLE patients receiving the vitamin D supplementation had a significantly decreased global fatigue score than placebo group, with special emphasis being placed on significant social life improvement in the treatment group.

Creatine (24)

Creatine supplementation has been shown to enhance muscle strength and function in other pediatric diseases (ex: muscular dystrophies), as well as increase bone density in patients with Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophies (47-49). Therefore, this approach was tested in cSLE, as many patients can experience musculoskeletal abnormalities (50).

Hyashi et al assessed the use of a single dose of 0.1g/kg creatine monohydrate in 15 youths (age 10-18) after 12 weeks compared to placebo-given controls. They assessed muscle function and bone mineral density (as well as secondary measures such as quality of life via Pediatric Quality of Life inventory (PedsQL)). Muscle function was analyzed using one- maximum repetition tests (1-RM) on bench press and leg press exercises, as well as the timed-up-and-go test and timed-stands. Bone mineral density was measured via dual X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan. A grade of 2 was given due to this randomized control trial design, and it had a low risk of bias. There were no self-reported adverse events with creatine supplementation. Creatine concentration in the muscle, however, showed that there was no statistical significance between the control group and treatment group or within the treatment group compared to baseline.

Summary:

The overall quality of evidence was low, with a low risk of bias. Of the extent studies, royal jelly and vitamin D supplementation were effective at reducing SLEDAI (royal jelly even being clinically significant) and fatigue (as measured in the vitamin D trial). Creatine supplementation was not shown to have any effect on muscle function, thought to be due to poor concentration in the muscle tissue of the patients. All treatments were generally well tolerated, with some minor symptoms reported for royal jelly and vitamin D. Additional well controlled trials are needed that focus on standardized dosing and methodology for individual therapies.

Aerobic Exercise

Only 1 study included in this review assessed the effect of aerobic exercise on cardiorespiratory outcomes in youth with cSLE (25).

Exercise has been shown to be a helpful adjunctive therapy in other pediatric rheumatologic disease (juvenile dermatomyositis (51, 52) and fibromyalgia (53)), as well as in adult SLE (54-56). cSLE patients are at increased risk of developing cardiovascular disease as adults, and many present with reduced exercise capacity and physical function compared to healthy controls (50, 57). Exercise has also been shown to be beneficial in the management of depression, a main presenting complaint of patients with cSLE, although this was not specifically examined in the study described below (58).

Prado et al compared healthy controls and non-treated cSLE patients to cSLE patients that received 12 weeks of twice weekly supervised aerobic exercise, including 29 females aged 7 to 15 years of age. Patients went through a 5-minute warm up, followed by a 20–50-minute treadmill exercise, and finished with a 5-minute cool down on the treadmill and stretching (considered moderate intensity). In this randomized control trial (evidence grade 2, with some concerns about bias, especially regarding missing outcome data and selection of reported results), exercise tolerance and other cardiorespiratory markers were measured, as well as secondary outcomes of changes in SLEDAI, drug regimen, bone mineral density, and body composition. cSLE patients presented with statistically significant higher resting heart rates and lower exercise tolerance compared to controls. With exercise treatment, these patients showed a statistically significant increase in exercise tolerance and decrease in heart rate, as well as an improved cardiorespiratory capacity comparable to healthy peers. However, no other parameters were statistically significant from control. No adverse events were reported.

Summary:

Exercise treatment may yield cardiovascular improvements in cSLE patients with no adverse events detected. Additional well controlled trials that investigate different levels/types of cardiovascular activities, as well as examine the effect on mental health outcomes, would be helpful.

Patient education/support

Four studies utilized patient education and support to modify treatment adherence and cardiovascular disease risk.

Peer Support (Forum) (26)

Living with chronic health conditions such as cSLE can be a socially isolating experience. Youth with other chronic health conditions report benefits from social support networks, including those found on social media (59). Aside from building a sense of community, utilizing the internet and posting activities also improves a sense of agency in patients to feel empowered in their own decision making (60). Interactive health communication has been shown to improve disease knowledge, clinical outcomes, and social support (61), and in the study below is hypothesized to also improve medication adherence.

Treatment adherence is a special concern for the cSLE patient population, as only 50-60% of patients with cSLE (62, 63) take their medications as prescribed due to complexity of treatment plans, psychological manifestations, and unique developmental challenges of adolescence, especially peer isolation (64). Barriers to adherence include side effects (real or perceived), lack of improvement of daily symptoms, low medical literacy, forgetfulness, cost, and refusal (65, 66). Poor treatment adherence has been linked to higher disease activity scores and subsequent decreased quality of life (67). Therefore, targeting medication adherence through bolstering social support may be beneficial.

Scalzi et al investigated the effect of a social media support forum compared to simple education modules in improving medication adherence in 27 patients aged 13-23. For 8 weeks, both control and treatment groups participated in consecutive weekly online education modules. The control group recorded answers to open ended questions posed by the modules in journals, which were read by study staff, while the treatment group answered the same question on a social media forum designed for discussion between patients. This study received an evidence grade of 2 due to its use of randomization in the study design, but had some concerns regarding bias risk, especially regarding outcome measurement. Medication adherence via Medication Possession Ratio (MPR; [total prescription days of supply/ (last prescription date – the first prescription date)]) and Medication Adherence Self Report Inventory (MASRI) were assessed, showing a statistically significant increase in MPR over study period in treatment group, but not in the control group. There was no difference, however, in MASRI data. Secondary measures such as quality of life, stress levels, self-efficacy, sense of agency, sense of community, and empowerment were also analyzed but not statistically significant, though quality of life and self-efficacy scores nearly reached significant levels compared to control. No adverse effects were reported by authors.

Patient education/support by provider

Personalized prescription treatment plan (27)

The pediatric rheumatologist is often reported to be the patient’s primary medical home (68). Patients place a high level of trust in their rheumatological provider and their team in managing their cSLE and associated symptoms (69). Therefore, enhanced education and support from the rheumatology practice may yield positive impacts on patient health and mental health related functioning. The following investigation targets medication adherence.

Harry et al conducted a randomized control trial (evidence grade 2, potentially high risk of bias due to concerns primarily about outcome measurement, but also missing data, deviation from intended intervention, and result selection) of 19 patients aged 18-24 years, using a personalized prescription treatment plan consisting of information about medication and current steps forward, plus automated reminders for a month over a 3-month study period in the treatment group. Both treatment and control group received SimpleMed+ pillboxes (an automated storage method for medication that can send reminders and reports use data), but control group did not have access to the other educational resources (treatment as usual). When comparing MASRI scores between the 2 groups, the baseline objective adherence was higher in the treatment group than the control group despite randomization. However, the control group decreased in adherence versus increased adherence observed in treatment, though these results were not statistically significant. No adverse effects were reported by the authors.

Nutritional education (28, 29)

For the reasons mentioned above, the rheumatology provider may be ideally suited to address nutritional health in the context of a multidisciplinary team. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is becoming more and more commonly recognized as an important sequela to SLE, with adults diagnosed with SLE 2-17 times more likely to suffer from non-fatal myocardial infarction and coronary artery disease than the rest of the population (70). This becomes increasingly concerning when studies have shown that children diagnosed with SLE have higher frequency of dyslipidemia (71, 72) and other risk factors for CVD (increased body fat mass and BMI (73, 74)). Environmental and social factors play a key role in addition to disease process, with one study linking dyslipidemia and increased BMI in parents with children diagnosed with SLE (75). Early detection and treatment of CVD is imperative in decreasing risk of end organ damage, including myocardial infarction, stroke, kidney failure. The rheumatology provider and their team may enhance patient outcomes through a holistic approach to care that includes dietary modification.

Cardiovascular risk factors in cSLE patients were targeted by Abad et al and da Silva et al (both receiving a grade of 2 with some concerns regarding risk of bias due to potential missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of results). They utilized the same cohort subjected to 6 monthly meetings with a nutritionist followed by 3 monthly reinforcement meetings, accompanied with supporting educational materials to assess changes in cardiovascular risk factors compared to a treatment as usual control condition. Abad et al focused on examining self-reported dietary composition, body fat mass, and BMI in 31 female patients aged 10-19 years. da Silva et al further analyzed this cohort for changes in lipid profiles with the same education and coaching. They were primarily interested in observing changes in serum lipid metabolism biomarkers (HDL, LDL, triglycerides) and PONa (paraoxanase 1), an enzyme seen to be reduced in adult SLE and negatively correlated with cardiovascular disease risk. Dietary coaching and education were found to significantly decrease carbohydrate intake compared to matched controls, as well as total fat, saturated fat, and trans-fat. Further, there was no significant difference between baseline BMI and body fat mass in treatment group after the 9-month intervention, compared to increases in BMI and body fat observed in the control group. This indicates a protective effect of dietary education. There were no adverse effects reported by the authors.

A follow up investigation of this cohort found sdLDL worsened in the control group over the 9-month study, but no significant changes were observed in treatment group. PONa/HDL ratio (both measures of decreased CVD risk) was significantly increased in the treatment group compared to control by the end of the study. This suggests the protective effect of dietary coaching and education on different measures of cardiovascular disease risk. Lastly, there were no adverse effects reported by the authors.

Summary:

The overall quality of evidence was adequate, all utilizing randomized control trials, but there were some concerns regarding risk of bias. The effect of support forums and personalized prescription treatment plans on treatment adherence were analyzed, with support forums showing a significant improvement in MPR. Furthermore, dietary coaching and education were effective at targeting different measures of CVD risk. There were no adverse events reported by the authors. Additional investigation is needed in this area utilizing standardized methodological measures.

Psychotherapy

Three studies in this review utilized forms of psychotherapy or psychiatric factors impacting medication adherence, fatigue, pain, and mood symptoms.

Mood and fatigue symptoms are common presenting complaints in cSLE that dramatically decrease patient quality of life and make disease management more complicated for a variety of reasons, particularly given the association with decreased treatment adherence (76, 77). Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is an empirically supported approach for treating fatigue, mood symptoms, and pain in clinical populations and populations with other chronic health conditions, including adult SLE (18). CBT has also shown promise in the management of other pediatric chronic health conditions such as type 1 diabetes (78), juvenile fibromyalgia (79), and IBD (80).

Cognitive behavioral therapy (31, 32)

Cunningham et al investigated the effect of a tailored CBT protocol (TEACH) developed from evidence-based protocols for managing pain, fatigue, and mood (79, 81). Fourteen patients aged 13-19 attended 6 weekly sessions for an hour with a doctoral-level psychologist. This study received a grade of 3 due to the lack of randomization, with some concerns about risk of bias regarding randomization and outcome measurement. Brown et al took a similar approach, utilizing CBT (focusing on cognitive restructuring and coping skills) and education over 6 weekly 45-minute sessions to assess changes in pain and quality of life compared to an education only and no-contact control group in 53 patients aged 12-18. This study received a grade of 2, but had the potential for high risk of bias due to deviations from intended intervention and outcome measurement. Cunningham and colleagues found participants reported significantly decreased depression and fatigue compared to baseline. However, both pain and anxiety measures did not reach significant levels of reduction despite a decrease in clinical symptoms. Brown and colleagues found no significant overall improvement comparing no-contact control to patients receiving CBT. Despite this, increases in individual levels of coping mechanisms like “coping self-statements” and “diverting attention” were significant.

Clinical psychotherapy (30)

Chang et al performed a retrospective cohort study through an online database (Clinformatics DataMart) of 873 cSLE patients (age 10-24) taking hydroxychloroquine to assess the exposure of a psychiatric diagnosis on treatment adherence and, pertinent to this review, examined the effect of psychotherapy utilization (defined in chart review as clinical treatment of at least 4 visits) on treatment adherence via MPR. This study received a lower evidence grade (3) as it lacked randomization, but had low risk of bias aside from that lack of randomization. There were significant increases in treatment adherence in patients with depression, anxiety, and comorbid depression and anxiety who were receiving psychotherapy as compared to patients not receiving psychotherapy. They noted that combination with psychotropic medication further increased MPR to levels above psychotherapy or medication alone. No adverse events were reported by the authors.

Summary:

Overall quality of evidence of these studies were low, with only one study receiving a grade of 2, as well as suffering from overall concerns about risk of bias. There was mixed support for the effect of CBT on outcomes of youth with cSLE; with one study finding no effect and the other reporting a significant effect on decreasing fatigue and depression. Another study indicated some promise of psychotherapy (broadly defined) on adherence-related outcomes. There were no adverse events reported by the authors of these papers. Clearly, additional well controlled trials that allow for replication (e.g., consistent use of manualized treatments) are needed.

DISCUSSION

A holistic approach to managing chronic health conditions such as cSLE that involves a blend of pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic treatments may help optimize patient outcomes. Eleven studies consisting of 1,152 patients were identified to meet the criteria for this review, utilizing a variety of nonpharmacologic therapies (patient education/support, dietary supplementation, psychotherapy, and aerobic exercise) to target a range of cSLE-related outcomes (treatment adherence, quality of life, disease severity, cardiovascular disease risk, fatigue, pain, depressive symptoms, anxiety, and muscle function). The existing literature shows some promise in using certain non-pharmacological therapies (e.g., vitamin D supplementation, cognitive behavioral therapy) in combination with standard medical treatments to improve outcomes of youth with cSLE, but additional well controlled studies are needed.

With regards to the dietary supplementation literature, vitamin D and royal jelly showed promise (e.g., were related to decreased disease activity in individuals with cSLE). These findings support a larger body of literature demonstrating immunomodulatory properties of both vitamin D and royal jelly in preclinical and clinical studies of immune-mediated or inflammatory conditions, with vitamin D being much more thoroughly investigated (36-46). On the other hand, creatine supplementation was not found to be effective in a controlled trial; however, this finding may have been influenced by dosage amount/administration route, as evidenced by lack of supplement concentration in the muscle. Creatine has primarily been investigated in musculoskeletal conditions, showing promise in strengthening muscle and bone. Extended research in cSLE with an increased dose or potentially a direct administration to the muscle might be beneficial, but risk-benefit analysis accounting for patient acceptability is warranted. In the current body of literature on dietary supplementation in cSLE, all supplements reviewed were generally well-tolerated, not requiring any patients to be removed from the studies. Additional research, particularly for vitamin D and royal jelly supplementation, using larger well controlled trials to investigate their effects on disease severity in this population would be beneficial.

Aerobic exercise can be another important component of cSLE disease management. Indeed, decreased resting heart rate and increased cardiorespiratory capacity in cSLE patients was observed in a single controlled study. Clearly, the benefits of aerobic exercise are well known both from a cardiovascular perspective and in term of managing mood symptoms in both clinical and healthy populations of youth (70-75). Additionally, aerobic exercise has been shown to be beneficial in combating mood symptoms and fatigue in other pediatric rheumatologic diseases (e.g., juvenile fibromyalgia) and in adults with SLE (82). Although risk factors for CVD were a target in the current study, more research looking at the impact of aerobic exercise on mood, fatigue, and other aspects of functioning in youth with cSLE, which have been shown to be positively impacted in other chronic disease populations via aerobic exercise, is warranted. Importantly, there was no clinical evidence of adverse events in this trial that provided active support and monitoring. Tailoring physical activity programs to meet the needs of youth with rheumatological conditions is important, with some promise shown in tailored approach to managing other pediatric rheumatological conditions (such as juvenile fibromyalgia) that combine physical activity and other effective methods drawing from injury prevention and cognitive behavioral therapy methods (83). Such approaches may be similarly applied to youth with cSLE in coming research.

As cSLE is an autoimmune condition that involves a complex medication/medical regimen and frequent medical visits, promoting good adherence can be key to optimizing patient outcomes. Furthermore, adolescence (when many are likely to develop cSLE) is a unique transitional period marked by poorer adherence for teens with chronic health conditions. Peers are known to play an increasingly important role in development during this age (84). The impact of inadequate treatment adherence on disease in patients with cSLE has been well-studied, and targeted therapies such as patient support and education are warranted. In the current body of literature, peer support forums and patient education were generally shown to significantly improve adherence in these controlled trials, although one study of a personalized prescription treatment plan did not yield compelling support. Overall, peer and provider supported interventions may play a role in improving adherence and optimizing functioning. Continued study on the impact of programs to promote adherence in this already vulnerable adolescent population, considering other periods of risk for poor adherence (e.g., transition from pediatric to adult rheumatological care), may be warranted.

In a similar vein, nutritional education by providers and the healthcare team was effective in lowering cardiovascular disease risk factors in this population at increased risk for future cardiovascular events. Therefore, provider support and utilization of the health care team to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease may be reasonable. The studies examined here utilized dietary reference intakes, incorporating energy intake and food guides to create individualized dietary plans for their patients (85). Ideally, engaging a nutritionist to help develop and support the individuals needs of patients with cSLE as part of multidisciplinary care may be valuable.

For a variety of reasons, psychotherapy may be beneficial to manage health- and mental health- related concerns that may co-occur with a chronic disease such as cSLE. Indeed, there is a small but growing body of evidence supporting the use of psychotherapy in youth with cSLE. However, the limited literature to date was mixed on whether standardized psychotherapy approaches such as CBT were associated with clinical improvements (e.g., in treatment adherence, depression and fatigue) in individuals with cSLE. The current body of literature focused on traditional in person treatments; however, such approaches can pose barriers for patients, particularly in light of the recent COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, upcoming work should investigate remotely delivered care and indeed such a trial is currently underway. Psychotherapy, and CBT in particular, has been shown to be effective in the adjunctive treatment of mood symptoms and fatigue in many pediatric and adult conditions. Other approaches (e.g., mindfulness, acceptance and commitment therapy) may also be valuable. In addition, blended approaches or testing of specific components of treatment (e.g., cognitive versus behavioral approaches) may be beneficial. Future large scale well controlled studies to further elucidate the effect of CBT and other promising psychotherapies may be valuable.

While existing literature shows some promise, overall, there is an extremely limited overall body of research investigating the effect of non-pharmacological treatments for youth with cSLE. Levels of evidence were generally low (varied from grade 2-3 with no systematic reviews of randomized control trials). Clearly, additional well controlled trials are needed across domains of investigation. It is important to conduct rigorous and replicable research with active controls, if possible, to better understand the effects of such treatments. At present, it is difficult to make overarching conclusions about efficacy of non-pharmacologic therapies in cSLE simply due to the relative lack of evidence in this field and the limitations of the studies involved.

Strengths

Although the pool of investigations was small, the review included 1,152 participants, which is a remarkable number considering the relative lack of literature in this patient population (i.e., prevalence of cSLE is estimated at 3.3-24 per 100,000 children (61)). Therefore, this review has encompassed a large swath of the cSLE population across the globe, including participants from Brazil, the United States of America, and Egypt. Moreover, a variety of promising approaches and targets were investigated. For several of these approaches, the findings from this review suggest benefits to advancing the research forward from phase 1 trials to phase 2 and 3 trials (86).

Limitations

The body of literature is small and lacks rigorous well controlled trials, with variations in race, sex, and disease severity and varying risks of bias. Particularly striking was lack of race reporting by authors in several studies, as well as the female predominance. This may be explained by cultural differences and availability of participants (as only one study excluded males), respectively. This review identified only a limited number of studies that all utilized diverse methods and assessed varied outcomes. Some of these studies were feasibility and accessibility studies preparing for larger trials, which may indicate well controlled trials are on the horizon. In addition, there was variation in race reporting across the studies, which may be due to cultural differences or assumptions based on the location of the study.

Future directions

More rigorous, high evidence-grade trials must be conducted before any overall conclusions can be drawn about the efficacy of non-pharmacologic therapies. Future research should also examine standardized outcome measures across studies.

CONCLUSION

This review is the first to examine the types and targets of non-pharmacologic therapies in children with SLE. Overall, findings suggest non-pharmacologic treatments for cSLE are only beginning to get attention. These include dietary supplementation, aerobic exercise, patient education, and psychotherapy. While these approaches may hold promise for improving a wide range of disease outcomes (e.g., disease activity, fatigue, mood symptoms) when used as an adjunctive to pharmacologic treatment, additional research is clearly needed. Future high quality clinical trials would allow for further understanding of the benefits of these non-pharmacologic therapies for this particularly vulnerable patient population.
