Immigrant-related policies and the health outcomes of Latinx adults in the United States: A systematic review

Abstract

Background:

United States’ federal and subfederal immigrant-related policy activity has increased in recent years. We hypothesize that these policies are structural determinants of health for Latinx communities, operating through access to resources, discriminatory enforcement, and stress.

Methods:

We searched seven databases for quantitative studies, published as of September 2021, examining the association between the presence of federal, state, or local immigrant-related policy(ies), over time or cross-sectionally, and mental or physical health outcomes among immigrant or US-born Latinx adults. We rated studies on methodologic quality.

Results:

Eleven studies were included. Policies included federal and state policies. Health outcomes included mental health (7 studies), self-rated health (n=6), and physical disability (n=1). Among immigrant, noncitizen, or Spanish-preferring Latinx adults, exclusionary policies were associated with poor self-rated health, physical disability, and poor mental health. Inclusive policies were associated with better health, though null findings were more common than among studies of exclusionary policies. Only three studies separately examined policy effects on US-born or citizen Latinx adults and these findings were often null. All studies received a weak overall study quality rating; Among quality domains, studies were strongest in confounding control and weakest in outcome information bias and reporting missing data approaches.

Conclusions:

These results support the hypothesis that immigrant-related policies, especially exclusionary policies, are structural drivers of health for immigrant or noncitizen Latinx adults. However, evidence is scant among US-born or citizen Latinx adults. Studies of policies and physical health outcomes besides disability are lacking, as are results disaggregated by nativity and/or citizenship status.

Introduction

Immigrant-related policies are policies that establish immigrants’ rights, eligibility for resources and services, and protections based on their documentation status.1 These policies can have a substantial effect on immigrants’ entry processes and incorporation into a host society.2 As a structural determinant of health, these policies can affect the health of immigrants and their US-born families through pathways of access to pro-health resources and services, experiences of policing and immigration law enforcement, including deportation, discriminatory enforcement of policies, and subsequent stress.3–5 In the United States, federal and subfederal immigrant-related policy activity has increased over the past 30 years, with a widespread uptick in subfederal level legislation after 2005 and again in 2017 and an increase in federal policies and executive orders between 2017 and 2020.6–9 High-profile policies passed during this time have included the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act (PRWORA) in 1996, Section 287(g) of the US Immigration and Nationality Act (287(g)) in 1996, with agreements signed from 2002 to present, Arizona Senate Bill 1070 (AZ SB 1070) in 2011, and the Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals program (DACA) in 2012. Some policies, like DACA, have been inclusive (i.e., expanded immigrants’ access to public resources and services and facilitated immigrants’ incorporation into US society), but many others, like AZ SB 1070, have been exclusionary (i.e., limited access to or deterred use of resources and limited immigrants’ incorporation into US society).6,10 From 2005 to 2018, subfederal exclusionary policies outweighed inclusive policies in quantity or severity.7,11 Federal policy climate also became more exclusionary of immigrants, especially under the Trump Administration.9

Latinx communities are most likely to be affected by immigrant-related policies, in part because they make up the largest proportion of the immigrant population in the US, and in part because of the racialized nature of immigration policy and enforcement in the US. In 2018, Latinx immigrants comprised 44% of the 44.8 million foreign-born persons in the US and immigrant and US-born Latinx persons comprised 18% of the US population.12–14 Additionally, because of the conflation of Latinx, immigrant, and undocumented immigrant, Latinx persons of any nativity, documentation, or citizenship status may be racially or ethnically profiled during the enforcement of policies because they are suspected of being undocumented.15 They may also be subject to other structural, institutional, or interpersonal discrimination supported or reinforced by these policies.16,17 Thus, while immigrant-related policies cannot explicitly target immigrants from any one world region, they are likely to have a disproportionate effect on Latinx immigrants. Additionally, while immigrants are the target of these policies, some studies have documented spillover effects, whereby immigrant-related policies also affect US-born and other US citizen Latinx populations. Spillover has been documented for US citizens’ decreased enrollment in and access to services and resources,18–22 experiences of being “othered”, and experiences of worry because of policies and their racialized enforcement. 16,23.

Recent reviews and other studies have provided a rich understanding of the mechanisms by which immigrant-related policies, especially exclusionary policies, can affect Latinx immigrants’ health3,4 and may affect Latinx US-born adults’ health. For example, exclusionary policies have resulted in decreased enrollment in public insurance and safety-net programs and increased uninsured rates and wage reductions among immigrant and US-born Latinx individuals,20,21,24–27 all of which have implications for worse mental and physical health.21,28 Additionally, exclusionary policies can be acute and chronic stressors for Latinx individuals, in part because these policies can expose them to heighted immigration enforcement, policing, and racial and ethnic discrimination.3,4 For example, exclusionary state and local policies have been linked to worse mental health for Latinx families and higher perceived discrimination among immigrant and US-born Latinx persons;17,28 Other studies have shown an inverse association between discrimination and mental and physical health.29–31 Additionally, experiences with or worry about deportation have been associated with poorer health and health behaviors among Latinx adults and children.32–35

The research on immigrant-related policies and these various pathways to health is rich, but studies allowing for inference on the total causal effect of immigrant-related policies on health is limited. Some studies of immigrant-related policies have used intermediate variables as the primary outcome (e.g., health care access instead of health), thereby potentially underestimating the total effects of these policies on health outcomes. Other studies use the intermediate variables as the primary exposure (e.g., deportation rather than the Secure Communities program), which informs our understanding of specific mechanisms, but also incorporates other specific dimensions (e.g., experiencing a raid, experiencing deportation) into the well-defined policy definition. A recent review of immigrant policies and the health of infants, children, and adolescents in immigrant families identified just 17 studies that explicitly examined the policy–health outcome association,36 suggesting that there may be few studies that have examined the total policy–health association among adults.

Federal- and subfederal-level immigrant-related policies continue to be passed each year in the US and the study of their effects on health is an active area of research. To keep abreast with this research, there is a need for an updated review of this literature, with a specific focus on studies of policies and health outcomes in Latinx adults, and critical appraisal of US-focused studies to identify methodologic gaps and to advance future quantitative research examining policy–health associations. Thus, the purpose of this systematic review was to identify, synthesize, and critically analyze published quantitative studies examining US federal and subfederal immigrant-related policies and their association with physical and mental health outcomes among immigrant and US-born Latinx adults. We build on previous reviews by narrowing the definition of health to only include physical and mental health outcomes and by conducting a bias analysis to identify potential sources of bias that can be addressed in future studies. Further, we stratify findings by immigrant and non-immigrant or citizen status to identify evidence of spillover effects of these policies onto US-born/citizen Latinx adults.

Methods

Search strategy

We conducted a literature search of peer-reviewed, English-language, quantitative studies published from 1995 and onward that examined associations between immigrant-related policies in the US and the mental and physical health outcomes of Latinx adults. We conducted the search in Pubmed, Social Sciences Citation Index (via Web of Science), and Ebscohost (Social Science Full Text, Race Relations Abstracts, Chicano Database, EconLit with Full Text, and SocIndex) in 2018, 2019, 2020, with a final update in September 2021. The search strategy included terms related to policy, Latinx ethnicity, immigration, and mental and physical health, with an emphasis on outcomes that might be influenced by stress exposures (see eAppendix 1 in the Supplemental Digital Content). We supplemented the search with a manual review of the bibliographies of relevant review articles identified in the search process and the bibliographies of articles selected for inclusion in the review.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they (1) reported on a federal, state, or local immigrant-related policy or set of policies in the US, (2) examined a mental or physical health outcome, and (3) included immigrant or US-born Latinx adults (18 years and older) in the study population. Studies were excluded if they did not explicitly mention a policy or policy period as the exposure (e.g., a study examining the health effect of an immigration raid that did not explicitly tie the raid to a policy; studies only examining differences by nativity status without linkage to a policy) to maintain, as best as possible, a well-defined policy exposure. Studies were also excluded if outcomes were limited to access to care, care utilization, or insurance status because they are intermediary variables on the way to and distinct from physical and mental health outcomes. The initial search strategy included smoking to reflect its status as a risk factor for several chronic diseases, but studies of smoking and other health behaviors were ultimately excluded because they are not physical or mental health outcomes. Studies examining birth outcomes were excluded in this review because we categorized these as effects on non-adult populations. Last, grey literature, including dissertations, were excluded.

Study selection, data extraction and bias analysis

We conducted this systematic review in accordance with The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).37 Two independent reviewers (DMC, KKS) conducted title, abstract, and full-text reviews and conflicts were resolved between the two reviewers at each stage. The initial round of reviews was based on title and abstract and the second round of reviews was based on full text. The two reviewers also independently conducted data extraction and bias analysis. Bias analysis of the studies was completed using a modified version of the Effective Public Health Practice Project’s Quality Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies.38 We included some items from Cochrane’s Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies – of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool39 in the modified tool (see eAppendix 2 in the Supplemental Digital Content). Variables for data extraction and bias analysis were identified a priori and then were modified based on feedback from both reviewers. We did not complete a meta-analysis because only two pairs of studies examined the same policies and outcomes.

Results

The literature search identified 1,766 unique articles, of which 10 studies met inclusion criteria. One additional eligible study was identified from the bibliographies of the included studies or from content-related review articles identified in the search. Thus, a total of 11 studies were included in this review. The most common reasons for exclusion were that studies only included youth or birth outcomes, did not explicitly name policies or policy indices, or did not have mental or physical health outcomes. A PRISMA flow diagram of selection for inclusion is displayed in Figure 1.

Study characteristics

Study characteristics are provided in Table 1 and more detailed study information is provided in eTable 1. All studies (n = 11) had a publication year of 2005 or later, with the majority (n = 9) published after 2015. Approximately half of studies (n = 5) employed a cross-sectional study design. Just over half of studies (n = 7) were large, with sample sizes greater than 10,000 participants.

Policies

In total, 17 policy exposures were examined across all studies. We identified three categories of policy exposures: federal immigration policy eras, state-level policy indices, and individual policies at either the state or federal level. eTable 2 provides a complete list and definition of each of the policies identified in the studies. Two studies examined federal immigration policy eras, which consisted of historical periods defined by the enactment or enforcement of immigration policies (e.g., The Bracero Program which encouraged and authorized Mexican immigrants to work in the US to fill labor shortages between 1942 and 1964).40,41 Two studies developed an index of state-level immigrant-related policies; one index consisted of a count of exclusionary immigrant-related polices42 and the other was based on a set of inclusive or exclusionary policies that were identified though factor analysis.43 Seven studies examined individual federal or state level policies.44–50 No studies included policies enacted at a sub-state level, but one study examined federal policies (i.e., 287(g) agreements and the Secure Communities Program) that either had differential adoption at the local jurisdiction level or were gradually rolled out at the county level.49

Policy exposures differed in their immigrant inclusive and exclusionary status. Two studies examined only inclusive policies,45,46 four studies examined only exclusionary policies,42,44,49,50 and the remaining studies compared inclusive policies to exclusionary policies.41,43,47,48

Policies and Health

Seven studies examined physical health outcomes:41,42,44,46–49 six studies of self-rated health and one study of physical disability. No studies of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or other chronic physical health conditions met eligibility criteria. Seven studies examined mental health outcomes,40,43,45–47,49,50 including self-reported measures of depressive symptoms, psychological distress, and poor mental health days.

There were only two pairs of studies that examined the same policy exposures and health outcomes: two studies on in-state resident tuition policies and self-rated health and two studies on Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) and psychological distress. As such, we summarized findings by health outcome categories of self-rated health, physical health, and mental health, and by policy inclusive/exclusionary status. Additionally, we disaggregated findings by nativity, citizenship, or language-preference status to examine policy effects among at-risk groups. A summary of findings is provided in Table 2 and detailed study findings are included in eTable 1.

Self-rated health

Exclusionary policies were generally associated with worse self-rated health among immigrant, noncitizen, or Spanish-preferring Latinx adults, but no association was observed among US-born, US citizen or English-preferring Latinx adults. Across four studies, five of the six exclusionary policies were associated with at least one measure of worse self-rated health among immigrant, noncitizen, or Spanish-preferring Latinx adults.44,47–49 For example, 287(g) task enforcement agreements and the Secure Communities Program were associated with a higher prevalence of fair or poor self-rated health and 287(g) jail enforcement agreements were associated with a lower prevalence of excellent or very good health among immigrants with noncitizen household members.49 In contrast, the three studies that examined policy associations among US-born, citizen, or English-preferring Latinx adults did not find associations with self-rated health.44,47,48 Lastly, though the authors did not disaggregate findings by nativity or citizenship status, more exclusionary state policy indices were associated with worse self-rated health for all Latinx adults.42

Findings for inclusive policies were less consistent. In the two studies of in-state reduced tuition expansion policies, for example, both studies found that reduced tuition-only policies were associated with better self-rated health among Mexican noncitizens47,48 and one of these studies found a similar positive association for in-state reduced tuition with financial aid policies.47 In contrast, the other study found that in-state reduced tuition with financial aid was associated with worse self-rated health among Mexican noncitizens.48 In the only study of DACA and self-rated health, no evidence of an association was observed among noncitizen Latinx adults.46 Only the two studies of in-state reduced tuition expansion policies included citizen and US-born Latinx adults. In one study, reduced-tuition-only policies were associated with an increased prevalence of fair or poor health,47 but there was no observed association between this policy and self-rated health in the other study48 and neither study observed an association between reduced tuition with financial aid policies and self-rated health.

Physical disability

In the sole study (to our knowledge) examining physical disability, greater disability accumulation was observed among Mexican-origin immigrants who had exposure to the exclusionary Post-Immigration Reform and Control Act Era (1986-2014).41 Although lower physical disability accumulation was observed among the immigrant-inclusive Limiting Era (1965-1985), no associations were observed with other inclusive eras such as the Railroad Era (1910-1941) or the Bracero Program Era. No US-born Latinx adults were included in this study.

Mental health

Exclusionary policies were also generally associated with worse mental health among immigrant or noncitizen Latinx adults. Across three studies, five of the six policy exposures (e.g., 287(g) task enforcement agreements, Variable Deportation immigration era) were associated with worse mental health, including greater psychological distress47,49 and greater depressive symptoms.40 In the only study that differentiated findings for a US-born Latinx sample, findings were mixed, with in-state reduced tuition bans having an association with increased psychological distress scores, but also a decrease in severe distress.47 Last, among the two studies that could not make further distinctions by nativity or citizenship status, exclusionary state policies and Presidential executive orders were associated with a greater number of poor mental health days for Latinx adults.43,50

Among the four studies of inclusive policies, findings differed by policy type among immigrant or noncitizen Latinx adults. Both studies of DACA found that DACA recipients had lower odds of psychological distress, worry, or stress compared with DACA ineligible participants.45,46 Arrival during the immigrant-inclusive post-Mexican Revolution era was also associated with lower depressive symptoms.40 No association was observed between either in-state reduced tuition expansion policies (with or without financial aid) and psychological distress scores.47 This in-state reduced tuition policy-focused study was also the only study that included US-born Latinx adults; similar null associations between policies expanding reduced tuition and psychological distress were observed, except for reduced tuition with financial aid policies, which were associated with a decrease in severe distress.

Risk of bias analysis

Threats to causal inference were assessed over five domains of potential bias (study design; selection processes, loss-to-follow-up, and missing data; confounding; information bias; and intervention integrity). All studies were assessed to have low global study quality (two or more weak ratings); four studies had weak ratings in at least four of the five bias domains.40,41,46,50 Study quality ratings are displayed in Table 3.

Studies performed the best in confounding control, with most studies (n = 10) receiving moderate or strong ratings, either because of control through study design (e.g., difference-in-difference) or through adjustment for confounders in statistical models. For the purposes of this study, we classified confounders as potential common causes of the exposure and outcomes. Confounders adjusted for in models included state- or regional-level factors, such as state-fixed effects, other immigrant-related policies in a state or region, and population-level measures of socioeconomic status.

The studies we examined performed worst in information bias compared with other domains. Among studies with a weak total information bias rating (n = 10), all received a weak rating for outcome information bias because these studies relied on self-reported measures of health and thus participants could have been aware of their policy exposure when reporting their health. In contrast, only three studies had a weak exposure information bias rating, with one study having a loosely-defined exposure of the range of executive orders issued in the early part of the Trump administration50 and two studies using policy eras with overlapping definitions of the policy periods, thus introducing questions about the validity and reliability of these exposure definitions.40,41 The second most common area of weakness was in selection processes, loss-to-follow-up, and missing data bias. Of the nine studies with a weak rating in this area, half of studies (n = 5) lacked information about missingness in outcome data and many lacked information about how missing data was addressed (n = 6) or if findings were robust to the presence of missing data (n =8). Across all 11 studies, most (n = 10) did not report if there was differential missingness of outcome by exposure status. In contrast, only two studies received a weak rating for selection bias. Many studies received a moderate rating, in part, because they had samples that were somewhat or very likely to be representative of the source population (n = 8), but studies often lacked information about participation rates or differences in pre-exposure characteristics between the comparison groups.

The third most common area of weakness was in intervention integrity. Seven studies were given weak ratings because participants could have received an alternative policy exposure that might have influenced the results and these alternative policies were not accounted for in the analysis. Last, approximately half of studies (n = 6) received a weak rating for study design because they utilized a cross-sectional study design or a cohort-design with no pre-post measure of outcomes. Studies with moderate ratings (n = 5) received them because they employed a difference-in-difference.

Discussion

In this systematic review, we aimed to identify, synthesize, and critically analyze quantitative studies examining associations between immigrant-related government policies in the US and physical and mental health outcomes among Latinx adults, making distinctions between immigrant and US-born adults when possible. Our review builds on recent reviews2–4,51 by focusing exclusively on quantitative studies of Latinx adults living in the US, disaggregating findings by nativity and/or citizenship status to examine spillover effects, and critically analyzing sources of bias to identify areas for improvement in future work. In general, for Latinx immigrants or noncitizens, exclusionary policies were associated with worse self-rated and mental health, but inclusive policies were less consistently associated with better health. Just three studies included or disaggregated results for US-born or US-citizen Latinx adults. Often, the policy–health associations for these Latinx adults were null.

Our findings for immigrant or noncitizen Latinx adults are consistent with findings from other reviews that have examined the relationship between immigrant-related policies and immigrants’ health and health care or resource access in a variety of contexts.2,36,51 In Juárez and colleagues’2 review of policies in high income countries, exclusionary policies were associated with poor mental health, poor self-rated health, and mortality among immigrants of different races or ethnicities. In Martinez and colleagues’51 review of studies of undocumented adults and children, exclusionary policies were associated with depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Additionally, our study builds on these reviews by separately examining effects for US-born or US-citizen Latinx adults.

Prior evidence suggests there is spillover of policies on the US-born or naturalized citizens for insurance enrollment,20,21 safety net program enrollment,22 and stress and worry related to immigration enforcement,16,23 but examination of spillover has not always extended to studies of physical health. Examining this spillover on health outcomes was challenging because many studies were limited to immigrant or noncitizen Latinx populations or could not disaggregate Latinx populations by nativity or citizenship status. With only three studies to draw from, we were limited in our ability to draw conclusions about the spillover effects of a range of exclusionary or inclusive policies on the health of US-born or US-citizen Latinx adults. In these studies, policy associations with self-rated health or mental health were generally null or, in some cases, were in the opposite direction of what was expected.44,47 These findings suggest that nativity, citizenship, or documentation status may mitigate spillover effects onto US-born or US-citizen Latinx adults health, but more research is needed.

Our review demonstrates that, to date, quantitative studies of immigrant-related policies among Latinx adults in the US are limited to measures of self-rated health and mental health. Aside from physical disabilities, no other physical health outcomes were examined, despite the potential for physical embodiment.4,52 Infant birth outcomes, which have been previously reviewed,36 are the other predominant outcome examined in the immigrant-related policy literature. This limited scope of outcomes may be the result of existing data limitations (e.g., general ever vs. never assessment of physical health outcomes in some surveys, reliance on self-reported physician-diagnosed disease for an at-risk population who may not have physician access, lack of sufficient geographic variability in non-pooled cohort studies). Additionally, publication bias may favor the non-publication of studies of physical health outcomes with null findings.

We also conducted a risk of bias analysis to evaluate threats to causal inference in existing studies to inform and improve future studies. Many studies were strongest in areas confounding control, selection into the study, and exposure measurement. Even with moderate ratings in the selection into study areas, it is important to note that undocumented immigrants were likely to be underrepresented in many studies, especially those using national survey data, and thus results are likely to underestimate the policy–health association. Despite other study strengths, outcome information bias, loss-to-follow-up and missing data bias, and intervention integrity were the most prevalent sources of weakness. Weakness in information bias stemmed from the predominant reliance on self-reported measures of health. The dominance of self-rated mental health and self-rated health in studies of policies is undoubtedly driven by proposed stress or racial discrimination mechanisms and may also be driven by data availability in national datasets. More than half (n = 7) of studies utilized data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System or the National Health Interview Survey, in which these self-reported outcomes are readily available from year to year. The non-report of missing data or missing data strategies was also prevalent. Because missing data can introduce bias to effect estimation,53 researchers should, at minimum, examine and report the amount of missing data in the sample and explicitly state the missing data analytic approach to improve transparency of methods for replication and assessment of missing data influence by readership. Finally, threats to “intervention” integrity arose because participants could have received an alternative policy exposure, but these policies were not accounted for in the analytic approach. For example, few studies included other immigrant-related policies in their analytic models or studies using difference-in-difference designs did not report whether necessary common shocks assumptions were met for other immigrant-related policies.

Research Recommendations

Based on our study synthesis and bias analysis, we offer the following methodologic recommendations for future research on immigrant-related policies and health.

  1. Include US-born Latinx persons and disaggregate by nativity, citizenship or documentation status when possible. Despite existing evidence of spillover effects of immigrant-related policies on US-born or US-citizen Latinx adults for public insurance or safety net program enrollment or stress,16,20,21 only three studies in this review disaggregated findings by nativity status, citizenship status, or language-preference. Policy–health associations for the US-born or US-citizen Latinx adults were often, though not always, null. Future studies should include US-born Latinx adults and test for interaction with or stratify by nativity, citizenship, or documentation status to document for whom and to what degree these policies influence health.

  2. Leverage innovative strategies to reach vulnerable immigrant populations. Studies which relied on data from national surveys (e.g., NHIS) may be less likely to include undocumented immigrants and thus are more likely to underestimate policy–health associations. Community-based participatory research or community-engaged research strategies have successfully been conducted in immigrant communities54–56 and have served to build trust, increase participation, and meet communities’ needs. Collaborations between community-based projects across geographic areas can be leveraged to improve the inclusion of undocumented individuals in policy–health studies.

  3. Examine different physical health outcomes. Physical health outcomes are largely absent from the immigrant policy published literature. If concerns about outcome measurement are a barrier, future studies could explore the use of measured health outcomes in some electronic health record databases, the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, or pooled cohort studies with anthropometric measures. Additionally, collaborations between community-engaged projects may also present an opportunity to use other data sources to explore additional physical health outcomes.

  4. Account for multiple policy effects. Because of the complex, multi-level, ever-changing nature of the immigration system in the US, immigrant-related policies co-exist with or are enacted during similar periods as other immigrant-related policies across jurisdiction levels. Half of the studies did not account for this complexity. Researchers need to identify other co-occurring immigrant-related policies and incorporate them into the analytic approach. This can include a transparent discussion in difference-in-difference studies about whether the common shocks assumption was met or needed to be relaxed, controlling for other policies in analyses, using summative policy indices43 to capture multiple policies in one exposure, or using other approaches to address policy clustering.57

Strengths and Limitations

The search terms in our systematic review are limited in three ways. First, our specific physical health outcome search terms emphasized health conditions that are sensitive to stress and access to resources (e.g., care, employment) that are influenced by policies, but these terms were not exhaustive. For example, we did not include terms specific to cardiovascular disease events. In a sensitivity search, we included terms of heart attack, myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke arrhythmia, heart disease, angina, and chest pain, and this search yielded the same number of studies over the total publication period as our original query, confirming that we did not miss studies of cardiovascular disease events. Even with the inclusion of general terms such as “health” and “mental health”, we may have missed some studies. Second, we included search terms related to policy or laws, but did not include terms for specific policies and may have missed studies that did not use our policy-related keywords. For example, some manuscripts where DACA is referred to as a program, not a policy, may not have been captured in our search. We searched the bibliographies of included studies and relevant past reviews, which helped to identify any additional studies, but very recent studies were still at risk of non-identification. Third, because the purpose of our review was to examine studies among Latinx adults, we included the terms Latino or Hispanic to improve the specificity of the search. Studies which did not include these terms in the databases searchable fields (e.g., keywords, title, abstract) would not have been captured in the searches and, as such, may not have been identified.

This systematic review is strengthened by a narrow scope that focused explicitly on US immigrant-related policies, immigrant and US-born Latinx adults, and their physical and mental health outcomes. By taking this approach, we examined a total causal effect of immigrant-related policies on health, rather than mechanism-specific (e.g., access to care, stress, access to employment) effects. Second, the stratification of findings by nativity/citizenship status allowed us to try to examine potential spillover effects of policies onto US-born/citizen Latinx persons. Last, this study is strengthened by a formal risk of bias analysis, which was only reported in one other review,2 to identify areas for methodologic improvement of future research on immigrant-related policies.

Conclusions

Our findings support the hypothesis that immigrant-related policies are structural drivers of health for immigrant or noncitizen Latinx adults. Generally, exclusionary policies were associated with worse self-rated health, mental health, and physical disability among immigrant or noncitizen Latinx adults compared with their health before the enactment of policies or in comparison to populations thought to be unaffected or minimally affected by the policies. Inclusive policies were associated with better health, though findings differed by the specific policies. However, the quantitative study of US-based immigrant-related policies and mental and physical health is a relatively new area of research, as evidenced by our identification of only 11 eligible studies, most of which were published after 2015. With the ongoing passage of immigrant-related policies at the federal and subfederal levels, additional research on mental and physical health outcomes can expand our understanding of the pervasive effects of these policies. As we highlight above, future research can be improved by addressing traditional threats to causal inference, disaggregating Latinx populations by nativity or citizenship status, adopting innovative strategies to reach vulnerable immigrant populations, examining other physical health outcomes, and accounting for multiple policy effects in analyses.
