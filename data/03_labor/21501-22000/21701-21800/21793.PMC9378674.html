Abstract

Globally, obesity persists at epidemic rates. Men are underrepresented within behavior-based obesity prevention research. As men prefer individualized, self-guided interventions, electronic delivery of treatment modalities have potential to reach this population. The purpose of this study was to systematically review primary, secondary, or tertiary behavioral obesity prevention interventions that used controlled designs; targeted men; and incorporated at least one electronically delivered treatment modality explicitly designed to elicit an intervention effect. Literature searches were delimited to peer-reviewed articles; published between 2000 and 2021; in the English language; indexed in PsycINFO, CINHAL, MEDLINE, CENTRAL, and WOS electronic databases. Interventions satisfying inclusion criteria were critiqued for methodological quality using the Jadad Scale (0=lowest quality; 10=highest quality). Eleven studies satisfied the inclusion criteria (n=1,748; total participants) with five reporting group-by-time intervention effects on the primary variable targeted. Jadad scale quality assessment scores ranged from 5.00 to 9.00 with a mean of 7.72. Majority of the interventions applied a randomized control trial design (n=10). Most interventions were theory-based, with eight rooted in social cognitive theory. Behavior change strategies included self-monitoring (n=10), personalized feedback (n=8), health counseling (n=8), and goal setting (n=9). Community-level theories have the potential to guide future obesity prevention interventions targeting men.

Introduction

The epidemic prevalence of adult obesity is a significant public health crisis due to its association with ischaemic heart disease, stroke, and diabetes; three of the world’s leading causes of death.1 Globally, 1.9 billion of the world’s adult population are overweight (body mass index [BMI] between 25 and 29.9 kg/m2); 650 million of which classify as obese (BMI ≥ 30).2 Obesity rates tend to be greater in middle-and-high-income countries.3 In the United States, Australia, and the United Kingdom, 40.5, 27.5, 23% of women and 35.2, 27.5, 20% of men are classified as obese, respectively.4 Given its widespread prevalence, scalable interventions are needed to prevent and control obesity outcomes and obesity-associated risk factors.

Current treatments for obesity include behavioral, environmental, pharmacological, and surgical.5 Even with pharmacological and surgical interventions, some lifestyle modification is required.5 Subsequently, behavioral interventions will remain relevant to successful prevention of obesity. Although men and women are both impacted by obesity, less is known about the efficacy of behavioral weight-loss interventions for men with obesity, in part because men are underrepresented within this body of research literature.6 A review of international studies5 found that men comprised only 27% of enrollees across 244 lifestyle weight loss randomized controlled trials. Behavioral interventions in the United States (n=140) had a significantly smaller proportion of males than international trials (21% vs. 34%).5 Research examining this paradox implies differences in delivery preferences between men and women. Qualitative studies have found men prefer individualized, self-guided interventions; whereas women prefer face-to-face, group-based interventions.5,7,8

Low perceived importance of obesity prevention is a salient barrier to recruiting men for behavioral interventions.9 Culturally, men may feel less social pressure than women to engage in weight management for body image purposes;10 although, men do feel obesity impedes their career success.11 For those men recruited into obesity prevention interventions, program adherence is typically low.12 Further still, those men that are successful in losing weight, maintenance of initial weight loss is difficult to sustain beyond one year.13 Electronic delivery of obesity prevention intervention modalities that provide features important to men may overcome these barriers. In this context, electronic modalities can be conceptualized as intervention materials received by participants through an electronic medium, where said materials are explicitly intended to elicit a treatment effect. Given their potential reach capacity, electronically delivered treatment modalities may be more feasible for reaching men relative to traditional, in-person interventions.14 Furthermore, electronically delivered treatment modalities may lend themselves more to a self-paced nature, which men prefer.15 With this backdrop, the goal of this review was to systematically evaluate the efficacy of electronically delivered behavioral obesity prevention interventions targeting men.

Methods

Reporting of search and screening processes adhered to PRISMA16 criteria. The protocol for this systematic review was registered with PROSPERO17 international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42017068493); two modifications were made to the initially registered protocol: (1) duration of the systematic search was expanded to the time of submission of the manuscript; (2) study designs were expanded from randomized controlled trials only to controlled trials only. Intuitional Review Board (IRB) approval of this study was not required as no human subjects were enrolled in this study. Inclusion criteria for this review were: (1) (i) primary prevention behavioral obesity interventions that targeted behaviors designed to prevent obesity (e.g., physical activity, diet), regardless of current body weight classification; (ii) secondary prevention behavioral obesity interventions (overweight body mass index, 25.0—29.9); (iii) tertiary prevention behavioral obesity interventions (obese body mass index, 30.0+); (2) controlled study designs that included either no-treatment, wait-list, active, or standard control group(s); (3) explicitly analyzed data for non-clinical, male participants 18 years of age and older (e.g., no combined gender groups or male vs. female comparison groups); (4) implemented at least one electronically delivered treatment modality. For the purposes of this study, electronic modalities were operationally defined as intervention materials received by participants through an electronic medium that were explicitly intended to elicit a treatment effect. For example, if instructions for a walking program were delivered to participants via an online website, the walking program would be considered an electronically delivered modality. However, if a pedometer was provided to participants to record steps from the walking program, the pedometer would not be considered an electronic modality as it was not intended to elicit an intervention effect. Instead, the pedometer in this example would be categorized as a tool for assessment.

Literature searches were delimited to peer-reviewed articles; published between January 2000 and November 2021; in the English language; indexed in APA PsycINFO, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) Plus, the Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE), the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Web of Science (WOS) electronic databases.18 The following text string with Boolean logic was applied to conduct the systematic search in the selected databases: ((overweight OR obes* OR adipos*)) AND ((male* OR man OR men)) AND ((random* OR clinic* OR trial OR intervention OR evaluation OR experiment OR program* OR pilot OR feasibility OR treatment)) AND ((weight loss OR weight maint* OR weight reduc* OR weight management OR weight control OR obesity treat* OR obesity prev* OR overweight treat* OR overweight prev*)) AND TOPIC: ((electronic OR web OR internet OR online OR non-face-to-face)) NOT ((women OR female*)) NOT ((rats OR mice)) NOT ((child*)).

Results from each database were downloaded into Microsoft Excel© spreadsheets and evaluated for adherence to the inclusion criteria. Endnote© bibliographical software was used to organize studies through the data extraction procedure. The Microsoft Excel© spreadsheets are available to the public per e-mail request. Each phase of data extraction was assessed by two independent reviewers. Cohen’s Kappa (κ) values were calculated for each phase of extraction (identification, screening, and eligibility) to evaluate level of agreement. Cohen’s κ coefficient values greater than 0.9019 were considered an acceptable level of agreement. For articles where disagreement of inclusion occurred, the two reviewers discussed the articles. A third reviewer provided a tie-breaking vote if a consensus on inclusion or exclusion could not be reached. Really Simple Syndication (RSS) alert notifications were set to ensure new studies added to each database were considered following preliminary data extraction.

Studies that passed through all distillation phases were critiqued for methodological quality using a modified version of the Jadad scale by two study authors.20–22 The Jadad Scale evaluated the rigor of each trial quality by assessing randomization, blinding, withdrawals, exclusion criteria, intervention used, control used, and data reporting factors. The intervention characteristics are operationalized into three constructs: namely, internal validity, reporting, and intervention. Due to the inherent difficulty of double blinding for behavioral interventions, the Jadad scale was slightly modified. Item 3 of the scale was modified from “was the study described as double blind?” to “was the study described as double blinded to participants at baseline and blinded to accessors at all time points?” In addition, given the benefits of no-treatment cohorts for controlling self-selection and novelty effect biases, item 5 was modified from “did the study have a control group?” to “did the study have a no-treatment control group?” Summation of these factors resulted in a global score ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater methodological rigor (see Table 2).

Data related to intervention features including (1) study name; (2) study location; (3) target population; (4) primary study purpose; (5) study design; (6) intervention modeling methodology; (7) intervention duration; (8) intervention assignments; (9) theoretical framework; (10) evaluation measures; (11) funding sources; and (12) intervention results were extracted and summarized in Table 1. As summarized in Table 3, data pertaining to intervention modalities were also extracted. Extracted data were summarized to address whether: (12) there were significant (i) time, (ii) group, (iii) group x time, or (iv) no significant results for the primary outcome variable; (13) the intervention (i) fully, (ii) partially, or (iii) did not operationalize behavior change theory constructs; (14) process evaluation measures were collected; (15) the intervention included an accessible website with intervention materials; (16) the intervention’s electronic platform included a (i) face-to-face tutorial, (ii) electronic tutorial, or (iii) no tutorial; (17) intervention tailoring was applied; (18) phone or video delivery was used; (19) text messaging was used; (20) group interaction (e.g., discussion boards) transpired; (21) physical material(s) (e.g., not 100% electronic delivery) were included; (22) one or more face-to-face intervention session(s) were provided; (23) incentives were used; (24) as well as information about intervention dosage. Means with standard deviations were extracted and summarized in Table 1. Sample sizes were also extracted to calculate intervention attrition rates using Microsoft Excel©. Attrition rates were calculated using the total sample sizes of enrolled participants and are summarized in Table 1.

Results

Search Strategy Results.

The systematic search strategy returned hits from the APA PsycArticles (n = 3), CENTRAL (n = 29), CINAHL Plus with Full Text (n =20), MEDLINE (n = 85), WOS (n = 109), databases for a total of 248 identified peer-reviewed articles (Cohen’s κ=0.92). Initial screening of titles and abstracts removed duplicates (n = 21), studies not related to obesity prevention (n = 95), cross-sectional and modeling obesity-related research (n = 29), review articles (n = 5), as well as formative and/or fidelity research (n = 51) (Cohen’s κ =0.94). Screening of full text of the remaining articles (n = 47) resulted in further identification and removal of formative and/or fidelity research (n = 3), studies that targeted clinical populations (n = 1), as well as studies not related to obesity prevention (n = 6). Interventions that did not exclusively analyze males (n = 25) or did not include at least one electronically delivered intervention modality (n = 2) were also removed (Cohen’s κ=0.98). Next, a descendent search was conducted on the eligible studies (n=10). One additional study was identified through this process and included in the final tally of reviewed articles. In total, 11 studies agreed upon by the researchers were included in the systematic review (Cohen’s κ=0.98). Two of the articles reported 6- and 12-month results23,24 of the same study; therefore, ten unique interventions were identified, enrolling a total of 1,748 participants across all the trials. Nine of the interventions produced significant results23–31 on the primary outcome variable targeted by each intervention (see Table 1 for primary variables for each study); of these, only five25,27,28,30,31 produced group-by-time intervention effects. Four23,24,26,29 reported time, but not group effects. Of those nine trials reporting significant results that calculated percent weight loss, only four23,24,27,30 reported ≥5% weight loss from baseline, the benchmark for clinically significant weight loss.32 Jadad scale quality assessment scores ranged from 5,31 7,24–26,29,30 and 9,23,27,28,33,34 with a mean score of 7.72. Studies were conducted in Australia,23,24,27,28,34 Japan,25 Netherlands,26 South Korea,31 and the United States.29,30,33 Recruitment occurred at universities,23,24 work sites,25–27,31 and communities. 27, 28 Most of the studies used a randomized controlled trial design 23,252624,27–30,33,34 as well as intention-to-treat and per-protocol analysis23–25,27–30,33,34 to model intervention data. Durations of studies ranged from three,23,27 four,25,31 six,30 nine,28 and 1226,33 months.

While all but one study29 rooted their interventions in theory, none operationalized and measured behavior change theoretical constructs. Discussion

Men with, or at risk, of obesity are one of the most challenging populations to recruit for behavioral weight management and treatment programs.7,8 Electronic delivery of behavioral obesity prevention modalities has been suggested as a medium to reach male populations due to this demographic’s preferences for weight loss programs that incorporate autonomy, self-regulation, and individualized feedback.15 The purpose of this investigation was to systematically review electronically delivered behavioral obesity prevention interventions targeting men during the past two decades. Based upon the results of this study, the evidence for the efficacy of this of intervention format is promising. However, further research is required to gauge their full prevention and treatment potential.

The mean Jadad Scale score was 7.72. Examination of the internal validity, reporting, and intervention Jadad Scale construct scores revealed potential for increasing the quality of this line of intervention. Scores for the internal validity construct could be increased by incorporating additional layers of blinding and evaluating trials with no-treatment control groups. The reporting construct could be improved by increasing program adherence and decreasing intervention attrition. All the studies received the maximum intervention construct score of 2; however, using the clinical weight loss endpoint of ≥5% would standardize the therapeutic dose for this intervention category.

Of the interventions, most were conducted in Australia23,24,27,34 (n=4) and the United States of America (n=3).29,30,33 Given the global prevalence of obesity risk in male populations, relative to the total number of interventions implemented, it would be beneficial to develop and test scalable programs in more geographical locations and cultural milieus. As obesity is a global phenomenon, generalizability of treatment methods and modalities can only be determined by testing interventions across male populations imbedded in diverse cultures.7 Even in the context of Westernized societies, there are demographic distinctions in male populations which may make an intervention that appears efficacious in one group, ineffective in another.15

From a design perspective, more interventions are needed that include long-term outcome evaluation of study findings as well as nontreatment control groups. For most of the interventions, the final data collection point occurred at six months or less. Only two studies collected data at 12 months.26,33 Meta-analyses suggest weight loss associated with behavioral interventions begins to rebound at 12 months from baseline.13 Given the reach potential of electronically delivered obesity prevention modalities, strategically administered booster treatments (e.g., emails with personalized feedback) may help sustain intervention outcomes.15 To accurately evaluate the long-term effects of interventions on maintenance of weight loss, outcomes should be measured over multiple years (e.g., 24 months) from baseline.64 All but one study25 used a no-treatment control group. The empirical rigor of long-term evaluation can be enhanced by enlisting no-treatment cohorts to control for self-selection and novelty effect biases.

Social cognitive theory was used by several studies23,24,27,28,30,33–35 to develop intervention treatments. Within the context of social cognitive theory, self-regulation (e.g., self-monitoring of behaviors) and self-efficacy (e.g., individualized feedback to strengthen an individual’s belief in their capacity to execute behaviors) were core constructs for developing intervention behavior change strategies. Actualization of these theoretical constructs occurred through self-monitoring (e.g., online behavioral diaries),23,24,26–31,33,34 customized feedback (e.g., tailored information sheets),23,24,26,27,30,31,33,34 health counseling,23–25,27,29–31,33 and goal setting.23–25,27,28,30,31,33,34 Evidence of the efficacy of these constructs for promoting weight loss in men with obesity is mixed. While formative research suggests these constructs are important to men’s weight loss,59 additional experimental evidence is needed due to instances of (1) time, but not group-by-time effects; (2) null results potentially due to issues with program adherence; (3) inability to calculate construct change scores.

Those studies23,24,26,29 which reported significant time effects, but not group-by-time effects,65 may imply a self-selection bias or a novelty effect as explanations for observed behavior change, as opposed to efficacious treatment effects. Furthermore, those studies that reported low compliance24–26,33 may have elicited type III errors (i.e., correctly rejecting the null hypothesis for the incorrect reason), limiting the ability to assess modality effectiveness. While most of the reviewed studies were rooted in behavioral theory, none fully operationalized and measured the constructs from the theories they reified. Given the problematic nature of program adherence and attrition with this intervention demographic,5,8 it would be advantageous for future studies to operationalize theoretical constructs to calculate construct change scores. Change scores calculated for both theoretical constructs and obesity prevention outcomes could then be linked to uncover causal pathways between behavioral modalities and treatment effects.

Recommendations to enhance electronic behavioral interventions for men with obesity.

Expanding behavior change interventions from the micro- to macro-level is essential to scale behavior change interventions at the population level.14 The diffusion of innovation theory66 describes how a new idea, behavior, or product gains momentum and spreads through a population or system. Individuals within a population can be categorized by their current state of readiness to adopt an innovation.66 Innovators proactively adopt innovations. Early adopters are opinion leaders that embrace change opportunities. Early majority are open to change but require evidence that the innovation is effective before adoption occurs. Late majority individuals are skeptical of change and will only adopt an innovation after it has saturated the market. Laggards are highly skeptical of change and the most difficult group to convince to adopt an innovation.

Macro, community-level interventions diffused into work environments may capitalize on men’s beliefs concerning obesity prevention and improved job performance.9 Men perceive employees with obesity as less productive, more likely to report absent from work, and to reflect poorly on the organization’s image.9 Electronically delivered modalities that illustrate connections between achievement of obesity prevention goals and improvements in workplace performance may increase men’s program adherence. Such trend data is amenable to electronic delivery through graphs, charts, and customizable messages. Achievement of goals could be coupled with workplace incentives such as additional vacation time, reduced health insurance premiums, or financial bonuses. In the reviewed studies, only five programs used incentives.27,28,30,33,34 Worksite programs that connect weight loss with employer-supported incentives may capitalize on men’s motivation to improve work performance by managing obesity risk.

The diffusion of wearable devices and video conferencing software into the general population presents a unique opportunity for interventions applying self-monitoring modalities. Approximately 1 in 5 adults in the United States regularly wear electronic fitness trackers (e.g., Apple Watch, FitBit, Nike+).67 In a study of Australian adults, approximately 40% used some form of digital platform for physical activity.68 Additionally, video conferencing software (e.g., Zoom) and streaming services (e.g., YouTube) have increased since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic and social distancing policies.68 In 2021, 82% of male internet users visited YouTube.69 Video communication can add an element of human interaction and may increase adherence due to greater perception of personalization of intervention delivery.29 Similar to those reviewed studies25,31,36 that matched participants to their current stage of change using the transtheoretical model,26 future studies may match participants to their present stage of innovation adoption66 for video software and wearable devices. Pairing participants in this way may allow for additional modality customization while simultaneously increasing prescription adherence and decreasing rates of attrition. The interventions reviewed for this study targeted diet and physical activity as behavioral mediators of weight loss. In recent years, short sleep duration79 and sedentary behavior80 have emerged as risk factors of adiposity. Short sleep duration can cause dysregulation of the hormones that control hunger, leading to increased caloric intake and subsequent weight gain. Furthermore, sleep disturbances such as sleep apnea may also increase obesity risk. Increased sedentary time is also associated with obesity risk.80 Current recommendations suggest replacing sedentary behavior with physical activity.81 Among the studies reviewed for this investigation, none included treatment modalities to address sleep duration or sedentary behavior. Future weight loss research studies for men should consider intervening on these factors. Wearable devices and video software can assist in self-monitoring these behaviors.82 For example, wearable devices can remind participants when they have been sedentary for more than an hour and prompt them to engage in physical activity. Such devices can also be programmed to remind participants to begin preparations for sleep as their intended bedtime draws near. Furthermore, devices synced to the participants mobile phone can activate a sleep mode which can reduce the brightness level of their mobile devices as well as mute the device sound, aside from the alarm clock, to prevent unintentional sleep disturbances.

Study Limitations.

Heterogeneity between interventions, outcomes, and study designs precluded quantitative meta-analysis of the reviewed studies. However, most of the interventions applied similar treatment modalities, which permitted a narrative approach to synthesize the studies. Reviewed articles were limited to five databases. While these databases are comprehensive, it is possible some studies were missed due to the databases used to execute the search protocol. Studies were also limited to articles published in the English language, which also limited the geographic range of the reviewed studies. Five of the studies23,24,27,33,34 in this review were iterations of the SHED-IT trial, which may have limited this review’s capacity to evaluate the internal validity of this intervention prototype. The Jadad Scale20–22 is a recognized measure of trial quality; however, its brief nature prevents it from capturing all dimensions of intervention rigor.

Conclusion.

Despite their potential, only a small number of electronic-based interventions developed explicitly for males have been scientifically evaluated. Considering the widespread prevalence of men with obesity and obesity-associated risk factors, future research should continue studying electronic delivery of behavioral obesity prevention interventions for their efficaciousness in male populations. The behavior-change modalities amendable to electronic delivery, namely, self-monitoring of health behaviors, personalized prescription of behavior change goals, and behavior change goal-setting autonomy, are viewed positively by males.83 The rapid diffusion of wearable monitoring devices and video software into the general population offer greater potential to expand this mode of intervention delivery. In addition to diet and physical activity,84 future studies should include sleep duration/disturbances and sedentary activity as targeted behavioral outcomes.
