Feasibility, Acceptability, and Efficacy of Mindfulness Training in People With Upper Motor Neuron Disorders: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Objectives:

This systematic review aims to gain a comprehensive understanding of the feasibility, acceptability, and efficacy of mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) on depression, anxiety, fatigue, and health-related quality of life among individuals with upper motor neuron disorders (UMNDs).

Data Sources:

PubMed, PsycINFO, Excerpta Medica Database, and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature were searched for relevant studies published between January 2001 and June 2021.

Study Selection:

Clinical trials published in English evaluating MBIs in adults with the 4 most common UMNDs (multiple sclerosis, brain injury including stroke, spinal cord injury, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis) were included.

Data Extraction:

Two reviewers independently performed the risk of bias assessment using standardized tools and extracted desired data electronically.

Data Synthesis:

A total of 44 studies were included: 26 randomized controlled trials, 10 nonrandomized controlled trials, and 8 pre-post intervention studies. The average ± SD duration of MBIs was 8±2 weeks. On average, 85%±14% of participants completed the MBI, and the retention rate at follow-up was 80%±16%. Only 14% of the studies delivered MBIs virtually, and feasibility metrics were similar to in-person studies. Among studies reporting acceptability data, most participants reported satisfaction with the MBI. Randomized controlled trials that evaluated the effects of MBI on depression, anxiety, fatigue, and quality of life revealed greater relative improvement in these outcomes among MBI participants compared with controls, with differences greater when compared with passive control than active control participants. None of the studies included in this review studied dose response.

Conclusions:

Based on current data, MBIs are feasible and offer a promising approach to address the biopsychosocial needs of individuals with UMNDs. MBIs are associated with a high acceptance rate among participants, with notable improvements in depression, anxiety, fatigue, and quality of life post intervention. Future studies are needed to evaluate alternate models of delivery of MBIs and the dose-response relationship. Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation 2022;103:2410–28

Upper motor neuron disorders (UMNDs) result from damage to upper motor neurons (UMNs), UMN pathways, and connections in the cortex, internal capsule, brainstem, and spinal cord.1,2 UMNDs may occur because of a traumatic event, nontraumatic event, or inherited or sporadic neurodegenerative disease.1,3–8 The most prevalent UMNDs are multiple sclerosis (MS) and brain injury (BI), including stroke, spinal cord injury (SCI), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) combined, affecting approximately over a million people per year in the United States.4,9–16

These 4 common UMNDs result in a significant decline in physical function5,7,8,17,18 and often develop secondary complications that affect their quality of life (QOL), including cognitive impairment,19–21 fatigue,22 chronic pain,23,24 depression,25–27 and anxiety.25–27 The prevalence of depression and anxiety among individuals with UMNDs (23%–46%) is considerably greater than the general population (~12%).27–34 It is well documented that these secondary complications are the product of biological, psychological, and social processes.35–38 For example, biological factors such as direct damage to the neurons after UMNDs result in pain. However, psychosocial factors such as cognition, behaviors, and social support can influence the experience of pain and the ability to adapt to the pain.39

Thus, it is essential to evaluate treatments that address not just biological aspects of UMNDs but also psychological and social processes, with the overall goal of increasing QOL and adaptive functioning in the presence of UMNDs.

Mindfulness meditation refers to cultivating nonjudgmental awareness and acceptance of the present moment.40 Research examining the effect of mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) has grown exponentially over the past decades. MBIs are not only associated with positive changes in psychological processes41 but also with improved biological processes. For example, MBIs have been shown to decrease inflammation42,43 and decrease cortisol levels by altering hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis function,42–45 which is impaired in individuals with UMNDs such as MS, BI, stroke, and SCI.46–50 Finally, MBIs appear to improve social processes, including increased relationship satisfaction and perceived social support,51,52 both of which are affected among those experiencing a UMN disorder.53–57

Recently, several meta-analyses were conducted examining MBIs among 3 different UMNDs (BI, MS, and SCI separately).58–62 A 2021 meta-analysis of meditation and yoga interventions for mild traumatic BI found moderate effects of MBIs on depression, QOL, fatigue, and physical health.58 Similarly, 3 meta-analyses of MBI studies conducted in 2019 and 2020 in individuals with MS revealed a small to moderate effect on mental well-being59; moderate to large effects on well-being, stress, anxiety, and depression60; and small effects on fatigue and pain.61 Additionally, a 2020 systematic review of 5 interventions involving mindfulness components delivered to individuals with SCI reported some support for reduced pain, depression, and anxiety. However, this review included studies with yoga and cognitive behavioral therapy, and only 1 study in this review delivered MBI, making it difficult to determine the efficacy of MBI in this population.62

Although mounting evidence supports the effectiveness of MBIs in improving mental health and QOL among individuals with UMNDs, to date, these interventions are not recommended routinely to people with UMNDs. Participation in MBIs requires both physical and psychological engagement.63 The myriad of complications (eg, mobility limitations, cognitive impairment, pain, fatigue) developed in people with UMNDs might be seen as barriers to participation in MBIs. Therefore, it is essential to understand the feasibility and acceptability of MBIs in this population to aid the clinical translation of these interventions. One recent systematic review of 6 studies of MBIs delivered to individuals with MS aggregated participants’ qualitative feedback and suggested that the MBIs are valued among people with MS and highlights several aspects of delivery that may increase the feasibility and acceptability of MBIs among people with MS.64

Although there are recently published systematic reviews and meta-analyses58–62 evaluating the efficacy of MBIs in people with MS, BI, and SCI, additional review of the feasibility and acceptability of MBIs and participant adherence with MBIs in these populations would add to the existing literature and serve to inform the development and implementation of future MBIs. To our knowledge, this is the largest systematic review of MBIs delivered to people with UMNDs and the first to present detailed feasibility metrics, such as recruitment, retention, and adherence rates, and acceptability data with an objective to gain a comprehensive understanding of the feasibility and acceptability of and adherence to MBIs among people with UMNDs. Additionally, to evaluate the efficacy of MBIs we calculated the relative change in 4 health-related outcomes (depression, anxiety, fatigue, QOL) in the MBI group compared with control conditions. Relative change has not been reported in any systematic review of MBI studies of UMNDs. Therefore, the objective of this study is to present (1) feasibility, (2) adherence, (3) acceptability, and (4) efficacy of health-related outcomes of MBIs in people with UMNDs.

Methods

The systematic review was registered with Open Science Framework-Center for Open Science.

The systematic review followed the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses guidelines.65

Inclusion criteria

Selected studies included clinical trials on MBIs, published in English between January 1, 2001, and June 21, 2021, conducted in adults (aged 18 years or older) with the following UMNDs: MS, BI, SCI, and ALS. The date range was chosen to increase the relevance of the findings to current practice, given that the management of UMNDs and the delivery of MBIs have undergone considerable changes over the past several decades.

Information sources and search

A systematic search was performed using the following databases: PubMed, PsycINFO, Excerpta Medica Database, and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature. Also, we reviewed recently published systematic reviews and meta-analyses to identify additional studies that were not identified in the initial search. The initial search strategy was developed for PubMed and subsequently adapted for the other databases. The following Medical Subject Headings of the National Library of Medicine terms were used: mindfulness, meditation, stroke, traumatic BI, SCI, ALS, primary lateral sclerosis, and MS. We performed a separate search for each UMN disorder category separately to avoid any lost data. Please see appendix 1 for details on the search strategy.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts returned by our search strategy (fig 1) after the removal of duplicates were screened for eligibility by 2 authors. After an initial review of abstracts, the full-text articles of eligible abstracts were retrieved and assessed by these authors for final inclusion. Our main goal was to study the feasibility and acceptability of MBIs. Therefore, we excluded abstracts with no full texts and studies with interventions in which mindfulness was only a small part of the main intervention. We excluded non–peer-reviewed literature and duplicate publications of the same study in which authors often presented secondary outcomes in a separate article.

Data collection process

A data extraction form was used to extract the desired information. Data extraction was performed by 2 authors independently. Data elements included study population, study design, sample size, number of eligible people who consented, retention rate at study completion and follow-up, adherence with the intervention (session attendance and home practice completion), type of intervention, control condition, study duration, health-related outcome measures, and acceptability/satisfaction measures. We audited any missing data and outliers after data collection. If a systematic error was identified, we reviewed all data affected by the systematic error. Any discrepancies during this process were settled by consultation between the 2 authors after referring to the original article. After data extraction, the information was reviewed by all authors.

Measures

For feasibility, we calculated recruitment and retention rates. The recruitment rate was defined as the total number of identified or screened eligible people who provided consent for the study. The retention rate was calculated as the total number of enrolled participants who completed the study and individual follow-up assessments. We calculated the retention rate for study completion, that is, retention at the completion of the intervention and immediate postintervention assessment, and for each follow-up period separately. In addition to individual results from each study, we calculated mean and SD if data were normally distributed or median and interquartile range (IQR) if data were not normally distributed. This was used to report the distribution of sample size, recruitment rate, intervention completion rate, and retention rate at follow-up. For adherence to the intervention, we reported session attendance (ie, mean attendance at weekly sessions, percentage who attended the majority of weekly sessions, or percentage who completed all sessions) and adherence to recommended home practice (ie, percent of assigned home practice sessions completed), if the data were available. To assess the acceptability of MBIs to the participants, mean quantitative acceptability or satisfaction score was calculated, and qualitative data were summarized to assess participants’ acceptability or satisfaction with MBIs, if available.

We reported the results of all health-related outcomes if results were provided. We used “+S” in study tables to suggest that the MBI resulted in an improvement or no change in that particular measure post intervention, and results achieved statistical significance compared with controls in controlled studies or compared with baseline in pre-post studies. Similarly, we used “0” if no statistically significant change was seen in the MBI group compared with control in controlled studies or compared with baseline in pre-post studies. Because of recognized limitations for using meta-analyses when combining data from various patient-reported outcome tools,66 we calculated the relative change in outcome measures to assess the efficacy of MBIs. This was defined as the change in an outcome score from baseline in the treatment/intervention group minus the change in an outcome score from baseline in the control group, divided by the baseline score in the treatment/intervention group. This relative difference or change provides an estimate of percentage improvement or percentage worsening in an outcome score of the treatment group relative to the control group. We used the relative difference/change to estimate the direction and approximate magnitude of effect for depression, anxiety, fatigue, and quality of life. This method was recently reported in a systematic review on MBIs.41

Risk of bias in individual studies

To assess the quality of studies and risk of bias, we appraised studies using the risk of bias in nonrandomized studies of intervention (ROBIN)-II for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and ROBIN-I tool for non-RCTs.67,68 The ROBIN-I tool was used to assess bias in 7 domains (supplemental table S1, available online only at http://www.archives-pmr.org/). Each domain was evaluated as having a low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias. If there was inadequate information to assess a particular domain, no information was assigned.67 Similarly, the ROBIN-II tool was used to assess the risk of bias in 5 domains (see supplemental table S1), with each domain evaluated as having low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias.68 The overall risk of bias for each study was assigned based on the Cochrane Scientific Committee recommendation available at https://methods.cochrane.org/risk-bias-2 and https://methods.cochrane.org/methods-cochrane/robins-i-tool. We used the Quality Assessment Tool developed by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute for pre-post studies.69 This tool includes 12 criteria to assess study quality (see supplemental table S1). Pre-post studies were evaluated as good, fair, or poor based on the guidance provided by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute. The overall risk of bias for each study is reported in table 1, and details on the risk of bias for individual studies can be found in supplemental table S1.

Results

Study selection

Of the 521 articles identified, 44 studies met our eligibility criteria (see fig 1). Of these, 26 studies (59%) were in people with MS, 15 (34%) in people with BI, 1 (2%) in people with SCI, and 2 (5%) in people with ALS. The study by Tavee et al85 included both people MS and people with peripheral neuropathy, but we only included data on patients with MS in this systematic review. Among the 44 studies included in our review, 26 were RCTs, 10 were non-RCTs, and 8 were pre-postintervention clinical trials without controls (table 2). A total of 7 studies were exclusively designed to assess the feasibility of MBIs in individuals with UMNDs (see table 1).

Study characteristics

The total number of participants in the 44 studies was 2313, and the mean ± SD sample size was 53±41 participants. The mean age was 45±11 years, and on average, 55%±32% of the study participants were female. Consistent with condition distribution, there was a higher percentage of women in MS studies compared with studies in other UMNDs. The intervention duration ranged from 2–12 weeks, with an average length of 8±2 weeks.

Interventions

Of the 44 studies, 80% (n=35) were exclusively delivered in person: 33 synchronously in a group and 2 synchronously in 1:1 session109,110 (see table 2). Six studies (14%) were exclusively online: 2 synchronously in a group,71,83 3 asynchronously individually (participants completed content within a prespecified timeline),80,111,113 and the delivery setting and mode was not clear in 1 online study92 (see table 2). Among the remaining 3 studies, 1 study compared in-person delivery with online delivery and control,99 another study conducted half of the intervention in person and half via telephone,100 and 1 study included in-person 1:1 sessions and daily listening to audio recordings at home.101,109,110

Of the 44 studies, 22 (50%) used the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program,114 7 (16%) used Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), an adaptation of the MBSR that incorporates some elements of cognitive-behavioral therapy tailored to improve symptoms of depression,115 and 5 studies (11%) reported that their mindfulness intervention was based on both MBSR and MBCT. The MBSR program protocol consists of 8 weekly 2- to 2.5-hour sessions, a full day (6–7 hours) retreat between sessions 6 and 7, as well as approximately 40–45 minutes of daily homework. The MBCT program consists of 8 weekly 2-hour sessions and does not specify home practice length.81,86,93 Several of the studies modified the MBCT and MBSR programs to increase its feasibility in individuals with UMNDs, such as adjusting the duration of the program or the duration of each session or eliminating a full-day retreat.95,109,110 The remaining 10 studies (see table 2) used various mindfulness exercises, such as mindful breathing, body scanning, and moment-to-moment awareness. In 2 studies, mindfulness exercises preceded an adjuvant therapy such as music listening101 and physical exercises.77 In 4 studies, mindfulness exercises focused on attentional, emotional, and behavioral regulation.75,81,84,96 In 3 online studies, MBI included daily audio/video-guided mindfulness exercises.101,111,113 In 1 study,85 a Buddhist monk delivered 3 forms of mindfulness meditation (walking, sitting, moving meditation) during each weekly session.

In addition to weekly sessions, the home practice was recommended in 32 studies (73%) (see table 2). The recommended duration for home practice ranged from 3–60 minutes daily, but the majority recommended 40–45 minutes daily. In 3 studies, the daily home practice of MBI was the main intervention.101,111,113

Control

Two types of control conditions were seen in MBI studies: (1) passive controls in which participants did not receive any intervention other than usual care or being placed on a waiting list in which these participant were offered the intervention after completion of the study or (2) active controls in which participants received an alternative intervention of duration similar to MBI (eg, physical exercise, psychoeducation, health promotion, stress management) (see table 2). Among the 36 clinical trials with a control group, 22 (61%) included a passive control group, 9 (25%) included an active control group, and 4 (11%) had both active and passive control groups (see table 2). One study did not plan to have a control group, but data from dropouts were used as a control.104

Follow-up

Less than half (n=19, 43%) included a follow-up assessment in addition to the postintervention assessment (see table 2). The follow-ups ranged from 1–12 months, with the majority 2–6 months after the completion of the intervention. However, 4 studies conducted a long-term follow-up 12 months after intervention completion.79,93,96,112

Risk of bias of the included studies

Among the 25 RCTs included in the evaluation of health-related outcomes, 19 (76%) were appraised to have a low risk or some risk of bias, and 6 (24%) had a high risk of bias (see supplemental table S1). Non-RCTs (n=10) either lacked information (n=7) required to assess the risk of bias or had serious concerns (n=3). Of 8 pre-post studies, only 1 pre-post study was considered poor quality, and the majority were considered either fair or good quality (88%).

Outcome measures

Feasibility

Feasibility measures included recruitment rate, the retention rate for the postintervention period, and the retention rate for follow-up assessments (see table 1). Among the 27 studies that provided data to calculate the recruitment rate, the mean rate was 67%±24%. Based on data from 40 studies, an average of 85%±14% of participants receiving an MBI completed the intervention and postintervention assessment (ie, retention). The mean retention rate at post intervention for in-person groups was 86%±13% and for virtually-delivered MBI groups was 80%±18%. The mean retention rate at post intervention was similar in passive controls at 87%±10% and 87%±9% in the active controls. The average retention rate at the first follow-up assessment was 80%±16% in MBI groups compared with 84%±16% in the passive control group and 85%±10% in the active control. However, the average retention rate at first follow-up was lower in online MBI groups at 70%±14% compared with in-person MBI groups at 83%±15%. Additionally, we noted that the retention rate at follow-up was lower in the 2 ALS studies112,113 in the MBI group (57% at 3 months and 40% at 6 months) than other UMNDs.

Adherence

Of the 44 studies included in this review, 20 studies provided participants’ adherence with either mindfulness training sessions or assigned home practice (see table 1). Five studies provided mean attendance at weekly sessions, 8 reported the percentage of participants who attended most or at least half of the assigned weekly sessions, and 3 studies presented the percentage of participants who completed all sessions (see table 1). Adherence to attendance to weekly MBI sessions ranged from 72%–95%. There was no significant difference in adherence to MBI sessions with in-person or online delivery modes (see table 1).

Although 32 studies (73%) recommended daily home practice of mindfulness exercises (see table 2), only 9 studies reported adherence with home practice (see table 1). In the studies that reported participants’ daily practice duration, average daily practice duration ranged from 17–55 minutes (see table 1), and overall adherence to home practice ranged from 55%–87% of recommended practice (see table 1).

Acceptability

Data regarding acceptability was reported in 6 BI101,104,106,108–110 and 3 MS studies.80,83,95

Quantitative feedback

Three studies (1 BI and 2 MS)83,95,108 reported satisfaction on a measure that used a 5-point Likert scale, and 1 BI study104 used a 4-point Likert scale, which was translated to a 5-point scale for comparison. The average satisfaction rating in these 4 studies among those receiving MBIs was 4.6 of 5. One study101 on mindful listening of music reported that 94.6% of participants found appointments convenient, and 73.3% reported it was feasible to engage in daily sessions for up to an hour.

Qualitative feedback

Five studies reported feedback or comments provided by participants.80,83,106,109,110 In 1 MS study, participants reported satisfaction with online program engagement and social interactions.83 The majority of comments reported indicated satisfaction (eg, “I am very happy with the online platform”; “I really enjoyed the online version of mindfulness program”). A smaller number of comments reported boredom with repeated practice. In this study, the main barriers to practicing MBI were reported as difficulty finding time, feeling fatigued, and experiencing pain. Another MS study evaluating online MBI evaluated acceptability via spontaneous and structured feedback sessions and found positive feedback toward the course, although no direct quotes were included.80

One BI study collected open comments from participants and found that participants generally enjoyed and perceived benefits from mindfulness meditation.109 Another BI study that collected posttreatment feedback reported that participants were very positive and consistently reported benefits that affected many areas of their life, with some participants even describing the intervention as “life changing.”106 A third BI study defined acceptability as the absence of adverse effects and noted that no significant adverse effects arose but that 3 participants found some of the practices challenging because of the emotions that surfaced.110

Health-related outcomes

The most commonly reported health-related outcomes were depression, anxiety, fatigue, and QOL. For calculating the relative difference or change, we only included data from RCTs because all the studies in non-RCTs either lacked the information to perform risk of bias or had serious concerns. More than a 5% relative difference in health-related outcome score was considered clinically significant.41 The outcomes included in each study and the tools used for assessment are presented in table 2.

Depression

Data from 16 RCTs were included in the analysis of relative change. There was a clinically significant relative reduction in symptoms of depression measured by various tools in 15 studies (fig 2). Postintervention median relative reduction in depression symptoms for participants in MBIs was −27% (IQR, −15% to −42%) compared with all controls (see fig 2), −43% (IQR, −40% to −44%) compared with passive controls only, and −14% (IQR, −13% to −21%) compared with active controls. This relative reduction in depression scores persisted even during 1–6 months of follow-up (see fig 2). Three studies reported follow-up data at 12 months (1 ALS, 1 MS, 1 BI).79,99,112 Relative reduction in depression scores persisted in ALS (−53%) and BI (−43%) but not MS (−2%) compared with usual controls at 12 months.

Anxiety

Data were available from 16 RCTs to analyze relative change. There was a clinically significant relative reduction in symptoms of anxiety measured by various tools in 15 studies (see fig 2). Postintervention median relative reduction in anxiety symptoms for participants in MBIs was −23% (IQR, −10% to −47%) compared with all controls (see fig 2), −46% (IQR, −26% to −47%) compared with passive controls, and −10% (IQR, −5% to −14%) compared with active controls. This relative reduction in anxiety scores persisted in all but 1 study73 during 1–6 months of follow-up (see fig 2). Three studies reported follow-up data at 12 months (1 ALS, 1 MS, 1 BI).79,99,112 Relative reduction in anxiety scores persisted in ALS (−59%) and BI (−22%) but not in MS (0%) compared with passive controls at 12 months.

Fatigue

Data from 7 RCTs were included in our quantitative analysis. Fatigue was collected as an outcome measure in MS (n=4) and BI (n=3) studies. There was a self-reported clinically significant reduction in symptoms of fatigue measured by various tools in 6 of the 7 studies (fig 3). Postintervention median relative reduction in fatigue symptoms for participants in MBIs was −19% (IQR, −11% to −24%) compared with all controls (see fig 3), −28% (IQR, −23% to −50%) compared with passive controls, and −11% (IQR, −9% to −21%) compared with active controls. The majority of studies reporting fatigue had active controls (n=5). This relative reduction in fatigue scores persisted only in 3 studies70,73,103 during 1–6 months of follow-up (see fig 3). Only 1 study among participants with MS reported effects of MBI 12 months post intervention, and the relative reduction in fatigue scores was not clinically significant (−2%).

Quality of life

Data from 10 RCTs were included in the quantitative analysis. A clinically significant improvement in self-reported QOL was measured by various tools in half (n=5) of the studies (see fig 3). Postintervention median relative improvement in QOL total or subscores for participants in MBIs was 12% (IQR, 4%–35%) compared with all controls (see fig 3), 12% (IQR, 7%–33%) compared with passive controls, and 7% (IQR, 4%–28%) compared with active controls. This relative improvement in QOL persisted or improved in 4 studies71,70,103,113 during 1–6 months of follow-up (see fig 3). Only 2 studies, 1 in ALS and 1 in MS, reported effects of MBI 12 months post intervention. The relative improvement in QOL was clinically significant in ALS (16%),112 but it was clinically not significant in MS (3%).79 In 1 MS study, QOL was measured by 2 different tools. In this study, the relative improvement in QOL post intervention (22%) was higher and clinically significant at 6-month follow-up (13%) when compared with QOL measured by the Hamburg Quality of Life Questionnaire in MS at post intervention (12%) and 6-month follow-up (−8%).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the most extensive systematic reviews of MBIs delivered to individuals with UMNDs evaluating the feasibility, adherence, acceptability, and efficacy of MBIs for commonly measured health-related outcomes. The majority of the studies in our review were conducted among individuals with MS (59%) and were 8 weeks in duration, with weekly sessions ranging from 1.5–2.5 hours. Over three-quarters of the studies reviewed reported on interventions based on MBSR, MBCT, or a combination of the 2. Additional daily home practice, typically practicing mindfulness for 30–45 minutes, was recommended in nearly half of the studies. Many of the programs were modified to add breaks or reduce the length of the program and home practice to decrease the burden of the intervention.

Regarding feasibility, results from this review suggest that individuals with UMNDs are likely to complete MBIs. On average, retention in MBIs was 85%, and retention at follow-up was 81%. Individuals with UMNDs also tended to adhere to the interventions. Although definitions differed somewhat, all studies reported over 72% of participants to be adherent to MBIs. Although only 9 studies document adherence to home practice, results suggest that participants engaged in at least half of the assigned home practice,76,79 and in many studies, over 80% of the assigned home practice was completed.82,95,101

Among the 8 studies that reported MBIs’ acceptability, quantitative reports of satisfaction with the intervention were high (4.5/5), and qualitative data echoed this. Although relatively few included studies explicitly reporting on participants’ acceptability of MBIs, a study by Simpson et al supports the assertion that MBIs are likely acceptable, at least to those with MS.116 However, most of the interventions reviewed in the present study adapted intervention delivery or content to better suit the participants with UMNDs. Given the importance of determining the acceptability of MBIs to individuals with UMNDs and the need to collect information to improve adaptations of such interventions, it is recommended that future studies include and report participant satisfaction/acceptability data.

The present study’s quantitative results related to MBI efficacy echo previous meta-analyses.59–61,117,118 Specifically, the present study found that MBIs led to clinically significant improvements in depression, anxiety, fatigue, and QOL symptoms compared with passive and active control groups (ie, >5% relative change). Although there was a greater relative improvement in health-related outcomes in the mindfulness condition compared with passive control conditions than active control conditions, MBIs consistently demonstrated at least a 7% relative reduction in symptoms compared with a variety of active control conditions.41 This is similar to the effects of MBIs on depression, anxiety, and fatigue seen in a meta-analysis of patients with MS, BI, and SCI.60,62,117,119 Thus, the present study’s findings add to the growing evidence highlighting the effectiveness of MBIs at improving health-related outcomes for individuals with UMNDs.

In light of the growing interest in, need for, and availability of remotely delivered health care,120 it is valuable to examine differences in online vs in-person MBIs. Additionally, it is important to consider the effect of synchronous (eg, using a virtual meeting platform to conduct live group or individual sessions) vs asynchronous (eg, allowing participants to listen to, watch, or practice intervention content at their own pace) online delivery. Given the potential benefit of remote intervention delivery to individuals with UMNDs, it was surprising that the vast majority (80%) of the interventions were delivered exclusively in person. Feasibility metrics were similar in online and in-person studies. Adherence metrics were also similar, although only a few studies reported such data. The small number of studies reporting acceptability data preclude meaningful comparisons between the acceptability of in-person vs online or synchronous vs asynchronous delivery. Given that MBIs delivered in person and online have similar retention and adherence rates, we recommend future studies examine the cost-effectiveness of online vs in-person delivery as well as the effect that online delivery may have on the acceptability of and accessibility to MBIs for people with UMNDs.

It is also crucial to study further the minimal duration of weekly sessions and minimal dose and frequency of home practice required to initiate and maintain changes in health-related outcomes in people with UMNDs. Online-delivered MBIs, including those delivered via mobile applications, remove some critical barriers people with UMNDs face in accessing health-promoting interventions, including needing and finding transportation, finding time amid multiple medical appointments, living far from a location in which MBIs are offered, or needing to avoid in-person appointments because of increased risk of contracting an infectious disease.120 Future RCTs comparing different doses of MBIs delivered via online or mobile applications are critically needed.

Study limitations

There were several limitations to this review. We excluded studies published prior to 2001 and only included studies published in English. We did not include any gray literature (non–peer-reviewed) because required data were not accessible to calculate feasibility metrics. Intervention inclusion criteria were limited to descriptions available, which may have made it difficult to ascertain all aspects of the interventions as they relate to MBIs. Efficacy data were limited to only 4 health-related outcomes because of the limited availability of other outcomes in the literature. Among the RCTs included in the efficacy analysis, 19% of the studies in depression and anxiety analysis, 29% of the studies included in fatigue analysis, and 10% of the studies included in QOL analysis were categorized as high risk of bias (see supplemental table S1). Given this, one should be cautious when interpreting health-related outcome results from these studies. However, it is unlikely that feasibility and acceptability metrics are affected by a high risk of bias. Available studies across our 4 UMNDs varied greatly, with very few publications focusing on individuals with ALS or SCI. As a result, the generalizability of our findings to people with ALS and SCI should consider these limitations.

Conclusions

Based on our systematic review, we conclude that MBIs are feasible and acceptable to people with UMNDs and that people with UMNDs are generally adherent to MBIs. MBIs improved symptoms of depression, anxiety, and fatigue and improved quality of life in people with UMNDs, particularly compared with passive controls but also compared with active controls. Greater information on acceptability/satisfaction is needed. Future studies are needed to assess the feasibility and effectiveness of alternate modes of delivery and doses of MBIs on health-related outcomes.
