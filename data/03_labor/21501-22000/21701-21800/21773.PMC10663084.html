Abstract

Purpose

Open radical nephrectomy with inferior vena cava thrombectomy (O-CT) is standard management for renal cell carcinoma with inferior vena caval thrombus. First reported a decade ago, robotic radical nephrectomy with caval tumor thrombectomy (R-CT) is minimally invasive option for this disease. We aim to perform a systematic review to assess the safety and feasibility of R-CT in terms of perioperative outcomes and compare the outcomes between R-CT and O-CT.

Materials and Methods

The PubMed®, Scopus®, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and Web of Science™ databases were searched using the free-text and MeSH terms “renal cell carcinoma”, “inferior vena cava”, “thrombosis” or “thrombus”, “robot”, and “thrombectomy. Studies reporting perioperative outcomes of R-CT and studies comparing R-CT with O-CT were included. The review was done in accordance with PRISMA guidelines.

Results

The searches retrieved 28 articles describing R-CT, including seven comparative studies. This systematic review included 1375 patients, out of which 329 patients were in single arm studies and 1046 patients were in comparative studies. Of 329 patients who underwent R-CT, 14.7% were level I, 60.9% level II, 20.4% level III and 2.5% level IV thrombus. Operative time ranged from 150–530 min; blood transfusion was administered in 38.2% (n=126). The overall complication rate was 30.3% (n=99). R-CT, in comparison to O-CT, was associated with a lower blood transfusion rate (18.4% vs 64.3%, p=0.002) and a lower complication rate (14.5% vs 36.7%, p=0.005). Major complication and 30-day mortality rates were similar in both groups.

Conclusion

R-CT has acceptable perioperative outcomes in carefully selected patients. Compared with O-CT, R-CT is associated with a lower blood transfusion rate and fewer overall complications. In experienced hands with carefully selected patients, R-CT is feasible and safe with acceptable outcomes, however, selection bias limits definitive inference of these results and optimal patient selection criteria remains to be described.

Introduction

1.0

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) presents with inferior vena cava (IVC) thrombus in approximately 6% of cases.1 Radical nephrectomy with IVC thrombectomy was first reported by Skinner et al. in 1972.2 Since then, it has become the standard of care for non-metastatic RCC with IVC thrombus, and is associated with 5-year overall survival of 40–68%.3 For many years, open radical nephrectomy with IVC thrombectomy (O-CT) was the only available option for management of RCC with IVC thrombus.4 Due to the technical complexity of this operation, even with the rapid adoption of laparoscopic radical nephrectomy, there have been few reports describing the use of a pure laparoscopic approach for patients with RCC and IVC thrombus.5,6

Robotic surgery has the advantages of a surgeon-controlled camera allowing three-dimensional views, wristed instrumentation, and improved dexterity and precision, and has revolutionized the field of urology-oncology.7,8 Although the first robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy was performed in 20009, it was not until a decade later that robotic-assisted radical nephrectomy with IVC tumor thrombectomy (R-CT) was described for RCC with IVC thrombus.10 Since the first case series of R-CT was reported in 201110, there have been various reports detailing the safety and feasibility of R-CT for IVC tumor thrombus. As we enter the second decade of R-CT in January 2022, it is important to acknowledge that most of the research published on R-CT has been generated from a single institutional analysis and there is paucity of a robustly performed research evidence. Our goal is to provide a contemporary evidence of accumulated knowledge from the last 10 years. To that end, we performed a systematic review to assess the safety and feasibility of R-CT in terms of perioperative outcomes and compare the outcomes between R-CT and O-CT.

Materials and Methods

2.

Protocol and Registration

2.1

This review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) recommendations.11 The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; ID-CRD42021294089).

Search Strategy

2.2

A systematic search of the PubMed®, Scopus®, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Web of Science databases™ was performed and all literature available through November 2021 was included. The search strategy included a combination of the following free-text and MeSH terms: “renal cell carcinoma”, “inferior vena cava”, “thrombosis” or “thrombus”, “robot”, and “thrombectomy”. No limits were applied. Bibliographies of the included studies and previous review articles were hand-searched to identify further potentially relevant literature. The search results were exported to Rayyan software12 for screening and evaluation.

Study selection and Data extraction

2.3

Two authors (H.G. and D.K.) separately reviewed the records to select the studies. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion. The studies were grouped into single-arm series reporting perioperative and oncological outcomes of R-CT and comparative studies reporting perioperative outcomes between R-CT and O-CT. Studies comparing various techniques of R-CT were assessed separately.

For single arm series, study eligibility was defined using the PICOS (patient, intervention, comparator, outcome, study type) approach. Inclusion criteria were:(P) studies focused on patients with a diagnosis of kidney cancer and IVC thrombus; (I) undergoing R-CT; (C) no comparator arm and (O) evaluating perioperative outcomes (operative time, blood loss, transfusion rate, rate of conversion to open surgery, overall complication rate, major complication rate, length of hospital stay); (S) in retrospective or prospective studies.

For comparative series, inclusion criteria were: (P) studies focused on patients with a diagnosis of kidney cancer and IVC thrombus; (I) undergoing R-CT; (C) undergoing O-CT and (O) evaluating perioperative outcomes (operative time, blood loss, transfusion rate, overall complication rate, major complication rate, length of hospital stay, short term mortality and readmission rate); (S) in retrospective or prospective comparative studies.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) studies reporting R-CT on animal or cadaveric models; (2) non-original studies including review articles, editorial comments, meeting abstracts, or letters to the editor.

Study appraisal

2.4

Studies were categorized according to the 2011 levels of evidence (LOEs) for therapeutic studies13and papers were categorized according to the IDEAL recommendations.14 The level of IVC thrombus was stratified using the Mayo Clinic classification.15 Renal cancer was staged using the 7th American Joint Cancer Committee Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) system16 because it was the staging method most used during the study period. The methodology used to report complications was assessed using the Martin criteria,17 and the systematic review was performed in agreement with the PRISMA statement11 as per the European Association of Urology guidelines.18

The risk of bias assessment for case reports and case series was performed using the criteria refined by Murad et al.19 and the guidelines suggested by Knoll et al.18 The modified Newcastle Ottawa Scale20 was used to assess the risk of bias in non-randomized comparative studies.

Statistical analysis

2.5

For pooled analysis of single-arm studies, categorical variables were reported as the proportion and continuous variables as the weighted mean difference. Information on missing data was included, and the denominator was reported for each variable. The results of the comparative analysis were presented as the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval for dichotomous variables. The cumulative weighted mean difference could not be calculated for continuous variables due to the non-availability of means. A p-value <0.10 was used to indicate statistical heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was used when heterogeneity was significant and a random-effects model when it was not. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis was performed using Review Manager v5.2 software designed for composing Cochrane Reviews (Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) and Stata (version 15; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA)

Results

3.

Quality of studies and level of evidence

3.1

Figure 1 shows the flow diagram for this systematic review. Most of the surgical series were retrospective and limited to single-center experience (LOE 4). Few studies were multi-institutional collaborative analyses (LOE 4). There were no randomized controlled comparative studies. Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 show the results of the risk of bias assessment for the studies included in this review.

Perioperative outcomes after R-CT: single-arm studies

3.2

Table 1 summarizes the various case reports and case studies reporting outcomes of R-CT. A total of 21 studies10,21–40 were included. All the studies were published in English except for one33, for which an English translation was retrieved. Eight (38.1%) of the 21 single-arm studies were case reports involving one or two patients and nine (42.9%) included more than 10 patients. All studies provided level 4 evidence according to the Oxford classification13 and most were classified as phase 1 or 2a for surgical innovation based on the IDEAL recommendations.14 (Table 1).

Overall, 329 patients who underwent R-CT were included in the analysis. For clarity, we present the denominator for each variable assessed in our results to transparently account for missing data in some studies. Most of the patients included had a right-sided renal tumor (74.9%, n=245). Most patients had level II thrombus (60.9%, 199/327) while level I (48,14.7%) and level III (67,20.4%) formed the other major component. Only 2.5% (n=8) patients had level IV thrombus. The estimated weighted mean primary tumor size was 8.2 cm (range, 5.8–12 cm,). As per the TNM classification for RCC, 76.4% of patients (230/301) had a stage T3b tumor and 12.9% (39/301) had pN1 disease, 11.9% of patients (36/301) had metastatic disease at the time of surgery. Four studies reported the use of neoadjuvant systemic therapy and overall, 11.1% of patients (n=13/118) received neoadjuvant therapy. Clear cell RCC was the most common histopathological subtype (73.2%, 181/247) and most of the tumors were Fuhrman grade II (37.1%, 79/213) or III (38.9%, 83/213).

The perioperative data for R-CT is shown in Table 2. Most of the surgeries were performed by extensively experienced robotic surgeons and involved multi-disciplinary approach with intraoperative collaboration of hepatobiliary surgeons and cardiovascular surgeons (Supplementary Table 3). Angioembolization was performed before surgery in 32.5% (102/314). The mean/median operative time was 150–530 min with a weighted mean operative time of 281.9 min. Overall, the estimated median blood loss was 150–2000 ml (n=327), and 38.2% (126/329) received perioperative blood transfusion. Lymph node dissection was performed in 60.7% (102/168) with a lymph node yield ranging from 7–44 nodes (n=84). The rate of conversion to open surgery was 1.2% (4/327). Intraoperatively, complications were encountered in 1.8% (6/327) with vascular injury reported in 0.9% cases (3/327), bowel injury in 0.6% cases (2/327) and liver laceration in 0.3% cases (2/327). The in-hospital stay ranged from 1.2 to 18 days. The overall perioperative complication rate was 30.3% (99/327) and the major complication rate (Clavien-Dindo grade III or higher) was 8.5% (28/327). The surgical margin positivity rate was 0.01% (2/282). Overall, 34.2% (n=36/105) patients received postoperative systemic therapy. Fourteen studies included data from postoperative follow-up, the duration of which ranged from 3.2 months to 23 months (Table 2).

Predictors of perioperative outcomes

3.3

Only one study reported on predictors of perioperative outcomes following R-CT. In a study of 120 cases, Shen et al.36 identified greater thrombus length, a higher level of thrombus (III–IV), and early surgical learning phase (as defined by first forty cases) to be independent predictors of operative time and blood loss in R-CT. They also reported IVC wall resection (cavectomy), III–IV thrombus, and an early surgical learning phase to be independent predictors of early postoperative complications.

Comparison of robotic versus open radical nephrectomy with IVC thrombectomy

3.4

Four studies involving 1046 patients, 110 patients undergoing R-CT and 936 patients undergoing O-CT were included41–44; all had a retrospective non-randomized comparative design and one analyzed data in the National Cancer Database (NCDB). The clinicopathological characteristics of the patients in these studies are shown in Table 3 and their perioperative outcomes in Table 4. All the comparative studies, except NCDB analysis, involved patients with level I/II thrombi.

Operative time

3.4.1

Four studies compared the operative time between O-CT and R-CT (Figure 2a). In a matched cohort analysis (matched using age, American Society of Anesthesiology score, tumor size and length of IVC thrombus) of 31 patient pairs undergoing robotic or open surgery,41 the median operative time was significantly shorter in the R-CT group than in the O-CT group (150 min vs 230 min, p<0.001). In contrast, Rose et al.43 and Vuong et al.44 found that the median operative time was significantly longer in the R-CT group than in the O-CT group [284min vs 242min, p=0.03 and 350.5min vs 208min, p=0.01 respectively]. In a multi-institutional analysis,42 there was no significant difference in median operative times between O-CT and R-CT (226 min vs 260 min, p=0.9). Therefore, no definitive inference could be made regarding operative time due to the limited number of studies (Figure 2a).

Blood loss and rate of blood transfusion

3.4.2

All four studies consistently reported a lower median blood loss value with R-CT (Figure 2b). The blood transfusion rate was lower in the R-CT arm than in the O-CT arm (18.4% vs 64.3%, odds ratio [OR] 0.15, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.04–0.49, p=0.002; Figure 2c).

Complication rate

3.4.3

The overall complication rate was significantly lower in the R-CT group than in the O-CT group (14.5% vs 36.7%, OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.15–0.71, p=0.005; Figure 3a). However, the major complication rate (grade III or higher) was not statistically different between the R-CT and O-CT groups (6.6% vs 18.4%, OR 0.46, 95% CI 0.16–1.33, p=0.15; Figure 3b).

Thirty-day postoperative mortality

3.4.4

The 30-day postoperative mortality rate was 1.8% (2/110) in the R-CT group and 1.9% (18/936) in the O-CT group (OR 1.14, 95% CI 0.31–4.20, p=0.9: Figure 3c).

Length of hospital stay

3.4.5

The hospital stay was significantly shorter in the R-CT group across all the studies (Figure 3d). In the NCDB analysis,42 the median hospital stay was 4 days in the R-CT group and 6 days in the O-CT group (p<0.001). In that study, multivariate regression analysis showed that the robotic approach was associated with 26% reduction in length of hospital stay (β= −0.30; 95% CI 0.46, −0.15, p<0.001). However, selection and reporting bias with unmeasured confounding make definitive inference difficult.

Unplanned hospital readmission rate

3.4.6

In an analysis by Beksac et al. using the NCDB,42 the unplanned hospital readmission rate in the O-CT group and R-CT group was similar (Table 4). In the study by Vuong et al.,42 the hospital readmission rate was lower in the R-CT group than in the O-CT group (0% vs 13.3%) however, the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.5).

Survival outcomes

3.4.7

Gu et al.41 compared survival outcomes between O-CT and R-CT over respective median follow-up durations of 44.8 months and 27 months (p<0.001). They found no significant difference in progression-free survival (p=0.7) or overall survival (p=0.18) between the two groups. Furthermore, there was no association between the surgical approach and overall survival or progression-free survival in multivariate Cox regression analysis. Similarly, Rose et al.43 reported no difference in median progression-free survival (p=0.7) or overall survival(p=0.16). (Table 4).

Technical aspects of R-CT

3.5

Robotic techniques for control of IVC (Figure 4)

3.5.1

R-CT have evolved over the past decade. In an early study, Abaza et al.10 described tangential clamping of the IVC for retrieval of level I thrombus while maintaining blood flow through a portion of the IVC. However, larger thrombi required cross-clamping of the IVC and cavotomy for thrombus retrieval.[45] Ramirez et al.27 recommended that the surgical steps of O-CT be replicated in R-CT. Gill et al.24 proposed several modifications to the open approach for R-CT, which included a “IVC-first, kidney-last” approach with renal mobilization only after extraction of the thrombus and repair of the IVC. They recommended a strict “minimal touch” technique during caval mobilization to prevent inadvertent manipulation and embolization of the thrombus. To minimize the risk of major hemorrhage, they proposed a “midline-first, lateral-last” surgical strategy whereby the inter-aortocaval region is dissected first and the lateral renal hilar region later.

The findings of a study by Wang et al.32 highlighted the crucial role of the first porta hepatis (FPH) and second porta hepatis (SPH) as landmarks in level II thrombectomy. They defined the FPH as an H-shaped sulcus on the visceral surface of the liver where the portal vein, common hepatic duct, and hepatic artery enter and exit the liver together and the SPH as the intersection of the left, middle, and right hepatic veins entering the IVC. For level II thrombus with proximal limit below the FPH, infrahepatic clamping seemed feasible, just like level I thrombectomy, by retraction of the liver and ligation of accessory hepatic veins (usually three). However, for level II thrombus with proximal limit of thrombus extending beyond FPH but below SPH, complete mobilization of the liver with ligation of 3–5 accessory hepatic veins and intrahepatic control of the IVC is required.

For patients with tumor thrombus invading IVC, Shi et al46 showed the feasibility of robot-assisted cavectomy and compared 30 patients undergoing robot-assisted cavectomy with 60 patients undergoing robot-assisted thrombectomy (cavotomy). They found significantly longer operative time, higher estimated blood loss and higher perioperative complications in the cavectomy group.

Robotic technique for level III and level IV thrombus

3.5.2

Management of level III tumor thrombus involves complete mobilization of liver and placement of tourniquet in suprahepatic infradiaphragmatic IVC proximal to thrombus. (Figure 5) The management of level IV tumor thrombus using a robotic approach is a technique in evolution. Hui et al.47 reported the use of thoracoscopic isolation and occlusion of the supradiaphragmatic IVC. Wang et al.49 reported the use of thoracoscopic strategy in six patients with level IV thrombus and described a “segmental thrombectomy” technique for patients with level IV thrombus with limited collateralization and no evidence of IVC wall invasion. This technique involves segmental removal of atrial thrombus using a thoracoscopic approach and removal of the infradiaphragmatic thrombus via robotic assistance with thoracoscopic control of the supradiaphragmatic IVC. Furthermore, the same group48 proposed a modified sequential vascular control strategy, which mimics the open ‘milking’ technique, permitting early unclamping of the suprahepatic IVC and porta hepatis pedicel and to limit the need for cardiopulmonary bypass in patients undergoing R-CT for level III and level IV thrombus. They compared perioperative outcomes in 15 patients, in whom a traditional vascular control technique was used with those in 12 patients in whom a modified sequential control technique was used. They reported a significantly shorter porta hepatis clamping time (19 min vs 47 min, p<0.001) and cardiopulmonary bypass time (60 min vs 87 min, p<0.05). They also reported fewer perioperative complications (83.3% vs 93.3%, p<0.05), a shorter hospital stay (9 days vs 15 days, p<0.001), and more rapid postoperative recovery of liver, renal, and coagulation function.

Robotic technique for left side renal tumor with thrombus

3.5.3

Tumor laterality is an important consideration in R-CT. The entire surgery for a right-sided tumor could be performed in the left lateral decubitus position whereas IVC thrombectomy for a left-sided tumor is performed in the left lateral decubitus position followed by radical nephrectomy in the right lateral decubitus position. Most early series included patients with left-sided tumors, who underwent preoperative angioembolization.24,25 However, Rose et al.35 advocated for left renal mobilization and ligation of the renal artery before IVC thrombectomy, thereby avoiding the need for preoperative angioembolization. Subsequently, Aghazadeh et al.42 reported a case in which they successfully treated a left-sided renal tumor with a level II thrombus by applying a single-dock approach using the Xi® robotic platform with the patient supine in a steep Trendelenburg position. Use of this technique eliminated the need for preoperative renal artery embolization and repositioning of patients.

Incorporation of endovascular approach for proximal IVC control and thrombus extraction

3.5.4

Various innovative endovascular approaches have been described for proximal control of the IVC. Kundavaram et al.28 used a 9-Fr intracaval Fogarty balloon catheter in a retrograde fashion to obtain proximal IVC control. Alahmari et al.38 described insertion and placement of a balloon catheter above the tumor thrombus through endovascular access via the right internal jugular vein under fluoroscopy for proximal control of IVC. Another technological innovation was vena cava endoscopy whereby a flexible cystoscope is inserted via a 10-mm additional trocar after thrombectomy to rule out any residual disease.28 These novel strategies could make minimally invasive thrombectomy easier to perform but need further validation.

Retroperitoneal approach for R-CT

3.5.5

Use of the retroperitoneal approach for R-CT is limited.49 With the potential benefit of direct access to renal artery and IVC in retroperitoneum, it can be utilized in patients with prior transperitoneal surgery with extensive adhesions. Wu et al.50 demonstrated the feasibility of robotic-assisted retroperitoneal approach for thrombectomy in patients with right side RCC and level I–II thrombus. They compared 16 patients undergoing retroperitoneal R-CT with 19 patients undergoing transperitoneal R-CT. They reported a significantly shorter operative time (130 min vs 145 min, p=0.003) and lower estimated blood loss (160 ml vs 240 ml, p=0.024) with retroperitoneal approach. The perioperative complication rate and length of hospital stay were similar in the two study groups.

Discussion

4.0

Since the advent of robotic surgery and rapid adoption of DaVinci® robotic assisted laparoscopic technology for prostatectomy and nephron-sparing surgery, the indications for robotic surgery have gradually expanded to include more complex procedures, including radical cystectomy51 and retroperitoneal lymph node dissection.52 However, it was not until eleven years after the introduction of robotic prostatectomy in 2000 that R-CT was described. Given that a decade has passed since publication of the first case series, we performed this comprehensive systematic review to assess the perioperative outcomes of R-CT. Our goal is to provide a contemporary summary of accumulated knowledge from the last 10 years. Given that the current evidence supporting the use of R-CT is modest, our review provides a comprehensive summary of accumulating evidence. The available literature supports the safety and feasibility of R-CT and that the perioperative outcomes with this approach are acceptable.

On comparative analysis with O-CT, R-CT was associated with lower blood loss and lower blood transfusion requirement; lower complication rate, and shorter hospital stay.

One of the key differences between R-CT and O-CT is the high rate of preoperative angioembolization for R-CT. Preoperative angioembolization in advanced RCC has always remained a point of contention.53–56 In O-CT, several studies have failed to demonstrate any significant benefit of preoperative angioembolization.55,56 However, angioembolization has been shown to be beneficial in R-CT by decompression of venous collaterals, enhanced robotic efficacy and early disconnection of left renal vein in left RCC.

The Mayo clinic classification15 is most utilized for level of tumor thrombus in RCC. However, there remains ambiguity in literature for a precise definition of level III thrombus and this becomes more pertinent in context of R-CT, owing to the surgical complexity for tumors extending above the hepatic veins. Originally, Neves and Zincke15 described level III thrombus as extension to intrahepatic vena cava but below the diaphragm. Later, Blute et al57 further refined level III thrombus as the thrombus at or above the level of hepatic veins but below the diaphragm and this has been widely popular. However, few robotic series24,26,30 classified any thrombus above the inferior margin of the liver as a level III thrombus. Hence, this warrants caution in interpretation of results and a subclassification for level III thrombus based on whether the thrombus is below, at the level of, or above the major hepatic veins might be useful.58

The surgical technique for R-CT continues to evolve, with constant innovations such as endovascular control of the proximal portion of IVC and use of a single-dock approach. However, the experience of R-CT remains limited. Comparing the evolution of R-CT with that of another complex urological procedure, namely, robotic-assisted radical cystectomy (RARC), there were at least 105 reports on the outcomes of RARC59 in the decade following publication of the first case series in 2003.60 Therefore, experience with R-CT remains comparatively limited despite significant improvements in robotic technology. One plausible explanation for the difference in uptake could be the considerable complexity of performing robotic thrombectomy, which involves manipulation of major vessels and a significant risk of hemodynamic compromise secondary to bleeding.

R-CT is evolving in the management of RCC with IVC thrombus and has acceptable perioperative outcomes. Although the conclusions of this systematic review are based on the best available evidence in the current literature, a major drawback is the lack of high-quality data, case selection bias, and variation in the methodology used for reporting surgical complications. Being observational in nature, these studies represent results of highly experienced robotic surgeons across few centers with selection and reporting bias and multiple confounding factors. Further, with the lack of any randomized controlled trial, the oncological safety of R-CT yet remains to be evaluated. Hence, a well-designed prospective controlled trial is needed to assess the true efficacy of R-CT.

Conclusion

5.0

The available literature published throughout the past decade has demonstrated that R-CT appears to be safe and feasible with acceptable perioperative outcomes in carefully selected patients performed by expert robotic surgeons. In comparison with O-CT, R-CT is associated with a lower blood transfusion rate, fewer overall complications, and a shorter hospital stay. However, despite the passing of a decade since the introduction of R-CT, adoption of robotic technology in this space appears to be limited to a small number of high-volume centers. Moreover, the overall level of evidence was low for included studies due to selection bias, multiple confounding factors and limited follow-up durations, thereby, making any definitive inference difficult.

Figure 5.

Surgical technique for robot assisted radical nephrectomy with inferior vena cava (IVC) thrombectomy for level III thrombus:

Liver dissection: It involves complete mobilization of liver with division of perihepatic ligaments, including round and falciform ligaments, right and left triangular ligaments and coronary ligaments in liver. Further additional short hepatic veins ligation is done and right, left and caudate lobes of the liver are mobilized off the IVC.

Vasculature control: Vessel tourniquets are placed in suprahepatic and infradiaphragmatic IVC. Clamping of infrarenal IVC, left renal vein, porta hepatis (Pringle maneuver) and suprahepatic IVC is done for thrombus retrieval.

Modified robotic sequential control technique: The IVC inferior to the hepatic vein is clamped after the proximal part of the thrombus is pulled down. Subsequently, the suprahepatic and the porta hepatis clamps are released. This allows for the return of blood from the liver into the IVC. Finally, complete tumor thrombectomy and IVC reconstruction is performed.
