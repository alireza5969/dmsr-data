Incidence, Current Guidelines and Management of Gastrointestinal Bleeding after Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Background: There is a significant increase in morbidity and mortality in patients complicated by major bleeding following transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). It has become more challenging to manage such complications when the patient needs to be on anticoagulation or antiplatelet agent post-procedure to prevent thrombotic/embolic complications.

Methods: We systematically reviewed all available randomized controlled trials and observational studies to identify incidence rates of gastrointestinal bleeding post-procedure. After performing a systematic search, a total of 8731 patients from 15 studies (5 RCTs and 10 non-RCTs) were included in this review.

Results: The average rate of gastrointestinal bleeding during follow-up was 3.0% in randomized controlled trials and 1.9% among observational studies.

Conclusion: Gastrointestinal bleeding has been noted to be higher in the RCTs as compared to observational studies. This review expands knowledge of current guidelines and possible management of patients undergoing TAVR.

INTRODUCTION

1

Both surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) and transthoracic aortic valve replacement (TAVR) are associated with an increased risk of postoperative bleeding complications [1-4]. There is a significant increase in morbidity and mortality in patients complicated by major bleeding following both TAVR and SAVR [2, 5]. In a study by Genereux et al., 40% of major late bleeding complications (MLBCs) associated with TAVR were due to gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) [3]. Previous literature demonstrated that non-access-site bleeding, female gender, frailty, development of new-onset atrial fibrillation, diabetes, and dual antiplatelet therapy are predictors of bleeding after long-term follow-up [6, 7]. Several studies showed that TAVR had been associated with a moderate risk of severe upper GIB [3, 8, 9].

As the number of TAVRs performed annually in the US continues to rise, it is essential to identify risk factors, incidences, and predictors of GIB following TAVR. Understanding these risk factors may help clinicians identify patients at greater risk of post-TAVR GIB. It may also help clinicians make more informed decisions about post-TAVR antithrombotic regimens for their patients to reduce the incidence of GIB, mortality, and overall healthcare costs. However, there is a paucity of studies evaluating patient characteristics and incidence of GIB and antithrombotic regimen recommendations for patients at high risk for GIB after undergoing TAVR. In this review article, we aim to summarize the current epidemiology of GIB following TAVR.

METHODS

2

Incidence of GIB after TAVR

2.1

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary Table 1) [10]. Two authors were involved in formulating the search strategies and independently going through the articles used for the meta-analysis (VV and RD). Between January 2011 and October 2021, MeSH terms such as “transcatheter aortic valve implantation,” “transcatheter aortic valve replacement,” “gastrointestinal hemorrhage,” and “gastrointestinal hemorrhage” were used to identify articles that included data on the incidence of gastrointestinal (GI) bleed in patients who had undergone transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) using PubMed. Inclusion criteria were patients above 18 years of age who had undergone TAVR and had been followed up for the incidence of GIB post-procedure. A total of 6903 results were obtained and were screened for articles fitting the inclusion criteria (Supplementary Table 1). After screening for duplicates, 6777 articles were selected for further perusal. After title/abstract review, 6746 articles were excluded, and 31 articles were included for further analysis in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Among them, 16 articles were excluded as they did not have data available for the incidence of GIB despite containing information regarding MLBCs. A total of 15 studies were used for the final analysis (Fig. 1). Among them, 5 were randomized control trials (RCTs), and 10 were non-RCTs (Table 1) [3, 5, 8, 9, 11-16, 17-22]. Among the non-RCT studies included, three were based on data from national TAVR registries, one was a multicenter study, and the rest were all single-center retrospective or prospective studies [5, 8, 9, 15-21]. We collected data regarding population number, age in years, time of follow-up, percentages of GIB, and other comorbidities given in the tables from the included studies. Among the 15 studies included in the final analysis, 3 reported the incidence of GIB in the immediate perioperative period only, while the rest reported the incidence of GIB in the follow-up period after hospital discharge following the TAVR procedure.

RESULTS

3

A total of 8731 patients from 15 studies (5 RCTs and 10 non-RCTs) were included in this review.

RCTs

3.1

3240 patients were included in the RCTs. The average age of the patients was 82.2 years, and 52.5% were females. Regarding comorbidities, diabetes mellitus was noted in 34.6%, hypertension in 87.6%, CKD in 42%, and smoking in 25.3% of patients. The average rate of GIB during follow-up was 3%. The mean duration of follow-up was 10.5 months (Table 1).

Non-RCTs

3.2

5491 patients from non-RCTs were included in the meta-analysis. The average age of the patients was 82.5 years, and 55% were female. Regarding comorbidities, 29.1% had diabetes mellitus, 80.1% had a history of hypertension, 48.3% had CKD, and 21.3% were smokers. The mean duration of follow-up was 19.8 months. The average rate of GIB during follow-up was 1.9%. Among patients who developed GIB, 24.2% of them required blood transfusion (Table 1).

Here we have also included meta-analyses of the RCTs that are relevant to the issue (Table 2), with Osman and colleagues conducting the most recent one, which showed a significant reduction in clinically significant bleeding (CSB) in the group on aspirin monotherapy as compared with DAPT (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.32-0.75, p=0.001, NNT=19) [23-30]. They did not find any difference between aspirin monotherapy and DAPT groups in terms of all-cause mortality (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.62-1.62, p=1.00) [31]. Another meta-analysis reported by Ando and colleagues did not show any statistically significant difference between SAPT and DAPT in terms of outcomes such as life-threatening bleeding (2.9 vs. 6.2%, OR 0.45; 95% CI 0.17-1.20, p = 0.11, I2 = 13%), major bleeding (2.9 vs. 4.3%, OR 0.66; 95% CI 0.23-1.89, p = 0.44, I2 = 0%), major vascular complications (3.0 vs. 6.0%, OR 0.53; 95% CI 0.14-2.01, p = 0.35, I2 = 29%) or a composite outcome of life-threatening and major bleeding (5.7 vs. 10.4%, OR 0.52; 95% CI 0.25-1.08, p = 0.08, I2 = 0%) [23]. Maes et al. compared DAPT and SAPT according to time-sensitive outcomes. Their meta-analysis showed significantly higher occurrence of 30-day composite outcome (17.6% vs. 10.9%, OR: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.00-2.98, p=0.050), with higher rate of major/life-threatening bleeding events in the DAPT group (11.4% vs. 5.2%, OR: 2.24, 95% CI: 1.12-4.46, p=0.022). No difference was found between SAPT and DAPT group in 30-day mortality rate (5.2% vs. 3.8%, OR: 1.40 [95% CI: 0.56 to 3.51], p=0.477) [32]. They also found that most of the bleeding events were related to GIB after the periprocedural period (>48 hrs. post-TAVR). Additionally, although a meta-analysis conducted by Kuno et al. included RCTs and non-randomized studies in their analysis, they also provided a sensitivity analysis limiting only to the included RCTs. It compared different anti-thrombotic strategies (SAPT, DAPT, or OAC+SAPT) for outcomes such as mortality and major and/or life-threatening bleeding according to VARC and VARC-2 criteria [33]. This sensitivity analysis showed no survival differences among the treatment arms, and SAPT had significantly lower bleeding rates than DAPT (HR 0.34, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.64) [24].

DISCUSSION

4

Treatment of GIB in patients with TAVR is complicated since it poses a great clinical dilemma: restarting antithrombotic therapy prematurely could lead to recurrent bleeding, whereas delaying treatment re-initiation puts the patient at significant risk for thrombotic complications. Therefore, in the section below, we provide up-to-date information regarding the incidence, current guidelines, and available literature on antiplatelet and anticoagulation therapy to enhance decision-making regarding managing bleeding events associated with TAVR.

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (TAVR) has been the gold standard treatment for symptomatic severe aortic stenosis in surgically inoperable risk patients, and the use has been expanded in intermediate and low-risk patients as well. However, despite the continuous improvement in techniques and expertise, thromboembolic and bleeding complications after TAVR remain frequent and strongly correlate to morbidity and mortality [34]. Various guidelines have provided and updated the rationale for the antithrombotic regimen to prevent thromboembolic events (Table 3). As larger RCTs are still awaited, current guidelines for antithrombotic regimens following TAVR are mainly based on observational studies and expert opinions. For mechanical valves, 2020 American Heart Association (AHA), 2017 European Society of Cardiology (ESC), and 2012 American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) guidelines all have recommended the long-term use of oral anticoagulants (OAC) using Vitamin K Antagonists (VKA) for all patients [35-37]. Novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs) would not be the agents of choice in patients with mechanical valves as the safety and efficacy of NOACs are unclear and are studied, especially in patients with end-stage renal dysfunction (ESRD) and on dialysis. Additionally, the RE-ALIGN trial (Randomized, Phase II Study to Evaluate the Safety and Pharmacokinetics of Oral Dabigatran Etexilate in Patients after Heart Valve Replacement) showed an increased risk of thromboembolic as well as bleeding events with no clear benefit in patients taking NOACs as compared with VKA [38].

For bioprosthetic valves, guidelines differ in management and depend on two major factors: 1) Presence of concurrent indications for oral anticoagulation, such as atrial fibrillation. 2) Presence of concurrent indications for antiplatelet therapy, such as recent acute coronary syndrome. The choice and duration of antithrombotic therapy vary amongst peri- and post-TAVR periods, considering the risk of bleeding. However, these recommendations have not been established yet and are under current investigation.

In post-TAVR patients without concurrent indication for therapeutic anticoagulation and no current indication for dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), guidelines recommend using SAPT with daily aspirin 75-100 mg or clopidogrel 75 mg daily in patients with contraindications to aspirin [39, 40]. A suggested reasonable alternative is to use DAPT for three to six months depending upon valve type, followed by lifelong SAPT. This is because post-TAVR DAPT has been shown to increase the risk of bleeding without improving clinical outcomes compared with SAPT [39]. In patients with concurrent indications for DAPT but no indication for anticoagulation, the duration of DAPT is governed by the concurrent indication followed by lifelong SAPT [35, 36]. ACC/AHA 2020 suggested VKA for three months or more in patients with a low risk of bleeding. Although anticoagulation plus antiplatelet therapy has been shown to decrease subclinical valvular abnormalities (e.g., subclinical leaflet motion abnormalities), it should not be used routinely in patients without concurrent indication of anticoagulation because of the increased risk of thromboembolic and bleeding events, as well as increased mortality [41, 42].

For post-TAVR patients with concurrent indications of therapeutic anticoagulation but no indication of antiplatelet therapy, only anticoagulation therapy using VKA or direct-acting anticoagulants (DOACs) is sufficient with no additional antithrombotic therapy is required. The choice of anticoagulation therapy depends upon the indication for anticoagulation and other clinical factors [22]. In patients with concurrent indications of both anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy, treatment with anticoagulant plus antiplatelet therapy is recommended, as shown in the POPular TAVR and PARTNER 2 trials [43, 44]. Because these guidelines are based on expert consensus and are not evidence-based, they yield low-grade recommendations and are not practiced unanimously (Table 3). A consensus document of the ESC working group was recently released in 2021, which provides a graphical presentation of pre and post-procedural antithrombotic therapies in patients undergoing bioprosthetic TAVR, with the presence and absence of indications for OACs and the presence or absence of recent (<3 months) coronary stenting [40]. Here we have provided a figure representing the choices of antithrombotic therapy related to TAVR procedures in patients with or without indications for anticoagulation (Fig. 2).

Since the commencement of TAVR in 2002, 6 RCTs have been conducted on antithrombotic therapy in TAVR patients. Table 4 includes a list of completed RCTs that evaluate various postprocedural antithrombotic regimens in patients undergoing TAVR [13, 39, 41, 45, 46]. Out of the 6 RCTs, 3 RCTs showed that DAPT was associated with a higher risk of bleeding events than SAPT in patients undergoing TAVR. The cumulative results from the RCTs suggest that the use of DAPT following TAVR should be limited because of the higher risk of bleeding, and more adequately powered RCTs are needed to define the optimal antiplatelet strategy following TAVR. An RCT conducted by Ussia et al. showed no difference between DAPT and aspirin alone groups in the incidence of bleeding events at both 30-day and 6-month, and this topic remains controversial. The strategy of adding clopidogrel to aspirin for 3 months after TAVR was not found to be superior to aspirin alone [45]. Furthermore, Rodes et al. showed an increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding in DAPT compared with SAPT [13]. Other RCTs comparing SAPT vs. DAPT showed an increased risk of bleeding (major and life-threatening) in patients on DAPT after undergoing the TAVR procedure [39]. In another RCT by Dangas et al., patients on rivaroxaban were found to have an increased risk of major and/or life-threatening bleeding after TAVR compared with antiplatelet therapy [41]. In the context of TAVR, it could be postulated that the risk of thromboembolism is high before the complete endothelization of the prosthesis frame, which would advocate the use of DAPT for at least the first 3 months after implantation. However, multiple RCTs have failed to demonstrate a statistically significant benefit in reducing ischemic events and increasing the risk of bleeding. RCT by Mieghem et al. compared edoxaban with VKA in patients with atrial fibrillation who underwent TAVR. The trial showed noninferiority of edoxaban to VKA for the composite primary outcome of major clinical adverse events (MACE). However, there was an increased incidence of major bleeding with edoxaban than with vitamin K antagonists [22]. Various meta-analyses on antithrombotic therapy's effectiveness and safety profile in patients undergoing TAVR have been conducted using observational and non-randomized studies. However, results from individual observational and non-randomized studies may not be reliable and may be skewed towards a preference for one over the other therapy because of the lack of randomization. Meta-analyses on randomized controlled trials would provide an important systematic tool in synthesizing evidence and an overall quantitative estimate derived from individual studies. However, because of the limited number of RCTs, only a few meta-analyses have been reported using only RCTs and presented in this article.

Our article focuses on the risk of GIB associated with antithrombotic therapy after TAVR. However, no meta-analysis of RCTs has been conducted until now that has provided any information on this issue.

Predictors and Risk Factors for GIB Following TAVR

4.1

The Society of Thoracic Surgeons score (STS) and European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE) were initially developed for predicting operative mortality in cardiac surgery. These scores are often utilized when risk stratifying patients undergoing TAVR. A retrospective analysis determined that STS may be used to predict 30-day mortality in patients undergoing TAVR [47]. While both the STS score and EuroSCORE have been used as predictors for operative mortality in patients undergoing TAVR, multiple studies have demonstrated that an elevated STS score and Logistic EuroSCORE are not independent risk factors for major bleeding complications [3, 48]. To our knowledge, no comprehensive score has proven clinically significant for predicting GIB in patients undergoing TAVR. Therefore, our review focuses on an analysis of individual study data.

A study was performed by Stanger et al. that identified the incidence of Upper GIB in patients in the period following TAVR [8]. This study was a single-center retrospective evaluation of 841 adult patients at a Canadian tertiary center who underwent TAVR between January 2005 and August 2014. This study concluded that patients on triple therapy with antithrombotic agents were at ten times greater risk of upper GIB than those without triple therapy. The study also showed that following TAVR, distal esophageal and gastroesophageal ulcerations were the most common endoscopic findings in patients undergoing TAVR. However, the retrospective and observational research could not compare different patient cohorts or identify baseline patient characteristics in patients who experienced GIB following TAVR (Supplementary Table 2).

Another retrospective multicenter study was performed by Genereux et al. that evaluated the predictors, incidence, and mortality of patients who underwent TAVR and experienced MLBCs >30 days post-TAVR [3]. This study evaluated 2,401 patients who were part of the PARTNER-1 randomized cohort and continued access registry who underwent TAVR and survived at least 30 days. This study concluded that GIB is the most common MLCB following TAVR. Importantly, it also identified atrial fibrillation/flutter, moderate-to-severe paravalvular leak, and baseline hemoglobin of 11.53 +/- 1.41 as multivariate predictors of late bleeding complications following TAVR. This study was strengthened by its large, multicenter patient population and its use of echocardiographic findings such as paravalvular leak to form associations with clinical results such as major bleeding complications. This study's primary limitation was not considering anticoagulation regimens for patients undergoing TAVR, such as taking warfarin before TAVR. Nevertheless, the authors were able to identify multiple specific patient predictors of GIB in patients undergoing TAVR that are useful for risk stratification (Supplementary Table 2).

A prospective study followed 926 patients with severe aortic stenosis who underwent transapical TAVR between 2007 and 2014 at Bern, Switzerland's single tertiary care center [5]. The primary outcome of this study was all-cause mortality up to 5 years post-TAVR. Of the 926 patients who underwent TAVR, 30.7% experienced at least one bleeding event in the 5-year follow-up period. Non-access site bleeding was associated with a higher risk of mortality, and the GI tract was the most common site of non-access site bleeding. Patients more likely to experience non-access site bleeding had chronic kidney disease and a higher Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Score. Its power was limited during this study as its population was based at a single tertiary care center. Given the limited number of patients and proceduralists at this center, it may be problematic to extrapolate the worse prognosis for non-access site bleeding to other patient populations (Supplementary Table 2).

Primary hemostasis disorders are known to be associated with GIB in patients with aortic stenosis. This phenomenon, known as Heyde's syndrome, is considered due to increased circulatory shear forces associated with severe aortic stenosis and its effect on the cleavage of acquired high molecular weight Von-Willebrand factor multimers. Heyde's syndrome, the incidence of which is estimated to be around 3%, often occurs in patients with gastrointestinal angiodysplasia and may lead to GIB [49, 50]. A single-center prospective study in Strasbourg, France, followed 372 patients who underwent TAVR and survived at least 30 days post-procedure [14]. Each patient was administered aspirin (75 - 160 mg) and clopidogrel (300mg loading dose and 75mg daily maintenance) during the procedure and three months following TAVR. Of these patients, 42 experienced late MLBCs, of which GIB was the most common etiology. The authors found that CT-ADP level, a surrogate marker for von-Willebrand multimer lysis, was associated with an increased risk of MLBCS. A CT-ADP level of >180s and 24 hours post-TAVR, in addition to paravalvular leak, were both independent predictors for MLBCs. In addition, stroke history, a BMI of 27.2 +/- 5.7, and bleeding history are predictors of MLBCs. The authors emphasize using colonoscopy and gastroscopy as screening modalities in patients undergoing TAVR. This study was limited by its small patient population, lack of patient anticoagulation regimen recording, and use of a surrogate marker for high molecular weight Von-Willebrand Factor (Supplementary Table 2).

One of the main risk factors for bleeding following TAVR is the choice of antithrombotic therapy, as seen in all previous studies. An open-label randomized clinical trial evaluated 222 patients undergoing TAVR at nine centers in Europe, Canada, and South America [13]. Patients were administered aspirin or clopidogrel post-TAVR or aspirin alone and followed for three months. The primary endpoints were death, MI, stroke, or major bleeding, including GIB. Aspirin was started at least 24 hours prior to TAVR, and clopidogrel was started within 24 hours before TAVR. At the end of 3 months, there were 5 GIB events. All GIB events occurred in patients in the DAPT arm of the trial within 30 days of the procedure. While its small enrollment size mainly limited this trial, it demonstrated that patients on a single antiplatelet might benefit from having less bleeding (Supplementary Table 2).

Treatment of GIB after TAVR

4.2

As mentioned above, the literature has established that antithrombotic therapy is an independent risk factor for UGIB [51-53]. TEE is also often used during TAVR to visualize valve placement, which carries a small risk of UGIB [54]. When administered with antiplatelet and anticoagulant agents, proton pump inhibitors reduce the risk of gastrointestinal hemorrhage [55, 56]. There is clear evidence of this benefit, with several studies mentioning findings of lower rates of gastrointestinal bleeding in patients receiving aspirin [57], dual antiplatelet therapy [58], and long-term anticoagulation therapy with warfarin [59] and NOACs [56]. However, the combined use of PPI with DAPT reduced anti-platelet efficacy [60] and increased the risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) [61, 62]. Long-term GI prophylaxis also has side effects. Hence, concurrent prescription of PPI, and anti-thrombotic therapy has been discouraged in most patients. Therefore, bleeding complications are most likely treated on a case-to-case basis as primary prevention is often out of scope.

Proton Pump Inhibitors

4.3

Aspirin can cause direct mucosal damage to the gastric lining and decrease prostaglandin production, increasing the chances of GIB [63]. While clopidogrel is not ulcerogenic, it may promote bleeding at sites with preexisting ulcers [64]. Anticoagulants also increase the incidence of GIB by similar mechanisms. Acid suppression is of prime importance in patients with active GIB. High-dose anti-secretory IV infusion of PPI decreases the rate of rebleeding [65]. H2 blockers are not as efficacious [66]. This has further been described in detail by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for managing DAPT and AC-related bleeding complications [67]. The guidelines universally support consideration for IV PPI therapy for the upper GIB. A recent study on patients with gastrointestinal bleeding in aspirin therapy treated with endoscopic hemostasis and PPI found a higher incidence of rebleeding in patients who continued aspirin after GIB but lower mortality [68].

Endoscopy and Endotherapy

4.4

There is strong evidence to perform upper endoscopy within 24 hours for most patients with upper GIB after adequate resuscitation [69, 70]. Early endoscopy is associated with lower rebleeding risk and mortality. At the same time, patients who have received TAVR have many comorbidities that put them at higher risk for any procedure; adequate resuscitation and stabilization of hemodynamics improve mortality in this group of patients. There is limited comparative evidence to choose between endoscopic techniques. The usual treatment is dual therapy with a combination of large volume (10-20ml) epinephrine (1:10,000) and thermal therapy with a heater probe or insertion of clips. Usually, endoscopy is combined with peri-procedure IV PPI therapy to reduce the risk of rebleeding and mortality. A randomized trial of 156 patients of IV omeprazole with or without endotherapy found that the combined endotherapy and PPI groups had reduced the risk of rebleeding by 11% compared to the group receiving PPI alone [71]. Hence, endoscopic intervention and PPI use are independently associated with decreased mortality in patients with bleeding peptic ulcers due to anti-thrombotic therapy.

Cessation of Antithrombotic Therapy

4.5

As mentioned above, treating GIB in the setting of antithrombotic therapy in patients with TAVR is complex as treatment depends on balancing the risk of thrombotic complications and the risks of rebleeding. Current guidelines are vague and often have either a GI or a cardiovascular focus, given that gastroenterologists and cardiologists often address the topic. An article by the British Society of Gastroenterology [72] suggested that acute GIB in anticoagulant or antiplatelet is a high-risk situation that requires early endoscopic evaluation with minimal or no cessation of anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy. The article states that endoscopic therapy should be performed with discontinuation of warfarin and substitution with heparin in patients treated with warfarin. Recommendations from the American College of Gastroenterology [73] state that when GIB occurs, low-dose ASA should be resumed in most patients as soon as possible after bleeding ceases (ideally within 1-3 days, and certainly within 7 days), and long-term PPI therapy should be prescribed in these situations. No commentary on clopidogrel, dual antiplatelet therapy, or anticoagulation is made, and the continued use is controversial. European Society of Cardiology guidelines comment on the management of GIB in the setting of acute coronary syndromes but not specifically for TAVR [74]. It suggests that major bleeding events such as GI, retroperitoneal, intracranial, or other severe blood loss events require interruption of anticoagulant and antiplatelet treatment if appropriate interventions cannot control bleeding. Under such conditions, neither antithrombotic therapies should be reintroduced until strict hemorrhage control is achieved for at least 24 hours. However, if complete control of hemorrhage is achieved, interruption of treatment might not be necessary. Therefore, none of the major society guidelines provide concrete recommendations for managing acute, life-threatening GIB. While there is a consensus to temporarily interrupt the antithrombotic therapy. There is no clear advice about proceeding after an acute bleeding episode. This further complicates patient care in an emergent setting. Therefore, we recommend a comprehensive evaluation of patients by cardiovascular and gastroenterology teams and formulating an individualized therapeutic plan on a case-to-case basis.

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION

GIB has been significantly associated with TAVR, and it is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Triple therapy has been associated with the highest risk of bleeding post TAVR and should be avoided in high bleeding risk patients. No accurate risk score calculator is available to stratify patients at a higher risk of GIB. After an episode of GIB, appropriate guideline-directed management should be offered to the patient based on their bleeding risk. The decision to stop or continue antithrombotic therapy needs to be individualized on a case-to-case basis since it depends on the risk of thrombotic complications and the severity of rebleeding guided by the severity of bleeding.
