Methodological parameters for upper airway assessment by cone-beam computed tomography in adults with obstructive sleep apnea: a systematic review of the literature and meta-analysis

Abstract

Background

A reliable method for analyzing the upper airway (UA) remains a challenge. This study aimed to report the methods for UA assessment using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) in adults with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).

Methods

We performed a systematic review (PROSPERO #CRD42021237490 and PRISMA checklist) that applied a search strategy to seven databases and grey literature.

Results

In 29 studies with moderate-to-high risk of bias, investigators mostly reported the body position during CBCT (upright or supine) and hard tissue references, diverging in UA delimitation and terminologies. The meta-analysis showed two subgroups (upright and supine), and no statistical differences were identified (p = 0.18) considering the UA area. The volume in the OSA group was smaller than that in the control group (p < 0.003 and Cohen’s d = −0.81) in the upright position. Patients with OSA showed smaller anteroposterior dimensions than the control group and were not affected by the position during image acquisition (p = 0.02; Cohen’s d = −0.52). The lateral measurements were also lower in the OSA group (supine) (p = 0.002; Cohen’s d = −0.6).

Conclusions

Patients with OSA showed smaller UA measurements in the upright (volume) and supine (lateral dimension) positions. The anteroposterior dimension was also reduced in patients with OSA compared to the control group, regardless of the position during CBCT acquisition.

Introduction

Sleep breathing disorders are a complex of abnormal respiratory conditions that affect the quality of life of the population. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is the most prevalent disturbance in the spectrum of sleep breathing disorders and is diagnosed in approximately 936 million individuals worldwide. Partial or total airflow obstruction in the upper airway (UA), which is characteristic of this disorder, may lead to recurrent and abrupt awakening during the night and oxygen desaturation [1-3].

Due to constant sleep interruption, OSA patients may experience non-restorative sleep, demonstrating symptoms such as excessive daytime sleepiness, neurocognitive and social interaction alterations, irritability, anxiety, and depression. Moreover, variations in oxy-hemoglobin saturation may lead to stroke, coronary artery disease, alterations in blood pressure, and arrhythmias, which are associated with high mortality and risk of developing cardiovascular diseases [2, 4].

Due to the important clinical manifestations of OSA, an appropriate diagnosis is required using polysomnographic examination (PSG) as a standard diagnostic tool. This exam evaluates several parameters: brain activity (electroencephalogram), muscle activity (electromyogram and periodic limb movements), sleep pattern (sleep efficiency, total sleep, wake after sleep onset, sleep onset, and latency time), rapid eye movements (REM and electrooculogram), sleep stages (N1, N2, N3, REM), arousal index (AI), respiratory disturbance index (RDI), oxygen desaturation index (ODI), mean and minimum oxyhemoglobin pulse saturation (SpO2), and apnea hypopnea index (AHI) (frequency of apnea/hypopnea events per hour of sleep). SpO2 and AHI were mainly used to confirm OSA diagnosis. The AHI cutoff references and threshold classify OSA diagnostic (AHI ≥ 5) at three levels: mild (AHI = 5–15 events/hour), moderate (AHI = 15–30 events/hour), and severe (AHI ≥ 30 events/hour) [4, 5].

UA collapse and patency are essential factors in the OSA etiology and are influenced by the neurologic system, sex, weight, muscle activity, craniofacial anatomy, and genetic factors. Despite the UA role in OSA development, the dynamic mechanisms involving airflow and UA permeability are unclear. Thus, the UA, OSA, and several possible associated anatomic factors have been explored by the scientific community [6-8].

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is a three-dimensional (3D) image exam used to evaluate craniofacial aspects and UA anatomy with great precision. Despite the widespread adoption of helicoidal computed tomography (CT), CBCT acquisition presents less X-ray exposure and more accurate images. Although OSA may not be diagnosed by CBCT, this examination is important in studying several anatomic factors that play an essential role for clinicians and researchers in evaluating the etiology, severity, and prognosis of the disorder. Furthermore, image technology advancements have provided different software for image analysis that enables study of the UA in all dimensional axes using different measurements such as volume, surface area, cross-sectional area, angles, and shape. The anatomic variations and image technology developments produce several possibilities for UA evaluation [6-8].

Therefore, it is a challenge for researchers to choose a uniform method to analyze the UA of patients with OSA and compare results with other authors in a reliable approach, especially considering the patient’s position, software for image analysis, UA measurements, terminology, references, subdivisions, and role of the respiratory cycle during imaging acquisition. Furthermore, this variability may affect the credibility of the studies, confounding the applicability of the scientific outcomes in clinical practice. The present study aimed to report, with a systematic review and meta-analysis, the methodological parameters for upper airway assessment by CBCT in adults with obstructive sleep apnea. Specifically, we hypothesized that UA measurements may be influenced by divergences in the position of the patient during the CBCT acquisition process.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

The present study was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [9]. The abstract was based on the abstract and search checklist extensions of PRISMA 2020 This systematic review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42021237490. The database search strategy was applied on February 17, 2021 after submission to PROSPERO.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria adopted in this systematic review were clinical trials or observational studies that evaluated the upper airway of adults with OSA using CBCT. Reviews, letters to the editor, personal opinions of authors, book chapters, abstracts from scientific events, studies conducted in children and adolescents, computed tomographic imaging modalities other than CBCT, studies without a polysomnographic and/or OSA diagnosis, animal studies, studies without upper airway evaluation, studies not written in the Latin (Roman) alphabet, studies on cadavers, case reports, and studies with syndromes associated with OSA were excluded.

Information sources and methods

The following virtual health databases and grey literature were searched and accessed as follows:

  1. PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/—supported by the National Center for Biotechnology Information [NCBI]).

  2. Scopus (https://www.scopus.com/—provided by Elsevier and accessed by the Federal University of Ceara Library).

  3. Web of Science (www.webofknowledge.com—maintained by Clarivate Analytics).

  4. COCHRANE (www.cochrane.org).

  5. LILACS (https://lilacs.bvsalud.org/en/—Latin American and Caribbean Health Science Literature).

  6. DOSS (https://www.ebsco.com/—Dentistry & Oral Sciences Source from EBSCO Information Services).

  7. EMBASE (http://www.elsevier.com/online-tools/embase/—Excerpta Medica dataBASE produced by Elsevier, Netherlands).

  8. Google Scholar (https://scholar.google.com/).

  9. ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global (https://about.proquest.com).

  10. OpenGrey (http://www.opengrey.eu/—System for Information on Grey Literature in Europe).

After identification and screening, the references of the included articles were searched to identify additional studies in this systematic review.

Search strategy

To better delimitate the study keywords, the clearly framed question “What are the methodological parameters for upper airway assessment by CBCT in adults with OSA?” was elaborated, guiding the definition of the population, exposure, comparison, outcomes, and studies (PECOS). Thus, this review considers the following characterization to perform the search strategy:

  • Population (P): adults with OSA

  • Exposure (E): upper airway data assessed by CBCT

  • Control (C): comparison group

  • Outcomes (O): methodological parameters of image assessment and secondary data of the studies

  • Studies (S): clinical trials or observational studies.

The PubMed database was first accessed to identify the Medical Subject Heading Terms (MeSH Terms) and develop the algorithm for all database searches. The MeSH terms “Sleep Apnea, Obstructive,” “Tomography, X-Ray Computed,” and “Tomography” were selected and combined using the Boolean operators “OR” and “AND.” Filters were applied only to ProQuest search.

The algorithm was adapted for each platform and was applied simultaneously to all databases without date limitation (Table 1).

Selection process

Peer review

The selection of potential studies was performed independently by two authors (M.L.G. and F.S.R.C.). A third author (F.W.G.C.) supervised the process and made the final decision regarding possible divergences.

Managing records

The data initially included were automatically deduplicated with Endnote® software, using the “Find duplicates” tool (EndNote®, Thomson Reuters, Philadelphia, PA, USA). After the automatic removal of duplicates, all the remaining titles and abstracts were independently evaluated and classified using the tools “include” and “exclude” from Rayyan® software (Rayyan® Qatar Computing Research Institute, Doha, Qatar) [10]. The studies included after the Rayyan classification were independently read in full by the same reviewers, and the inclusion criteria were also applied to the full texts.

Data collection process

All descriptive and quantitative data from the selected studies were manually extracted and categorized in spreadsheets by a single author (M.L.G.) using Microsoft Excel. The quantitative analysis was obtained as mean and standard deviation, and the data expressed in box plots were extracted using the WebPlotDigitizer—Copyright 2010/2020 https://apps.automeris.io/wpd/). The metric measurements, such as linear distance, area, and volume, were expressed in millimeters (mm), square millimeters (mm2), and cubic millimeters (mm3), respectively. All numeric values given by the studies in centimeters, square, or cubic were transformed in mm, mm2, or mm3. The velocity reports were expressed in meters per second (m/s). The resistance was given in Pascals per cubic centimeter per second (Pa/cm3/s), while tension and pressure were given in Pa.

Data items

The relevant data items to be exported were delimitated by two authors (F.W.G.C. and C.M.C.J.) with expertise in this study. The following variables were considered as follows: study design, sample, type of intervention, sex, age, body mass index (BMI), CBCT acquisition set, upper airway delimitations, upper airway measurements, examiners, reliability, polysomnographic data, and main outcomes.

Study risk of bias assessment

To assess the risk of bias (RoB) of the included studies, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT)—version 2018 (http://mixedmethodsappraisaltoolpublic.pbworks.com/)—was adapted for the present study and used as the Meta-Analysis of Assessment and Review Instrument [10-12]. The RoB judgment was based on the MMAT screening questions from item 3:

3. Quantitative non-randomized studies: case–control and cohort studies.

  • 3.1. Are the participants representative of the target population?

  • 3.2. Are measurements appropriate regarding both the outcome and intervention (or exposure)?

  • 3.3. Are there complete outcome data?

  • 3.4. Are the confounders accounted for in the design and analysis?

  • 3.5. During the study period, is the intervention administered (or exposure occurring) as intended?

The MMAT responses were categorized as yes, no, or cannot tell and were quantified in four levels of evidence according to the percentage of meeting the MMAT criteria (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%). To generate a visual graphic of the risk of bias, the RevMan software (Review Manager, software version 5.4.1, Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) was used with an adaptation to the MMAT-version 2018 questions. The MMAT responses yes, no, or cannot tell were interpreted as low, high, and unclear risk, respectively, in the RevMan RoB table evaluation.

Effect measures

Since different measures were evaluated, quantitative data were analyzed using standardized mean difference (SMD) ± standard deviation (SD).

Synthesis methods

A meta-analysis was performed using the RevMan software (Cochrane Collaboration), adopting a confidence level of 95% and a random model. I2 and Tau2 statistics were used to evaluate the heterogeneity. The meta-analysis included case–control studies with the same variables in the case and control groups. These studies were analyzed in subgroups with forest plots expressing SMD, random effects, and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The SMD was performed to minimize the heterogenicity of the different method scales (area, volume, anteroposterior, and lateral linear distances) and obtain the magnitude effect expressed by Cohen’s d value. The funnel plot assessed publication bias, considering the significance level (p < 0.05). A sensitivity analysis was performed by removing studies one by one to verify individual interference of each research in the final result of the meta-analysis.

Reporting bias assessment

The quality of evidence was evaluated regarding the study design, sample size, calculation risk of bias, consistency, objectivity, population heterogeneity, precision, reliability, study power, statistical analysis, conflict of interest, and other relevant aspects.

Results

Study selection

The initial search in the electronic databases identified 5844 studies, while the search in the grey literature found 143 articles. After removing the duplicated data, two reviewers independently screened 3038 titles and abstracts from the main databases and 137 from grey literature; the remaining 196 studies were read in full by the authors. The critical analysis in this phase excluded 166 studies. Therefore, 29 studies were included in data synthesis (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

Sample

All included records were observational studies (13 [45%] case–control/comparative studies and 16 [55%] cohort studies). The majority of articles were published in the United States (US) (10 [34%]) (Fig. 2). A total of 1201 patients with OSA and controls were evaluated three-dimensionally by the authors. Bimaxillary orthognathic advancement surgery, distraction osteogenesis maxillary expansion, mandibular advancement devices, and titration of adjustable mandibular positioning gauge represented the main interventions applied by the authors. Seven hundred and fifty-six adults composed the OSA population, with a mean age of 53 ± 5 years, ranging from 19 to 80 years, and a BMI of 29.5 ± 0.7. There were 506 (67%) males and 197 (26%) females, and 50 (7%) individuals without this information. Considering the case–control studies, the mean age of the control patients was 41 ± 7 years, ranging from 25 to 72 years, with a BMI of 25 ± 2. The control group comprised 92 (54%) males and 49 (29%) females, and the sex of 30 (18%) control patients was not reported. Furthermore, the PSG data reported were AHI, RDI, ODI, AI, REM sleep, NREM sleep, Min SpO2, and Mean SpO2, and eight (28%) studies did not report PSG parameters, although it had been mentioned as an OSA diagnosis method. Only three (10%) articles described the sample size calculations (Table 2).

CBCT evaluation

Among the studies, 17 (59%) fully described the image acquisition process. The most frequently reported patient position was upright (14, 48%), followed by supine (8, 28%). The other 6 (20%) studies did not report the position during the image acquisition, as the data were entered after searching for the characteristics informed by the equipment platforms. Thus, other studies (two [7%] for supine and four [14%] for upright positions) required for acquisition were identified. The most reported reference planes to perform CBCT were the Frankfurt plane (FP), perpendicular to the floor in the supine position, and parallel to the floor in upright CBCT. In addition, the natural head position was used as a reference for the upright tomographic acquisition. Seven studies (24%) did not report this information. Twenty software applications were used for image analysis: Amira, Torrance, Dolphin, Vworks, 3dMDVultus, MIMICS-Materialise Interactive, Invivo5, Analyze, Dental Slice®, the CS 3D Imaging Software, OnDemand3DApp, Romexis, ANSYS ICEM CFD 17.0, ANSYS Fluent, Maxilim, InVivoDental, 3D Slicer, INTAGE Volume Editor, ITK-Snap, and the CS 3D Imaging Software (Fig. 3). The Dolphin software was the most commonly reported (n = 20.6%). The reliability and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of CBCT measurements were described in 14 (48.2%) and 11 (37.9%) studies, respectively (Table 3).

Upper airway evaluation

The CBCT instructions regarding UA anatomy stability were found in 14 (48%) of the studies, while the image orientation and registration (for cohort studies) were reported in 8 (28%) and 2 (13%) articles, respectively. The UA evaluation methods diverged among studies. The UA and subdivisions were denominated by 14 different terms: superior UA, inferior UA, UA, nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, retropalatal space, retroglossal space, superior oropharynx, inferior oropharynx, velopharynx, glossopharynx, laryngopharynx, intraoral airway, and pharyngeal airway. Twelve studies (42%) used only hard tissue references for UA delimitation, while 9 (31%) used hard and soft tissue delimitation. Nine different hard tissue structures were described as follows: hard palate, posterior nasal spine (PNS), basion (Ba), most anterior and/or inferior portion of the second cervical vertebra (AIC2), most anterior and/or inferior portion of the third cervical vertebra (AIC3), hyoid bone, retrognathion point (RGn), B point (B), and mental point (Me). The soft tissue references were composed of the tip of the uvula, tip of the epiglottis, base of the epiglottis, and base of the tongue. The authors also reported the Frankfurt plane, palatal plane, and occlusal plane as references to UA delimitation. Volumetric, linear (axial, sagittal, and coronal), angular, shape, and uniformity measurements were performed. The area was reported as UA axial, sagittal, minimum, maximum, or mean cross-sectional area in different UA subregions. Using computational simulations, the crucial role of the respiratory cycle during imaging acquisition was only assessed in three studies. Two authors reported velocity, and one study evaluated resistance, pressure, and UA wall stress (Table 4).

Risk of bias in studies

The RoB assessment by the RevMan software evaluation is shown in Fig. 4 by a graph expressing each risk of bias item, presented as percentages across all included studies. Moreover, the RoB according to MMAT is expressed in Table 5. These analyses identified 8 studies with high risk (*25%/**50%), 17 with moderate risk (***75%), and 4 with low risk (****100%).

Results of individual studies

Each study was analyzed considering simple size calculation, description of the method, confounding factors such as reliability, patient positions during CBCT acquisition, instructions, image orientation, registration, matched samples, and standardization. The individual RoB of included studies is reported in Table 5 and summarized in Fig. 5.

Results of syntheses

UA area did not differ between OSA and control groups

The meta-analysis of area measurements evaluated 322 cases and 190 controls, and it did not show a statistically significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.18). The effect size was medium (Cohen’s d = −0.6 [CI95% = −1.47 to 0.27]). There was significant heterogeneity (Tau2 = 1.47, I2 = 94%, p < 0.001). The sensitivity analysis showed that individual removal of the study by Bruiwer et al. (2016) [11] significantly interfered in these results, leading to a smaller area in OSA group with a large size effect (p = 0.05, Cohen’s d = −0.89 [CI95% = −1.62 to −0.16]).

According to the CBCT acquisition positions, two patient subgroups were evaluated: upright and supine. In patients in an upright position, no differences were identified between the OSA and control groups (p = 0.14), and a significantly large effect size was found (Cohen’s d = −0.96 [CI95% = −2.22 to 0.30]. There was no heterogeneity between these studies (Tau2 = 1.11, I2 = 89%, p < 0.001), and the sensitivity analysis showed that the individual removal of the studies did not significantly change this outcome (p < 0.05). In the supine position, there was no significant difference between the OSA and control groups (p = 0.52). Heterogeneity between the studies (Tau2 = 1.77, I2 = 96%, p < 0.001) was considerably high. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that individual removal from the studies did not significantly change this outcome (p > 0.05) (Fig. 6).

OSA patients showed smaller UA volume in the upright but not in the supine position, compared to the control group

The meta-analysis of UA volume evaluated 214 OSA cases and 195 controls, showing a statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.005). The effect size was medium, with Cohen’s d = −0.53 (CI95% = −0.91 to −0.16). There was moderate heterogeneity between the studies (Tau2 = 0.22, I2 = 69%, p < 0.001), and the sensitivity analysis demonstrated that individual removal of the studies did not significantly change this outcome (p < 0.05).

Regarding the position during CBCT acquisition in patients positioned upright, the OSA group showed a significant smaller volume (p = 0.003) and a Cohen’s d = −0.81 [CI95% = −1.25 to −0.36] with a large effect size. The heterogeneity between these studies was moderate (Tau2 = 0.19, I2 = 63%, p = 0.02), and the sensitivity analysis showed that the individual removal of the studies did not significantly change this outcome (p < 0.05). In the supine position, there was no significant difference between the OSA and control groups (p = 0.92) considering the volume, and there was no significant heterogeneity between the studies (Tau2 = 0.00, I2 = 0%, p = 0.51). Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that individual removal of the studies did not significantly change this outcome (p > 0.05) (Fig. 6).

OSA patients showed smaller AP dimension than the control group not interfered by position

The meta-analysis of anteroposterior (AP) linear measurements evaluated 216 case samples and 117 control samples, showing a significant difference between the two groups (p=0.02). The effect size was medium (Cohen’s d = −0.52 [CI95% = −0.95 to −0.10]), and there was a significant moderate heterogeneity between the studies (Tau2=0.17, I2=63%, p=0.02). The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the individual removal of the studies significantly changed this outcome, and after removing the Ogawa et al. (2007)12 study, no differences were identified between the groups (p=0.05). Comparing upright and supine subgroups, no difference in the AP axial linear measurements was identified (p> 0.05) (Fig. 7).

OSA patients showed smaller LAT dimensions in the supine position, but not in the upright position, compared to the control group

Analysis of the lateral (LAT) linear measurements showed that 216 OSA and 117 control patients did not show a statistically significant difference (p=0.39). There was significant heterogeneity between the groups (Tau2=0.93, I2 = 90%, p < 0.001) with a small effect size (Cohen’s d = −0.36 [CI95% = −1.18 to −0.46]). In the upright subgroup, similar results were found, while in the supine group, a significant difference was found (p = 0.002), which was associated with a significant moderate heterogeneity (Tau2 = 0.00, I2 = 69%, p = 0.69) and with a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = −0.60 [CI95% = −0.98 to −0.21]). In the sensitivity analysis, the individual removal of studies did not significantly change this outcome (p > 0.05) (Fig. 7).

Publication bias

According to the funnel plot analysis, no publication bias was identified in the studies included in the syntheses for area, volume, and AP and LAT linear measurements (Figs. 6 and 7).

Reporting biases

Among the 13 case–control studies, 4 papers to be excluded from the meta-analysis due to the absence of all measurements in the OSA and control groups, considerable differences in measurements, or unhealthy control groups. Of the nine remaining studies evaluated by meta-analysis, four did not present an equal sample in the OSA and control groups.

Discussion

In this systematic review, scientific databases were searched to access tomographic three-dimensional upper airway evaluation methods in adult patients with obstructive sleep apnea polysomnographic diagnosis. The upper airway anatomy is a complex structure influenced by multiple factors such as muscle activity, gravity, weight, posture, breathing, and swallowing movements; the evaluation of methods that minimize the external influence on upper airway patency is essential for accurate analysis, diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis [7, 8, 13]. Although this dynamic characteristic represents an important aspect of upper airway assessment, this systematic review found that the literature is still unclear in reporting standardized airway assessment methods [7, 8]. The present study identified 29 observational studies that applied different upper airway evaluations regarding several aspects, such as CBCT equipment, CBCT acquisition, patient position, and instructions. Furthermore, the image software, processing, reliability, delimitation, nomenclature, and three-dimensional evaluations also diverged among the authors, indicating that there is no agreement or standardization for an accurate study of obstructive sleep apnea in the upper airway of adult patients.

Among the studies included in this synthesis of results, most of the publications are from the US, which may be justified by the prevalence of OSA in the country. According to Benjafiled et al. (2019)[1], the US is among the top ten countries with the highest number of individuals with OSA, with an OSA prevalence (AHI ≥ 5) of 33%. Although Brazil, Germany, Japan, China, and India are also among the top ten OSA rankings, publications from these countries only represented 3% to 10% of the studies included in this systematic review. The sex predominance found in this screening agreed with the literature, being more prevalent in men (67%) than in women (26%). The BMI (29.5 ± 0.7) of patients with OSA was not in accordance with previous studies that identified higher BMI values in the OSA population [1, 13, 14]. However, this report may be explained by the study eligibility criteria, which mostly excludes overweight patients to minimize the risk of bias. Most studies included in this paper did not report the sample size calculation or ethnicity, which is essential information to a proper statistical power and to validate the proposed hypotheses. Thus, the population reported by 90% of the studies may not accurately represent the OSA population [15].

Only 59% of the studies fully described the complete position method during CBCT acquisition, and the authors reported these steps differently. The upright position was found in 52% of the studies, while the supine position was found in 24%. Reference planes to perform CBCT have also been reported. For the supine position, the Frankfurt plane (FP) perpendicular to the floor was described as a reference. The Frankfurt plane parallel to the floor and natural head position were described for the upright position. Once the UA may dynamically be deformed with positional changes, sleep stages, and respiratory cycle, all these steps during CBCT acquisition are important to achieve a three-dimensional image with maximum accuracy, representing as much as possible the real UA characteristics of OSA patients [7, 8]. However, 24.1% of the included studies did not report all steps for CBCT acquisition. According to Sousa et al., (2016) [16], comparing CT images obtained in supine (Frankfurt plane perpendicular to the floor) and inclined supine (44° upward inclination) positions, the UA volumes were different, being greater with head inclination [16]. Hsu et al. (2019) [17] analyzed sagittal UP dimensions in the supine and upright positions. The authors identified that body position changes could influence the anterior–posterior distance of most constricted areas in the UA and hyoid bone. Owing to patient position and posture relevance, applying and reporting these details during CBCT acquisition is extremely important for adequate UA analysis, study reproducibility, and evidence quality.

Although the most commonly reported software is Dolphin Imaging, with accuracy and validation well elucidated, several authors diverged from the image software analysis. A total of 19 other systems were mentioned, with 62% commercial and 31% free access [6, 18]. The accuracy of the Romexis (free access and semi-automated) and Invivo5 (commercial and automated) software was demonstrated in a study by Kamaruddin et al. (2019) [18], which showed precision in reproducing the UA volume and minimum cross-sectional area. Amira and OnDemand3D also provided reliable UA volumetric, linear, and area measurements [19]. ITK Snap and 3D slicer are free access programs and are considered accurate software, especially for anatomic irregular volume and area measurements. Moreover, Gomes et al. (2019) [20], after analyzing a mathematical model, demonstrated that semi-automatic segmentations by ITK Snap showed great accuracy for irregular structures [21]. Although the software commonly reported in the included studies have demonstrated acceptable accuracy, more studies comparing different image applications are still unclear and need to be critically considered when determining the software of choice for UA evaluations and reliability of CBCT-generated 3D models [53]. The development of precise free applications is an important step for further studies to elucidate the complexity of UA patency in OSA.

The UA assessment differed among the articles regarding image acquisition instructions, image orientation, registration, UA denomination, and measurements. Only 48% of the studies considered instructions during CBCT to maintain the UA as stably as possible. Considering that the airflow is dynamic, with muscle action during inspiratory, expiratory, and swallowing movements, and that CBTC is a static image exam, the instruction to avoid profound respiratory movements, to not move, to not swallow, and to keep the gaze fixed at a static point during image acquisition is essential. However, this has not been described by several authors (52%) [7, 8]. In addition, only three authors reported imaging modalities considering the respiratory cycle, showing velocity, resistance, pressure, and UA wall stress data. Ideally, for meaningful clinical outcomes, at least two cycle phases should be evaluated [54]. However, the number of cycle phases has not been reported in these studies. Another relevant image factor is the head orientation and registration, reported in only 28% and 13% of the analyzed papers, respectively.

The 3D image evaluations express different anterior, posterior, superior, and lateral measurements depending on the space axes (x, y, and z). In the head orientation, these space planes are quantified, placed, and standardized in the same coordinate arrangement for all the images using craniofacial structures as anatomic reference, minimizing possible inconsistencies during the image analysis from different individuals. Moreover, images from cohort studies were obtained with more than one CBCT acquisition at different follow-up times. Because of the possible variances among head positions in this step, the baseline image from longitudinal designs should be oriented according to coordinate planes, and the follow-up 3D images should ideally be registered according to the oriented baseline image. The registration process for cohort studies is paramount when comparing anatomy variables in more than one-time point. Head orientation and registration are critical processes for obtaining dimensional outcomes with accuracy, precision, and reproducibility [22].

This systematic review showed no agreement among the authors regarding the UA terminology and subdivision references, identifying 14 UA terms and different soft and hard tissue references. The same airway region, from the hard palate to the epiglottis, was reported by the authors with four nomenclatures: upper airway, oropharynx, pharyngeal airway, and velopharynx. The nasopharynx and oropharynx were described with three and eight different delimitations, respectively. Furthermore, the inferior UA limit was reported by several structures such as the tip of the epiglottis, base of the epiglottis, Me, C2, C3, hyoid bone, and RGn, and may have been justified by the FOV size of the CBCT equipment used. Standardized UA terminology and delimitation are essential for a better anatomic understanding in the medical scenario, which is a factor to be considered in UA evaluations in OSA patients by CBTC. In addition, UA patency and OSA severity may be influenced not only by age, sex, or BMI. The tongue, soft palate, uvula, and epiglottis may also change the UA morphology during physiological neuromuscular activity, leading to a hard tissue choice for the most accurate evaluation [23]. UA denomination, delimitations, and subdivisions have been suggested according to the following description: nasopharynx (the posterior nasal cavity between the cranial base and hard palate), oropharynx (the region between the hard palate and hyoid bone), and hypopharynx (region above the hyoid bone) [24].

This study showed a moderate RoB in most of the articles (56.6%). This finding reflected the lack of information about sample size calculation, matched sample, CBCT instructions, image orientation/registration, and examiner reliability. Only one study, by Rodrigues et al. (2017) [25], met 100% of the MMAT criteria. A high RoB was shown in 30% of the studies representing UA 3D evaluation methods with low confidence in their results. These articles failed in several aspects such as sample size calculation, divergence between the patients with OSA and controls, reproducible measurement reports, clear definition of the evaluations, all CBCT parameters for image standardization, examiner reliability tests, and statistical outcomes. Importantly, only two articles were designed considering the minimization of confounding factors such as CBCT image acquisition posture and instructions for UA maintenance, image orientation and registration, and measurement reliability (ICC). All the described methodological parameters are essential to achieve an accurate, precise, reliable, and consistent outcome for UA analyses in patients with OSA using CBCT [15, 17, 22, 24, 26]. Based on the studies with a low RoB, this systematic review suggests steps for precise UA with CBCT in patients with OSA (Fig. 8).

Regarding the PSG data, the AHI, AI, RDI, ODI, Min SpO2, and Mean SpO2 were the parameters reported. Ten authors did not mention PSG data for the OSA diagnosis. Among the 20 studies that reported PSG, 4 did not mention the AHI; however, it is important to quantify the number of apnea and hypopnea obstructive events and diagnose and classify the disease. This outcome may be reflected in the non-standardized OSA sample reported by the authors. SpO2 is also a relevant parameter for OSA diagnosis. However, case–control studies did not report oxygen saturation [2-4].

The meta-analysis syntheses performed on the case–control studies identified that OSA patients showed a smaller volume than the control patients, while the UA area measurements did not differ between them. Interestingly, the subgroup analysis based on CBCT positions during image acquisition (supine and upright) showed that the volume measurements in the supine position did not differ between the groups. In contrast, these dimensions were significantly reduced in the upright OSA group. These findings highlight the role of body position during CBCT acquisition in UA measurements. Comparing OSA patients with the comparative group, the supine position in healthy patients may influence UA dimensions due to gravity forces in the pharyngeal muscles. Moreover, AP linear dimensions did not differ between the groups, while the LAT dimensions were smaller only in OSA individuals evaluated in the supine position, indicating the effects of posture, position, and gravity on UA anatomy. These outcomes suggest that the choice of CBCT equipment depends on the study to be performed. Case–control studies may show more bias with images acquired in the supine position, as control and OSA patients may demonstrate a different muscle response with gravity. Moreover, in studies involving patients with wakefulness, the supine position is not necessarily ideal once the sleeping conditions are not simulated [55]. However, cohort studies should preferably be performed in a position as similar as possible to the situation during sleep in which obstructive events occur, emphasizing the importance of CBCT scans performed not only in the supine position but also in the presence of sedation. The meta-analysis limitations by analyzing the groups and subgroups may be justified by the differences in the sample size and references for linear evaluations [8, 13, 23].

The current findings identified relevant aspects for a proper UA evaluation protocol, impacting the clinician’s decisions regarding CBTC upper airway analysis methods in adult patients diagnosed with OSA. Thus, clinicians should carefully consider all the steps for image acquisition, such as position during the CBCT examination, the anatomic references for the evaluations, and the importance of image orientation and registration. Although CBCT is considered an objective tool that provides anatomical and functional evaluation of OSA treatment outcomes [55], we highlight the importance of future studies investigating some aspects for the assessment of the UA in patients suspected with OSA, such as the utility of CBCT in the supine position and during sleep, the influence exerted by the cervical posture during imaging, and the effect of the respiratory cycle during CBCT acquisition, comparing groups with different standardized protocols in order to validate an accurate method. Furthermore, the studies’ divergences and the application of non-standardized methods reported in this study highlight the lack of studies with evidence of power quality.

Methodologically, the imbalance between OSA and comparative groups sample size and different methods used for CBCT assessment and PSG reports were potential limitations in this systematic review. Statistically, to minimize the issues related to the variety of methods for outcome assessment found in the included studies, we calculated the SMD by dividing the mean difference and the SD in each study. Thus, the advantage of this approach is the possibility of expressing the mean difference by units of SD, which permits their combination in a meta-analysis. Although SMD does not provide information in clinical units (e.g., square millimeters for area measurement), it provides information in statistical units. In addition, the SMD allows obtaining Cohen’s d (mean difference divided by the pooled SD), which is an important parameter for estimating the effect size [56].

Conclusions

The present systematic review identified that most of the methods used to analyze CBCT UA characteristics of adults with OSA were reported, predominantly considering the CBCT patient position during image acquisition and hard tissue references for upper airway delimitations. However, no standardized or consolidated methodological parameters have been found. The meta-analysis indicated that the divergence shown may interfere with the study outcomes, indicating the necessity of future well-designed investigations, such as case–control studies with standardized CBCT acquisition, PSG diagnosis, adequate image processing, UA delimitation, and evaluation, to provide better accuracy and reproducibility of CBCT measurements in the UA of patients with OSA.
