Substance-Related Cross-Commodity Purchase Tasks: A Systematic Review

Sarah C. Weinsztok[1]

Derek D. Reed

Michael Amlung

 

Cofrin Logan Center for Addiction Research and Treatment, Department of Applied Behavioral Science, University of Kansas

Author notes:

Correspondence to: [1] Correspondence concerning this article should be addressed to Sarah Weinsztok, Cofrin Logan Center for Addiction Research and Treatment, 1000 Sunnyside Ave, Lawrence, KS 66045, sweinsztok@ku.edu

Abstract

Objective:

In the context of behavioral economics, drug use is a choice to which an individual may allocate responding despite the presence of alternative response possibilities. To examine the demand for a drug in an environment in which other drugs or non-drug alternatives are present, researchers often use a cross-commodity purchase task. These tasks allow participants to make choices across several reinforcers at varied unit prices and may elucidate behavioral economic patterns of substitutability and complementarity. The objective of this study was to conduct a systematic review of cross-commodity purchase task studies with human participants.

Method:

Following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, we screened 46 full-text studies (from 456 total records obtained from PsycInfo and PubMed databases and reference list search), yielding a final sample of 35 studies.

Results:

The drug category with the largest number of studies assessed was tobacco and nicotine products. The most consistent economic relationships found were substitutability of traditional cigarettes by e-cigarettes and e-liquid, and both legal and illegal cannabis for the other; however, other substitutable and complementary relationships were observed (e.g., substitution of food for cigarettes, a complementary relationship between alcohol and cannabis).

Conclusions:

We discuss the implications of the results of this review from a treatment and harm reduction standpoint, highlight areas for future research particularly among drug categories with few studies and evaluating ecological validity of hypothetical measures, and make best practices recommendations for future cross-commodity drug-related purchase task research.

Using the behavioral economic framework of substance demand, researchers posit that drug use is temporally extended pattern of choices made in the presence of other contextual variables. Research conducted by Howard Rachlin and colleagues provided the field with a theoretical and empirical understanding of drug use in the face of alternative or competing environmental variables by describing how humans and non-human animals alike will attempt to maximize reinforcer value over time (e.g., Rachlin, 1978; Rachlin et al., 1976). Rachlin’s research has helped progress our understanding of drug consumption and these patterns of behavior across open versus closed economies, with alternative reinforcement available, and under the constraint of increased unit price. Rachlin et al. (1976) introduced Economic Demand Theory to the field of behavior analysis as a possible solution for examining substitution among qualitatively different commodities. Rather than employ Herrnstein’s matching law (Herrnstein, 1970) in which qualitatively distinct reinforcers may introduce bias (Baum, 1974), Rachlin et al. (1976) suggested that Economic Demand Theory may serve as an explanation for choices made across commodities, a way of graphically explaining an individual’s tendency to maximize overall reinforcement. Rachlin (1978) expanded the concept of maximizing reinforcer value by describing a function in which the duration of reinforcement is related back to its overall value, informing future responses (i.e., a “feedback function”). Conceptualizing drug use as behavior allocation and employing behavioral economic principles to understand changes in demand as a function of manipulating environmental variables allows researchers to determine how the complexity of a given organism’s environment may support, or suppress, drug use in certain contexts.

Initial laboratory studies examining drug self-administration under various economic contexts found that demand for a drug varies as a function of the constraints to access the drug such as price, open versus closed economies, availability of substitutes, etc. (e.g., Catania & Reynolds, 1968; Hursh, 1978; Meisch & Thompson, 1973; see Hursh, 1980, for a review and analysis). Generally, a researcher would arrange the experiment such that the subject would first complete training sessions to pair a given response with access to a drug. The experimenter would then increase the unit price (response required per unit reinforcer) of the drug across trials. The resulting behavior, plotted as consumption as a function of price, came to be known as a demand curve (Hursh, 1980, 1984; Hursh & Roma, 2016), translating the demand curve concept from microeconomics. Consumption values obtained in these experiments were largely consistent with the demand law (Allison (1983) and Rachlin (1976)), which generally states that consumption decreases as a function of increases in price. Various modifications have been made to this general arrangement, but most may be analyzed by plotting a demand curve and quantitatively analyzing the various indices observed directly from consumption values or derived from the demand equation. Observed indices of demand include intensity (consumption at free price), breakpoint (price that suppresses consumption to zero), Omax (maximum expenditure), and Pmax (unit price corresponding to Omax; Hursh & Roma, 2016). Various nonlinear demand curve models have been implemented (e.g., exponential [Hursh & Silberberg, 2008]; exponentiated [Koffarnus et al., 2015]) to estimate an alpha parameter that reflects the rate of change in elasticity over increased price.

While laboratory studies of single drug self-administration facilitated the application of behavioral economics principles to the study of addictive behavior, they were limited in their ecological validity due to the given drug’s closed economy in the laboratory and lack of available alternatives. In real-world contexts, organisms typically make choices between multiple concurrently available reinforcers instead of only one available option. To increase ecological validity of these assessments, researchers began to examine self-administration of multiple drugs, available concurrently. Early research with both humans and animals revealed that consumption of a constant, low-price alternative commodity may increase, decrease, or not change, as the dose of the initial commodity increases (e.g., Carroll et al., 1979; Griffiths et al., 1981; Mello et al., 1980; see Bickel et al., 1995 for an early review), and that providing response-independent reinforcers can decrease consumption of a reinforcer with a response requirement (Rachlin & Baum, 1972). Researchers found that these changes in consumption of a reinforcer as a function of the availability of an alternative can be well-characterized in economic terms as changes in essential value of a reinforcer due to the availability of substitutable or complementary reinforcers (Bickel et al., 1995; Hursh et al., 2013), both of which are defined functionally. For example, if an increase in unit price of Reinforcer A leads to both a decrease in its consumption and an increase in consumption of concurrently available Reinforcer B, available at a lower and fixed unit price, Reinforcer B may be functionally defined as a substitute (Hursh et al., 2013). Similarly, if consumption of Reinforcer B declines as consumption of Reinforcer A declines as a function of increasing unit price, Reinforcer B can be defined as a complement to Reinforcer A (Hursh et al., 2013). If Reinforcer A and Reinforcer B do not interact, these commodities would be considered independents (Hursh et al., 2013). Both substitutes and complements operate on continuums ranging from complete independence to complete substitution or complementary effects. 1 In the middle portion of this continuum, the presence of Reinforcer B has partial effects on the consumption of Reinforcer A, and consumption of Reinforcer B partially changes as a function of price increases for Reinforcer A. For example, if purchase of tobacco cigarettes declines due to an increase in price-per-cigarette, purchase of nicotine gum may concomitantly increase (i.e., serve as a substitute), decrease (i.e., serve as a complement), or remain unchanged (i.e., function independently of tobacco cigarette consumption).

Researchers extended these early cross-commodity studies of dose administration and subsequent observation of alternative drug consumption by developing purchase tasks with human subjects, which could be completed using hypothetical or real rewards (e.g., Bickel et al., 1992; Petry, 2001). Access to a drug could be achieved by reaching some terminal response requirement on a fixed- or variable-schedule or by spending real or hypothetical money. Rather than providing response-independent rewards and observing subsequent consumption of a drug, or by changing the dose of a drug and observing subsequent consumption of alternative rewards, researchers implementing these purchase tasks sought to characterize the economic relationship between two or more commodities by varying the price of one commodity across trials while holding the others at a fixed, generally lower, unit price. The resulting consumption of all commodities could be plotted using a demand curve equation, and substitutable, complementary, or independent relations could be empirically derived from the slope of the demand curves. Own-price elasticity refers to the changes in consumption of a variable price commodity in a current commodity arrangement as a function of changes in its unit price and is calculated as the slope of the demand curve. Likewise, cross-price elasticity refers to the slope of a curve of consumption of an alternative commodity, available at a fixed price, as a function of changes in unit price of another commodity (Hursh & Roma, 2016). Identifying own- and cross-price elasticity between commodities has furthered our understanding of how various drugs, or drugs and non-drug alternatives, interact to promote or diminish their consumption, which may have important implications for public policy as well as therapeutic interventions.

Via cross-commodity purchase tasks, behavioral economic drug researchers may functionally define alternative rewards based on their ability to reduce responding for a given drug. The effectiveness of a given alternative commodity in reducing consumption of a drug may be indicated by cross-price elasticity of the alternative commodity when both are available. Some alternative rewards may function as substitutes while other rewards are independent of drug consumption or may serve as complements. These economic relationships may be impacted by individual differences such as the alternative commodity’s relative reinforcing value or past exposure to the alternative commodity, or context-dependent influences on responding. This may be useful information both on the group and the individual level. For example, recent attention has focused on whether e-cigarettes function as a substitute to traditional cigarettes (e.g. Bickel et al., 2018; DeHart et al., 2019a; Johnson et al., 2017; Pope et al., 2019). Finding alternatives to cigarettes may serve as harm-reduction and may inform policy on pricing and taxing: whether to make alternatives that tend to function as substitutes more affordable than traditional cigarettes to induce purchase of cigarette alternatives. On the other hand, identifying whether two commodities function as complements (e.g., tobacco cigarettes and alcoholic beverages, tobacco cigarettes and food) can be helpful information in designing naturalistic interventions to reduce substance use. Other notable applications of cross-price elasticity to inform policy include a series of studies examining substitutability of illegal vs. legal cannabis in jurisdictions that have already legalized cannabis (e.g., Amlung et al., 2019) or were in the process of federally legalizing cannabis (e.g., Canada; Amlung & MacKillop; 2019)

Prior narrative and systematic reviews/meta-analyses have examined purchase tasks involving single commodities, including recent reviews focused on alcohol (e.g., Martínez-Loredo, 2021), tobacco (e.g., Reed et al., 2020), or illicit drugs (e.g., Strickland et al., 2020), or examined early cross-commodity research in both humans and animals (Bickel et al., 1995). Additionally, Bickel et al. (2018) recently reviewed cross-commodity research examining substitution in the Experimental Tobacco Marketplace. However, no review to our knowledge has synthesized all cross-commodity drug-related purchase task research conducted with human participants. The purposes of this current review were to conduct a systematic, narrative review of cross-commodity assessments of demand, to identify idiosyncrasies in methodology across studies, to examine the elasticity of demand of drug consumption in the face of alternative reinforcers, to investigate differences between purchase tasks that use hypothetical versus actual outcomes, and to discuss best practices and identify areas for future cross-commodity purchase task research.

Method

Search Strategy and Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA; Page et al. 2021) guidelines and flow diagram in conducting this review. The titles and abstracts of articles in the PsycINFO (EBSCO) and PubMed (Medline) databases were searched initially on December 20, 2021, using the terms (cross-price elastic* OR substitut*) AND (behavioral economic* OR purchase task* OR elasticit*) AND (marijuana OR cannabis OR tobacco OR nicotine OR alcohol OR cocaine OR heroin OR caffeine OR fentanyl OR drug). The search was not restricted by year or by journal (except for the availability of an English language version of the paper). Following the screening process (described below) the references of included articles were also screened to identify additional studies for inclusion that may not have been returned from the database searches. We also used Google Scholar to conduct a forward search of articles citing the seminal cross-price elasticity methodology chapter (Hursh & Roma, 2016). This review was not pre-registered.

The titles and abstracts of all retrieved studies were screened by 2 screeners [SW and MA] to determine initial eligibility for full-text screening. To meet criteria for inclusion, a study must have a) involved human participants; b) experimentally manipulated commodity price; c) included a purchase task of two separate commodities, with at least one being a drug (defined as alcohol, tobacco/nicotine product, or an illicit drug), in which subjects made choices between real or hypothetical access to commodities; d) involved holding the price of one commodity constant while the unit price of the other commodity or commodities was manipulated; e) been published in a peer-reviewed, English language journal; and f) provided some measure of cross-price elasticity between the two commodities or provided sufficient data for cross-price elasticity to be calculated. For the purposes of this review, we expanded the definition of “purchase task” to include studies that are distinct from purchase task methodology, but conceptually related (i.e., consumption of an initial commodity measured at escalating response requirements on tasks using plungers, e.g., Bickel et al., 1992). A study was marked for full-text screening if inclusion or exclusion could not be determined by screening the title and abstract alone. Two trained screeners and at least one author read the methods section for each study included in the full-text review and determined eligibility for data extraction using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria.

As shown in the PRISMA diagram in Figure 1, a total of 518 studies (456 unique studies after removal of 62 duplicates) were imported into Covidence™ systematic review software (www.covidence.org) for screening. An additional 410 studies were excluded during title/abstract screening for not meeting inclusion criteria. Of the remaining 46 full-text studies assessed for eligibility, 13 were removed because inclusion criteria were not met once the studies were read in their entirety. Therefore, full-text screening resulted in identification of 33 studies to be included in the review. The citations of each of the included articles were searched to determine other articles that merited inclusion (i.e., a “snowball search”), and 1 additional study was found that met inclusion criteria. The forward reference search of Hursh & Roma (2016) yielded 1 additional study that met inclusion criteria. This review did not require ethics committee approval.

Interrater Reliability

Interrater agreement between the two screeners for both title and abstract screening and the full-text review was calculated by dividing the number of articles in which inclusion/exclusion was agreed upon by the number of agreements and disagreements and multiplying by 100%. Interrater agreement was 94%. In cases in which there was a disagreement on whether the article should be included, at least two of the authors discussed the article until a consensus decision was reached.

Data Extraction

From each study, we extracted number of participants, all commodities assessed in the purchase task(s), method of unit price manipulation, whether rewards were hypothetical or real, the fixed k-value of the exponential or exponentiated demand equation (if relevant), data exclusion criteria prior to analysis (if any), whether substitution occurred between the commodities, and demographic information (if included). We relied on author report in the body of the manuscript. Cross-price elasticity values or sufficient raw data to determine cross-price elasticity was a required inclusion criterion; thus, the data used in this review are fully available from the included studies. We report how we determined all data exclusions and all measures in the study.

Results

Study Characteristics

Thirty-five studies with a sum of 6,547 subjects were included for analysis. Table 1 shows the general study characteristics. In 24 studies, the purchase task was composed of hypothetical outcomes. In the remaining 11 studies, the participants actually received a portion of the commodities earned (i.e., a randomly selected single item), or all commodities earned in the purchase task. Most included studies manipulated monetary price to determine cross-price elasticity of the commodity of interest; however, some manipulated response requirements, while one study, Cole et al. (2008), manipulated drug quality. The number of participants included per study ranged from as many as 1,062 (O’Connor et al., 2014), to as few as 5 (Spiga et al., 2005). We also observed a lack of consistency regarding exclusion criteria contingent on systematic responding on the purchase tasks. Of those studies that did implement criteria for exclusion of data prior to analysis (n = 13), the criteria established by Stein et al. (2015) was most used, followed by the Bruner and Johnson (2014) criteria, while a few researchers used idiosyncratic criteria established for the purposes of the present study. Twelve of the studies included in the current review were published prior to the publication of the systematic data criteria proposed by Stein et al. (2015) and Bruner and Johnson (2014).

For all but three studies, the cross-commodity purchase tasks included only drugs as purchasable commodities. The exceptions were Murphy et al. (2016; cigarettes and food items), Martinetti et al. (2019; alcohol and non-alcoholic beverages), and Ortelli & Martinetti (2021; alcohol and non-alcoholic beverages). For most studies, at least one drug commodity of interest was nicotine (n = 23) and/or tobacco (n =23), followed by cannabis (n = 9), alcohol (n = 9), and cocaine (n = 6). Three studies examined methamphetamine or amphetamine, three studies examined valium, two studies included ecstasy, two studies included very-low nicotine content cigarettes (VLNCs), and one study each examined methadone, opioids other than methadone, and benzodiazepines. A final study by Bickel et al. (1992) examined cross-commodity choices for cigarettes and coffee. Since many people consume coffee to obtain the psychoactive effects of caffeine, classifying this study as choices between two drugs or choices between a drug and non-drug (food) is ambiguous. Across all studies, individual response patterns on the cross-commodity purchase tasks varied. Thus, the following sections are a summary of general response trends identified by the authors.

Substitution Response Trends

Substitutability in Nicotine and Tobacco Product Purchase Tasks

We determined whether substitution occurred between each series of commodities, at the group level, across all studies. Most studies assessed substitutability of alternative nicotine or tobacco products for cigarettes. Products were considered substitutes if, as the unit price of cigarettes increased (resulting in a corresponding reduction in consumption of cigarettes), consumption of the fixed-price alternative products significantly increased. Nine of these studies administered the purchase tasks within the Experimental Tobacco Marketplace, an online store that displays available products, often both tobacco cigarettes and a variety of alternative products, alongside their purchase prices. Product purchasing can be hypothetical or result in actual access to the purchased commodities. Across 9 studies that assessed substitutability of e-cigarettes for tobacco cigarettes, e-cigarettes functioned as a substitute (DeHart et al., 2019a; DeHart et al., 2019b; Grace et al., 2015; Heckman et al., 2017; Johnson et al., 2017; Quisenberry et al., 2017; Quisenberry et al., 2016; Snider et al., 2017; Tucker et al., 2017). The number of participants in the studies ranged from 21 to 350. Eight of those 9 studies used hypothetical outcomes, while Quisenberry et al. (2017) provided actual outcomes. In 2 hypothetical purchase studies, the substitutability of tobacco cigarettes for e-cigarettes was also explored. In both studies tobacco cigarettes served as substitutes (Johnson et al., 2017; Snider et al., 2017). E-liquid, defined as the liquid that fills the tanks of e-cigarettes, also substituted for tobacco cigarettes in 5 studies (1 hypothetical outcome: Heckman et al., 2017; 4 actual outcomes: Freitas-Lemos et al., 2021; Pope et al., 2019; Pope et al., 2020; Stein et al., 2018), but in one study that examined the opposite relationship using actual outcomes, tobacco cigarettes did not function as a substitute for e-liquid (Stein et al., 2018).

Across the 13 studies that examined substitutability of nicotine gum for tobacco cigarettes (DeHart et al., 2019a; DeHart et al., 2019b; Denlinger-Apte et al., 2021; Freitas-Lemos et al., 2021; Heckman et al., 2017; Johnson & Bickel, 2003; Johnson et al., 2004; Johnson et al., 2017; Pope et al., 2019; Pope et al., 2020; Quisenberry et al., 2017; Quisenberry et al., 2016; Shahan et al., 2000), only 5 found that gum functioned as a substitute (Freitas-Lemos et al., 2021; Johnson & Bickel, 2003; Johnson et al., 2004; Johnson et al., 2017; Shahan et al., 2000). Five of the 13 studies employed hypothetical outcomes (DeHart et al., 2019a; DeHart et al., 2019b; Heckman et al., 2017; Johnson et al., 2017; Quisenberry et al., 2016), and only 1 of those 5 found that gum functioned as a substitute (Johnson et al., 2017); thus, 4 of the 5 studies that found gum substituted for tobacco cigarettes used actual outcomes (Freitas-Lemos et al., 2021; Johnson & Bickel, 2003; Johnson et al., 2004; Shahan et al., 2000). In the one study that examined the reverse relationship using a hypothetical purchase task, perhaps unsurprisingly, tobacco cigarettes did not function as a substitute for nicotine gum (Johnson et al., 2017).

Snus was examined as an alternative to tobacco cigarettes in 8 studies (4 hypothetical outcomes: DeHart et al., 2019a; DeHart et al., 2019b; O’Connor et al., 2014; Quisenberry et al., 2016; 4 actual outcomes: Denlinger-Apte et al., 2021; Freitas-Lemos et al., 2021; Pope et al., 2019; Quisenberry et al., 2017), and in 4 of those studies (2 hypothetical outcomes: O’Connor et al., 2014; Quisenberry et al., 2016; 2 actual outcomes: Denlinger-Apte et al., 2021; Quisenberry et al., 2017) snus functioned as a substitute for tobacco cigarettes. Nicotine patches, cigarillos, and smokeless tobacco/chew did not function as a substitute for tobacco cigarettes in any of the included studies. Dip was assessed as a substitute in 4 studies (Freitas-Lemos et al., 2021; Quisenberry et al., 2017; Quisenberry et al., 2016; Pope et al., 2019), but was found to function as a substitute in only 1 of the 4 studies, in an experiment with actual outcomes, and only when e-liquid nicotine content (an additional available alternative) was 0 mg/mL (Pope et al., 2019). One of 2 studies exploring nicotine patches, 1 of 2 studies exploring cigarillos, 2 of 3 studies exploring smokeless tobacco/chew, and 1 of 4 studies exploring dip used a hypothetical purchase task. Dissolvable nicotine pouches functioned as a substitute for tobacco cigarettes in the one study in which this relationship was assessed using a hypothetical purchase task (O’Connor et al., 2014). In 2 hypothetical purchase task studies (Heckman et al., 2017; Tucker et al., 2018), VLNCs were provided as an alternative to tobacco cigarettes. Across both studies, they functioned as a substitute. Additionally, de-nicotinized cigarettes were an available alternative to tobacco cigarettes in one study (Johnson et al., 2004) that provided actual outcomes and functioned as a substitute. Denlinger-Apte et al. (2021) explored the effect of menthol flavoring on substitutability of various nicotine products, providing the actual commodity/commodities following the purchase tasks, and found that non-menthol cigarettes, menthol vapes, menthol snus, and menthol little cigars and cigarillos functioned as substitutes for menthol cigarettes, but nicotine gum and nicotine patches did not.

Substitutability in Marijuana Purchase Tasks

Amlung and MacKillop (2019) and Amlung et al. (2019) examined the substitutability of illegal versus legal cannabis in hypothetical purchase tasks in US states that had legalized cannabis for recreational or medicinal purposes or in Ontario, Canada, a few months prior to the rollout of federal cannabis legalization in October 2018. Both studies found that legal and illegal cannabis functioned as a substitute for the other; however, the magnitude of the substitution effect was asymmetrical. In both studies, introducing a fixed price legal cannabis alternative had a significantly greater impact on elasticity for illegal cannabis than the reverse scenario. Peters et al. (2017) assessed the relationship between tobacco and cannabis cigarettes using a hypothetical purchase task and found that neither substituted for the other. Dolan et al. (2020) found that neither alcohol nor cannabis functioned as a substitute for the other in a series of hypothetical purchase tasks.

Substitutability in Drug versus Non-drug Purchase Tasks

A few studies also examined the relationship between a non-drug and a drug: non-alcoholic and alcoholic beverages, food items and cigarettes, and coffee and cigarettes (although we again point out that coffee may not be best classified be considered a non-drug/food commodity given psychostimulant effects of caffeine). Nevertheless, Bickel et al. (1992) found that coffee and cigarettes did not function as substitutes for each other. However, food items functioned as substitutes for cigarettes (Murphy et al., 2016). In one study, non-alcoholic beverages substituted for alcoholic beverages (Martinetti et al., 2019). In an additional study comparing alcoholic to non-alcoholic beverages, non-alcoholic beverages were not found to function as a substitute for alcoholic beverages, though the availability of a non-alcoholic beverage reduced demand for alcohol (Ortelli & Martinetti, 2021).

Substitutability in Polydrug Use Purchase Tasks

Six studies examined relationships across several classes of drugs among polydrug users. The methodology of these studies, based on Petry and Bickel (1998), deviated from the remaining studies in this review. Though the general purchase task arrangement was the same (one drug escalated in price while the remaining drugs remained at a fixed cost), participants were provided options among multiple drugs at their estimated “street” costs (i.e., the estimated amount that the illicit substance would cost in the natural environment). Due to the variety of drug classes assessed, and the differences in specific substance use disorder diagnoses, the results of this research are complex and comparisons across studies is not feasible. An in-depth analysis is also beyond the scope of this review; thus, we will provide general substitution response trends from each study.

Petry and Bickel (1998) initiated this methodology and assessed polydrug use among individuals with heroin use disorder. They found that valium substituted for heroin for half of the participants and that cocaine substituted for heroin at high heroin price points but appeared to have a complementary relationship with heroin at low heroin price points. In a later study among individuals with heroin use disorder, symmetrical substitutability was observed between pharmaceutical opioids and heroin (Chalmers et al., 2010). Jofre-Bonet & Petry (2008) examined individuals with heroin and cocaine use disorder, finding that for individuals with heroin use disorder, valium and cigarettes served as substitutes for heroin while marijuana and valium substituted for cocaine. For individuals with cocaine use disorder, marijuana and cocaine substituted for heroin while marijuana, heroin, and valium substituted for cocaine.

Assessing polydrug use in individuals with alcohol use disorder, several drugs were examined but the only substitutable relationship found was that alcohol substituted for cocaine as cocaine price increased (Petry, 2001). Cole et al. (2008) found symmetrical substitution between cocaine and ecstasy. While alcohol substituted for ecstasy, cannabis, and cocaine, and cannabis substituted for cocaine, these relationships were asymmetrical. Sumnall et al. (2004) also found both symmetric and asymmetric substitution relationships across different substances. Amphetamine substituted for alcohol and vice versa, and cocaine substituted for ecstasy and vice versa. However, while alcohol substituted for cocaine, the opposite relationship was not observed.

Complementary Response Trends

Bickel et al. (1992) observed a complementary relationship between coffee and cigarettes, while Spiga et al. (2005) found a similar relationship between methadone and cigarettes and Jofre-Bonet & Petry (2008) observed this complementary relationship between alcohol and cigarettes. Dolan et al. (2020) found alcohol and cannabis to function as complements. Cocaine served as a complement to heroin at low heroin prices, but substitutable for heroin at high heroin prices (Petry & Bickel, 1998); however, in later hypothetical purchase task studies, cocaine appeared to be complementary to heroin despite price (Chalmers et al., 2010; Jofre-Bonet & Petry, 2008), as was alcohol (Jofre-Bonet & Petry, 2008). Cocaine, marijuana, and alcohol all appeared to have complementary relationships (Jofre-Bonet & Petry, 2008). Food initially appeared to have a complementary relationship to cigarettes as cigarette prices first increased, though this relationship did not maintain as cigarette prices continued to increase (Murphy et al., 2016).

Discussion

Cross-commodity purchase tasks help researchers understand how consumption of a drug changes when alternative drugs or reinforcers are available. In the current review, we synthesized the body of research on cross-commodity drug purchase tasks with human subjects. Several general response patterns emerged. We found that for purchase tasks examining alternatives to cigarettes, e-cigarettes and e-liquid served as the readiest substitutes. Legal cannabis substituted for illegal cannabis, and vice versa, and among polydrug users, several different drug classes functioned as substitutes for each other. We also found complementary relationships among different drug classes. Oftentimes, results were idiosyncratic at the individual level, differing from the average response trends. In other words, some alternatives may function as a substitute for, or complement to, a drug for most individuals; however, this relationship may not be observed across all participants.

Most of the cross-commodity research to-date has focused on cigarettes and nicotine-replacement alternatives. While physicians and public health experts have stated that e-cigarettes are less harmful than traditional tobacco cigarettes (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2022), epidemiological data on the long-term health effects of e-cigarette use is not yet available (Balfour et al., 2021). Researchers and physicians do agree that e-cigarette use (compared to no use) is harmful (CDC, 2022). From a harm reduction standpoint, although e-cigarettes are not considered universally healthy, they may substitute for traditional cigarettes more frequently and may be more successful at reducing traditional cigarette consumption than other nicotine replacement therapy products (Grace et al., 2015; Johnson et al., 2017). The prospect of VLNC cigarettes is also appealing as a harm-reducing substitute because they reduce overall nicotine intake, thus reducing the reinforcing effects of smoking (Tucker et al., 2018). Together, these studies provide evidence that individuals may shift their preference away from combustible tobacco cigarettes to less dangerous (albeit not necessarily safe per se) alternatives when the cost of tobacco cigarettes is raised to a point at which consumption of a lower-cost alternative is deemed preferable by the individual.

Drug versus non-drug, cannabis, and alcohol cross-commodity purchase tasks require more research. Like the search for replacements for tobacco cigarettes, understanding the relationship between cannabis, alcohol, and other drugs or non-drug reinforcers may hold important public policy implications. Operating within a behavioral economic framework, and indeed, a choice perspective more broadly, identifying alternative sources of reinforcement is an important component of treatment interventions because individuals will seek to maximize reinforcement in their environment. Therefore, removing the reinforcement that is derived from an illicit substance or from alcohol when treating substance- or alcohol use disorder means that an effective long-term treatment should likely involve implementing non-drug or non-alcohol related alternative activities. Identifying alternative substance-free activities and sources of reinforcement has shown to be an important component to success in treatment interventions (Murphy et al., 2006, 2007). In the present review, only a handful of studies examined the substitutability of drug versus non-drug substances and no studies identified in the systematic review examined non-drug activities. While the results of the current body of research in this area is promising (i.e., individuals substituted non-alcoholic beverages for alcohol beverages and food items for cigarettes; Martinetti et al., 2019; Murphy et al., 2016), additional research is needed to replicate these results and expand this methodology to non-drug activities.

Substitution of a drug by a non-drug commodity did not always occur in these studies. Nevertheless, the systematic changes in demand for alcohol observed by Ortelli & Martinetti (2021) in the presence of a non-alcoholic alternative beverage may still have important treatment implications. Demand for alcoholic beverages was lower when a fixed-price non-alcoholic beverage was available than when alcohol was the only commodity available for purchase. This finding aligns with research that show reduced alcohol consumption when substance-free alternatives are promoted (e.g. Correia et al., 2005). However, behavioral economic theory would suggest that tests of substitutability are a necessary step in identifying non-drug alternatives that would support sustained behavior change. While Ortelli & Martinetti (2021) provide preliminary evidence that this may not always be a necessary boundary condition for treatment, several caveats should be noted. Though prior research promoting substance-free alternative reinforcement has not formally assessed the economic substitutability of the alternatives, it is possible that the activities did function as substitutes, explaining positive treatment outcomes. Additionally, purchase tasks do not assess behavior patterns over extended periods of time. Whether the mere presence of lower-cost non-drug alternatives would reduce alcohol consumption in a significant and durable way despite not functioning as a substitute in an economic sense is certainly an important future direction to explore.

Alcohol and cannabis cross-commodity purchase tasks using another drug as the fixed-price alternative may have important implications for treatment interventions, because the results of this research may identify complementary as well as substitute relationships between the commodities. There has been limited research focus on complementary relationships in the area of cross-commodity purchase tasks; identifying complementary relationships between substances may help researchers and clinicians alike to determine the environments that may be most likely to support relapse of substance use. In the current review, Jofre-Bonet & Petry (2008) and Dolan et al. (2020) found complementary relationships between alcohol use and cigarettes, and alcohol and cannabis use, respectively, suggesting that the environments in which one of these alternative commodities is consumed may serve also as a discriminative cue to consume alcohol. For example, for individuals with alcohol use disorder for whom these complementary relationships are present, exposure to an environment with heavy cannabis use or heavy cigarette use or cues may induce cravings for alcohol and may lead to alcohol consumption. Indeed, this cross-commodity effect has been demonstrated in laboratory cue exposure studies (e.g., Ritchie et al., 2022), although evidence of whether cue reactivity leads to actual increases in consumption remains inconsistent across studies. Continued research on complementary relationships between alcohol, cannabis, and other alternative drugs may help individualize treatment plans for individuals with alcohol or cannabis use disorder.

The results of the current review also shed light on the ecological validity of cross-commodity purchase tasks. There has been increased debate to whether and how purchase tasks can be arranged such that the behavior observed under the contrived constraints of the task have good correspondence with behavior in an individual’s natural environment, in which choices between which to allocate behavior are almost innumerable. The increase in focus on cross-commodity research moves the needle in that direction; participants are now able to allocate responding across a series of choices. The methodology of the polydrug studies included in this review are an example of how research may continue to increase in ecological validity, but also shows the costs inherent to enhancing generality to the natural environment. The reviewed polydrug studies presented an increased complexity due to the assessment of several drug classes within one purchase task, available at “street” prices, among individuals with different drug dependencies. This arrangement makes it difficult to isolate the maintaining variables within each purchase task. However, these studies are perhaps more ecologically valid than the studies in which only one drug alternative is offered, or the drug alternatives offered are all within the same drug class (i.e., nicotine). By holding income constant, researchers of polydrug use were able to mimic what real world choices may be made between alternative commodities when a preferred drug increases in price. An increase in ecological validity in research is generally a tradeoff; here, researchers lose some of the ability to conduct high-resolution purchase task analyses when participants are choosing from a broad range of drug classes but are perhaps able to make more direct links between the results of their research and behavioral allocation in the natural environment. Though these studies still maintain experimental control by holding prices constant across participants and conducting a purchase task similar to other studies, the results may be considered more realistic.

The magnitude of substitution (i.e., at what unit price demand for Drug A, at an escalating cost, becomes elastic and consumption of alternative Drug B supersedes that of Drug A) often differed across studies within each Results category, but we only reported whether Drug B substituted for Drug A (i.e., a binary yes/no outcome). This is because we found much variability in the study characteristics within each drug category. The method of assessing substance or alcohol use disorder, recruitment methods, whether outcomes were hypothetical or real, and demand equations and data exclusion criteria used all differed across studies. Thus, we determined that the studies differed too widely in methodology to make more fine-grained comparisons. These differences call for a set of agreed-upon best practices in cross-commodity purchase task research. While some of these differences may not be feasible to control across studies (i.e., recruitment methods and hypothetical or real outcomes), the ability to compare results across studies can help us derive public policy and individualized treatment interventions alike and may come from deriving common practices for demand analyses and data exclusion criteria.

What follows are some of our suggestions for best practices in conducting cross-commodity purchase task research.2 First, we recommend use of Hursh’s cross-price elasticity model (2014) to quantitively describe cross-commodity interactions. This model provides two metrics helpful in describing cross-price elasticity: a constant describing the interactions of two commodities (I) and a sensitivity parameter to describe the influence of a fixed-priced concurrent alternative commodity on an ascending price target commodity (β). Second, to arrive at a dataset amenable to Hursh’s cross-price elasticity model, researchers must assess demand for Commodity A in isolation using prototypical purchase task methods (i.e., alone price elasticity), and also assess demand for Commodity A when Commodity B is concurrently available at a fixed-price to render own-price elasticity for Commodity A and cross-price elasticity for Commodity B. Relatedly, we encourage researchers to also evaluate whether a symmetrical cross-commodity relation exists when Commodity B becomes the ascending price commodity and Commodity A is evaluated as the fixed price alternative (i.e., potential substitute/complement). Third, if analyzing data at the individual subject-level, we suggest that researchers use a base demand model (e.g., exponentiated [Koffarnus et al., 2015] or zero-bounded [Gilroy et al., 2021]) that permits inclusion of 0 consumption values, due to the participants may report no consumption of fixed-price Commodity B at very low prices of Commodity A if Commodity B is a perfect or near-perfect substitute, and likewise, potentially no consumption of Commodity A when its cost exceeds that of Commodity B if it is a perfect or near-perfect substitute (the exponential model of demand [Hursh & Silberberg, 2008] suffices if analyzing aggregate curves using mean consumption values).3 Fourth, we suggest that researchers supplement aforementioned analyses with linear slope analysis of the fixed-price alternative to render data consistent with the literature preceding development of Hursh’s quantitative model (for an example of an analysis using both cross-price elasticity models and linear slope analysis, see Amlung et al., 2019). Finally, we encourage researchers to set the fixed-price alternative to approximately 80% of the market price for that commodity—doing so renders the commodity attractive enough that the cross-price models would be sensitive to potential cross-price elasticity effects (note that additional research is needed to evaluate how pricing of the concurrently available fixed-price alternative modulates the cross-price effects).

Population variables may have moderated the effects found in the present review. For example, Snider et al. (2017) found greater substitution of e-cigarettes for traditional cigarettes may be observed in dual users of cigarettes and e-cigarettes when compared to non-users of e-cigarettes. Substitution effects of nicotine and tobacco products was the only category with a sufficient number of studies to explore the effect of population as a moderating variable. However, this still proved infeasible. While two studies required participants to be non-regular e-cigarette users or e-cigarette naïve (Freitas-Lemos et al., 2021; Tucker et al., 2017), and one study examined demand as a function of frequency of e-cigarette use across participants (Snider et al., 2017), the remaining studies included both e-cigarette users and non-users and did not differentiate between these groups in their analyses. Therefore, future research examining the impact of population-level factors on responding in cross-commodity purchase tasks is warranted. Additionally, researchers should consider providing subgroup demand data for studies in which population factors may not be the independent variable of interest, but for which the recruited participants may still vary in their use patterns (e.g., dual versus single use).

Some limitations are worth noting. First, we reported which studies used hypothetical versus actual outcomes next to the summary of results. However, as we were not able to conduct quantitative analyses across the studies, we did directly compare studies using hypothetical and actual outcomes. Correspondence between behavioral economic purchase tasks for hypothetical and actual rewards remains largely an open question. Wilson et al. (2016) compared demand for cigarettes between a traditional hypothetical purchase task and a modified purchase task using real outcomes in which participants purchased a week’s worth of cigarettes at varying prices across weekly sessions. The authors found low correspondence between the hypothetical and actual purchase tasks; however, the temporal window for the hypothetical purchase task was different from that of the actual purchase task (one day versus one week), and participants did not always adhere to the closed economy of laboratory-provided cigarettes during the week, often purchasing their own between sessions, limiting the conclusions drawn regarding correspondence between the purchase tasks. Two studies using the alcohol purchase task (for alcohol rewards alone) demonstrated generally high correspondence between hypothetical and incentivized versions of the task (Amlung et al., 2012; Amlung & MacKillop, 2015) and self-reported consumption on an alcohol purchase task correlated with actual alcohol consumption during a lab self-administration period. A recent study also demonstrated for the first time that in-the-moment reports of alcohol demand (using ecological momentary assessment) predicted drinking behavior in the natural environment (Motchsman et al., 2022). However, we are not aware of similar studies for other drug types, and no studies have directly examined correspondence between hypothetical and actual versions of cross-commodity purchase tasks. Accordingly, whether response patterns when outcomes are hypothetical can truly be compared to response patterns when the participant actually receives the outcome from the purchase task remains a nascent area. Future research should continue to address this question, particularly for cross-commodity purchase tasks, and future reviews should synthesize studies comparing hypothetical and actual outcomes as research is added to the literature base. Until such evidence is available, we caution against overgeneralizing findings from hypothetical purchase tasks to drinking and drug use patterns in the natural environment.

We also limited our review to purchase tasks with human participants. We limited our search because our primary focus for the results of this review were to discuss public policy implications and the utility of this research for treatment interventions for individuals with substance or alcohol use disorders. However, future reviews should synthesize the animal literature base of cross-commodity purchase tasks to provide a historical and conceptual basis for human behavioral economic research.

The present systematic review provides researchers with a synthesis of the current cross-commodity drug-related purchase task research. While we found strong evidence for e-cigarettes and e-liquid as substitutes for traditional cigarettes, as well as illegal for legal cannabis and vice versa, evidence for substitutable relationships across the remaining drug and non-drug commodities reviewed were not as consistent. We also found complementary relationships for several commodities. These results may hold important treatment implications, and we underscore that additional research is warranted, using methodology based on our recommendations for best practices for cross-commodity purchase tasks. Continued work in this area will further elucidate the clinical utility of identifying substitutes and complements to drug consumption for effective treatment intervention and harm reduction.

Public Health Significance Statement:

This review found that the use of behavioral economic purchase tasks to evaluate choices between multiple, simultaneously available drugs can be useful in identifying alternatives in the treatment of substance use disorders, such as providing e-cigarettes as an alternative to traditional cigarette smoking, or complementary relationships between drugs. The results of this review can help the scientific and clinical communities understand what environments may be more likely to induce drug use, and whether and how alternatives may serve as substitutes to drug use.
