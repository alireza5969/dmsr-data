Hepatic Adenoma Subtypes on Hepatobiliary Phase of Gadoxetic Acid–Enhanced MRI: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Abstract

BACKGROUND.

Accumulating evidence indicates that hepatocellular adenoma (HCA) may have a higher frequency of hepatobiliary phase (HBP) iso- or hyperintensity than previously reported.

OBJECTIVE.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the proportion of HCA that shows iso- or hyperintensity in the HBP of gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI, stratified by HCA subtype (HNF1a-inactivated [H-HCA], inflammatory [I-HCA], β-catenin–activated [B-HCA], and unclassified [U-HCA] HCA), and to assess the diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for differentiating focal nodular hyperplasia (FNH) from HCA.

EVIDENCE ACQUISITION.

PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials were searched through February 14, 2022, for articles reporting HBP signal intensity on gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI among pathologically proven HCAs, stratified by subtype. The pooled proportion of HBP iso- or hyperintensity was determined for each subtype and compared using metaregression. Diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for differentiating FNH from all HCA subtypes combined and from B-HCA and U-HCA combined was assessed using bivariate modeling.

EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS.

Twenty-eight studies (12 original investigations, 16 case reports or case series) were included, yielding 364 patients with 410 HCAs (112 H-HCAs, 203 I-HCAs, 33 B-HCAs, 62 U-HCAs). Pooled proportion of HBP iso- or hyperintensity was 14% (95% CI, 4–26%) among all HCAs, 0% (95% CI, 0–2%) among H-HCAs, 11% (95% CI, 0–29%) among U-HCAs, 14% (95% CI, 2–31%) among I-HCAs, and 59% (95% CI, 26–88%) among B-HCAs; metaregression showed significant difference among subtypes (p < .001). In four studies reporting diagnostic performance information, HBP iso- or hyperintensity had sensitivity of 99% (95% CI, 57–100%) and specificity of 89% (95% CI, 82–94%) for differentiating FNH from all HCA subtypes and sensitivity of 99% (95% CI, 53–100%) and specificity of 65% (95% CI, 44–80%) for differentiating FNH from B-HCA or U-HCA.

CONCLUSION.

HCA subtypes other than H-HCA show proportions of HBP iso- or hyperintensity ranging from 11% (U-HCA) to 59% (B-HCA). Low prevalence of B-HCA has contributed to prior reports of high diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for differentiating FNH from HCA.

CLINICAL IMPACT.

Radiologists should recognize the low specificity of HBP iso- or hyperintensity on gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI for differentiating FNH from certain HCA subtypes.

Hepatocellular adenoma (HCA) is an uncommon benign liver tumor that occurs predominantly in women of reproductive age, with an incidence of 34 per 1 million in women taking oral contraceptives [1, 2]. The most recent classification of HCA subtypes, published in 2017, categorized HCA into eight subtypes based on molecular and gene expression features [3]. Nonetheless, available investigations of HCA subtypes have largely explored four main subtypes based on the prior classification from 2006 [4]: HNF1a-inactivated HCA (H-HCA, representing 30–40% of HCAs), inflammatory HCA (I-HCA, 40–55%), β-catenin–activated HCA (B-HCA, 10–20%), and unclassified HCA (U-HCA, 5–10%). Accurate differentiation of HCA subtypes is important, as the subtypes have different risks of complication and thus warrant tailored therapy. For example, 46% of B-HCAs develop hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) or a borderline lesion between HCA and HCC [4], such that a recommendation for resection is warranted for this subtype. On the other hand, among subtypes, H-HCA has the lowest rate of hemorrhage and malignant transformation [3] and, if smaller than 5 cm, can be managed by observation and lifestyle changes such as weight loss [1].

Focal nodular hyperplasia (FNH) is a common benign liver lesion for which intervention is generally not recommended [1]. FNH and HCA exhibit similar clinical and imaging features [1, 5, 6]. If a diagnosis of FNH is certain by contrast-enhanced MRI, then no further imaging follow-up is needed. However, if diagnosis by MRI is equivocal, then biopsy may be needed to differentiate FNH from HCA, although biopsy has a risk of hemorrhage.

Gadoxetic acid (gadolinium–ethoxybenzyl–diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid [gadolinium–EOB–DTPA], Eovist in the United States, Primovist outside of the United States, Bayer Schering) is an MRI contrast agent with both extracellular and hepatocellular distribution. Approximately 50% of the injected dose enters hepatocytes and is excreted into bile, allowing imaging in the hepatobiliary phase (HBP) after contrast medium administration. HBP imaging may aid liver lesion characterization by providing information regarding the presence of functioning hepatocytes within the lesion. Gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI with HBP imaging is widely used clinically for differentiating FNH from HCA and for directing further management [7]. FNH is expected to be iso- or hyperintense in the HBP because of the presence of functioning hepatocytes with abnormal biliary ductules [6]. In comparison, HCA is expected to be hypointense in the HBP because these lesions are generally considered to lack bile duct proliferation [6, 7].

A systematic review from 2015 reported that HBP signal intensity had high diagnostic performance for differentiating FNH from HCA, with a sensitivity of 91–100% and a specificity of 87–100% for FNH [8]. However, five of the six studies included in the review considered HCA as a single entity without consideration of HCA subtypes [8]. Accumulating evidence that stratifies HCA by subtype indicates that HCA may have a higher frequency of HBP iso- or hyperintensity than previously reported, such that the application of HBP signal intensity alone to differentiate FNH from HCA can be misleading [9, 10]. For example, studies have observed HBP iso- or hyperintensity in 89% of I-HCAs [9] and 83% of B-HCAs [10], and additional case reports have described HBP iso- or hyperintensity in B-HCAs [11–14].

The European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) 2016 clinical practice guidelines [1] and the 7th International Forum for Liver MRI 2016 consensus report [15] indicated the need for further investigation into the association between HCA subtypes and HBP findings. The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the proportion of HCA that shows iso- or hyperintensity in the HBP of gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI, stratified by HCA subtype. The secondary objective was to assess the diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for differentiating FNH from HCA.

Evidence Acquisition

Literature Search Strategy and Article Selection Criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis is reported according to the PRISMA diagnostic test accuracy of reviews guidelines [16]. The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO CRD42022309023). The literature search was structured to identify articles on the basis of the following patient characteristic, index test, and target conditions to reflect the review’s primary objective: patients with liver lesion(s) evaluated by gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI that were pathologically proven to be HCA and that were classified by subtype (H-HCA, I-HCA, B-HCA, or U-HCA). Studies relevant to the secondary objective of investigating the diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for the differentiation of FNH from HCA were selected only from among those studies included in the analysis of the primary objective. Two radiologists (T.H.K. and S.W., with 3 and 5 years of experience, respectively, in performing systematic reviews) performed the literature search, study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment processes independently; disagreements were resolved by consensus.

PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases were systematically searched for articles through February 14, 2022, using the following search terms: (adenoma) AND (eovist OR primovist OR gadoxetic OR “gadoxetate disodium” OR “gadolinium-EOB-DTPA”). The references in identified articles were also searched for additional relevant articles. After removal of duplicate articles, articles were screened based on title and abstract and excluded if they were a letter, editorial, or abstract; a review, guideline, or consensus statement; or not relevant to the study question. Remaining articles were assessed at the full-text level and excluded if classification of HCA subtype was not provided; HBP signal intensity was not reported for each HCA subtype; evaluation was performed only in children; article was not in English; or the patient sample overlapped with that of another included study. Included articles were classified as original investigations or as case reports and case series.

Data Extraction

A standardized form was used to extract the publication, clinical, and MRI characteristics of all included articles. To assess diagnostic performance for differentiating FNH from HCA among studies included in the analysis of the secondary objective, a 2 × 2 contingency table was constructed to summarize the data in these studies using the following definitions: true-positive (HBP iso- or hyperintensity and reference standard diagnosis of FNH), true-negative (HBP hypointensity and reference standard diagnosis of HCA), false-positive (HBP iso- or hyperintensity and reference standard diagnosis of HCA), and false-negative (HBP hypointensity and reference standard diagnosis of FNH).

Quality Assessment

The methodologic quality of included articles was assessed using separate questionnaires for original investigations (Table S1, available in the online supplement) and for case reports and case series (Table S2, available in the online supplement). The questionnaires were based on QUADAS-2 criteria [17], which were tailored for the assessment of case reports and case series. All articles were assessed in the patient selection, index test, and reference standard domains in terms of risk of bias and concerns of applicability as well as in the flow and timing domains in terms of risk of bias.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome was the pooled proportion of HCAs showing HBP iso- or hyperintensity on gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI, stratified by HCA subtype. For studies presenting both qualitative and quantitative assessments of HBP iso- or hyperintensity, the qualitative results were used. The pooled proportions and 95% CIs were calculated using the Der Simonian–Laird random-effects model, with generation of forest plots and 95% CIs. Because variance from studies reporting a proportion at the extreme ends of the range from 0 to 1 would artificially inflate the weight that these studies contributed toward the pooled-proportion estimate, the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to stabilize the variance on the basis of the inverse variance method [18]. Heterogeneity among studies was quantified using Higgins inconsistency index (I2) [19]. Metaregression analysis with multiple pairwise comparisons of proportions, using HCA subtype as a covariate, was used to assess the statistical significance of differences in the proportion showing HBP iso- or hyperintensity among HCA subtypes.

For the secondary outcome (diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity to differentiate FNH from HCA), per-lesion sensitivity and specificity were derived from the 2 × 2 contingency tables. Pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates were calculated with hierarchic logistic regression modeling using bivariate modeling and point estimates [20]. The diagnostic performance was determined separately for differentiation of FNH from all HCA subtypes combined and of FNH from only B-HCA and U-HCA combined. The latter determination included B-HCA and U-HCA because these subtypes lack specific conventional MRI features for their differentiation from FNH; in addition, B-HCA has the highest potential for malignant transformation among HCA subtypes, and U-HCA has an unknown potential for malignant transformation [21].

A p value less than .05 indicated statistical significance except for the multiple pairwise comparisons performed as part of the metaregression analysis, for which a Bonferroni-corrected p value less than .008 indicated statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3) and Stata (version 13.0, StataCorp). The meta-regression analysis used the metareg function in the metafor packages in the R software.

Evidence Synthesis

Literature Search

Figure 1 summarizes the study selection process. The initial search identified 353 articles, including 249 unique articles after removal of duplicate records. A total of 189 articles were then excluded based on review of article titles and abstracts (letter, editorial, or abstract [n = 6]; review, guideline, or consensus statement [n = 79]; not relevant to study question [n = 104]). The full text was reviewed for the remaining 60 articles, resulting in exclusion of an additional 32 articles (HCA subtype classification not provided [n = 24]; HBP signal intensity not reported for each HCA subtype [n = 3]; evaluation performed only in children [n = 2]; article not in English [n = 2]; patient sample overlapped with another included study [n = 1]). These exclusions resulted in a final sample of 28 articles (12 original investigations [9, 10, 22–31] and 16 case reports or series [11–14, 32–43]) included in the evaluation of the primary objective of determining the proportion of HCAs showing HBP iso- or hyperintensity, stratified by subtype. Four of the included original investigations also reported the diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for differentiating FNH from HCA and were included in the analysis of the secondary objective [23, 26, 27, 29]. Table S3 (available in the online supplement) lists the 32 articles excluded during review at the full-text level along with the reason for exclusion of each article.

Characteristic of Included Articles

Table 1 provides clinical characteristics of included original investigations, Table 2 provides clinical characteristics of included case reports and case series, and Table S4 (available in the online supplement) provides publication and MRI characteristics of all included articles. All 12 original investigations were retrospective. The meta-analysis for the primary objective included 364 patients with 410 HCAs. In the study by Agarwal et al. [22], seven I-HCAs were diagnosed by biopsy, and an additional 17 lesions were presumed to represent I-HCAs because they showed MRI features identical to the biopsied lesions. Otherwise, all HCAs in all articles underwent pathologic evaluation. Nearly all studies determined HCA subtype by immunohistochemistry with at least one specific marker for each HCA subtype [44], except for two studies that did not explicitly indicate how HCA subtype was determined [26, 38] and three studies that determined subtype only by glutamine synthetase [22], cytokeratin [33], or amyloid and c-reactive protein [36] immunohistochemistry staining. The sample included 112 H-HCAs, 203 I-HCAs, 33 B-HCAs, and 62 U-HCAs.

In two studies, patients underwent MRI with administration of either gadobenate dimeglumine (MultiHance, Bracco Diagnostics) or gadoxetic acid. One of these studies provided separate results for each contrast medium [30]; for this study, only the 22 patients who underwent MRI with gadoxetic acid were included in the meta-analysis. In the other study, data were not provided separately for patients who were injected with gadoxetic acid and with gadobenate dimeglumine (24 and three patients, respectively) [25]; for this study, all 27 patients were included in the meta-analysis.

All articles defined HBP iso- or hyperintensity by qualitative assessment. The qualitative assessment used the liver parenchyma as the reference for determining HBP iso- or hyperintensity in 16 articles [10, 12, 14, 22, 23, 25, 27, 29–31, 34, 36–38, 41, 42], and the reference for this assessment was not specified in the other 12 articles [9, 11, 13, 24, 26, 28, 32, 33, 35, 39, 40, 43]. Only one article indicated how lesions with heterogeneous HBP signal intensity were classified by the qualitative assessment [9]; in that study, lesions were classified as showing HBP iso- or hyperintensity if this appearance was observed in at least 25% of the lesion. Three studies also performed quantitative assessment of HBP signal intensity [26, 27, 31].

The four studies included in the analysis for the secondary objective included a total of 150 FNHs and 137 HCAs [23, 26, 27, 29]. The number of FNHs per study ranged from six to 68. The reference standard for the diagnosis of FNH was pathology in three studies [23, 27, 29], and a combination of pathology and imaging follow-up in one study [26].

Quality Assessment

Table 3 summarizes the quality assessment. The 12 original investigations [9, 10, 22–31] were assessed with the QUADAS-2 tool. In the patient selection domain, four studies had high risk of bias due to a case-control study design for the differentiation of FNH and HCA [23, 26, 27, 29]; no study had high concern for applicability. In the index test domain, one study had high risk of bias because the index test (MRI) was interpreted based on the presence of identical features between biopsied and nonbiopsied HCA, indicating nonblinded interpretation [22]; four studies had unclear risk of bias because they did not explicitly describe interpretation of the index test (MRI) as blinded to the reference standard [24, 25, 28, 30]; and one study had high concern for applicability because it included results from three patients who underwent gadobenate dimeglumine–enhanced MRI [25]. In the reference standard domain, one study had high risk of bias and high concern for applicability because the reference standard for HCA subtype was glutamine synthetase immunohistochemistry staining [22], and one study had high risk of bias and high concern for applicability because it did not clearly explain how HCA subtype was determined and the reference standard for FNH incorporated findings from the index test (MRI) [26]. In the flow and timing domain, one study had high risk of bias because some FNH diagnoses were established by stability on MRI follow-up despite the possibility for HCA to be stable [26], and one study had high risk of bias because some nonbiopsied lesions were presumed to be I-HCA on the basis of MRI features identical to biopsied I-HCA [22].

The 16 case reports and case series were assessed by the tailored QUADAS-2 tool [11–14, 32–43]. In the patient selection domain, all articles were considered at high risk of bias; none had high concern for applicability. In the index test domain, no article was considered to have high risk of bias or high concern for applicability. In the reference standard domain, two articles had high risk of bias and high concern of applicability because the reference standard for HCA subtype included only cytokeratin [33] or amyloid and C-reactive protein [36] immunohistochemistry staining, and one article had unclear risk of bias and high concern for applicability because it did not clearly explain how HCA subtype was determined [38]. Finally, in the flow and timing domain, no article was considered to have high risk of bias.

Proportion of Hepatocellular Adenoma Showing Hepatobiliary Phase Iso- or Hyperintensity, According to Subtype

Figure 2 summarizes the proportion of HCAs showing HBP iso- or hyperintensity, overall and by HCA subtype. The overall pooled proportion of HCAs showing HBP iso- or hyperintensity was 14% (95% CI, 4–26%; I2 = 74%). The proportion was 0% (95% CI, 0–2%; I2 = 17%) for H-HCA, 11% (95% CI, 0–29%; I2 = 42%) for U-HCA, 14% (95% CI, 2–31%; I2 = 83%) for I-HCA, and 59% (95% CI, 26–88%; I2 = 44%) for B-HCA. In the meta-regression analysis, this proportion was significantly different among the four subtypes (p < .001). In pairwise comparisons, the proportion was significantly higher for B-HCA than for H-HCA (p < .001) and U-HCA (p = .005) but was not significantly different among other pairwise combinations (all p > .008). Figure 3 summarizes the results of metaregression analyses.

Diagnostic Performance of Hepatobiliary Phase Iso- or Hyperintensity for Differentiating Focal Nodular Hyperplasia From Hepatocellular Adenoma

Table 4 summarizes the diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for differentiating FNH and HCA in the four studies included in the analysis of the secondary objective [23, 26, 27, 29]. When combining all HCA subtypes, HBP iso- or hyperintensity had a summary sensitivity of 99% (95% CI, 57–100%) and summary specificity of 89% (95% CI, 82–94%) for the diagnosis of FNH. When only including B-HCA and U-HCA, the summary sensitivity was 99% (95% CI, 53–100%), and the summary specificity was 65% (95% CI, 44–80%) for the diagnosis of FNH.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we summarized the proportion of HCAs showing HBP iso- or hyperintensity on gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI, stratified by HCA subtype. The pooled proportion was highest for B-HCA (59%), which was significantly higher than the pooled proportion for H-HCA (0%) and U-HCA (11%) and showed a nonsignificant difference in comparison with the pooled proportion for I-HCA (14%). Furthermore, HBP iso- or hyperintensity had sensitivity of 99% and specificity of 89% for differentiating FNH from all HCA subtypes combined but sensitivity of 99% and specificity of 65% for differentiating FNH from only B-HCA and U-HCA. The high diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity among all HCA subtypes can be attributed to the low prevalence of B-HCA and U-HCA among all HCA (particularly the low prevalence of B-HCA, which had the highest proportion of HBP iso- or hyperintensity). The findings are concerning because B-HCA has the highest rate of malignant transformation among HCA subtypes. Thus, radiologists need to be aware that application of HBP iso- or hyperintensity in isolation to differentiate FNH from HCA may be misleading.

A meta-analysis published in 2017 also evaluated HBP signal intensity stratified by HCA subtype [45], although the study evaluated a smaller total number of HCAs and included a very limited number of B-HCAs. That study reported HBP hypointensity in 100% of H-HCAs, consistent with the observation of HBP iso- or hyperintensity in 0% of H-HCAs in the current study. In addition, the earlier meta-analysis reported HBP hypointensity in 75% of I-HCAs, 92% of U-HCAs, and 59% of B-HCAs, varying somewhat from the percentages that would be expected based on the proportions of HBP iso- or hyperintensity in the current study.

H-HCA and I-HCA both have recognized typical features on conventional MRI sequences [46], and according to the EASL 2016 guidelines for the management of benign liver tumors [1], MRI has greater than 90% specificity for these two subtypes. For example, H-HCA typically shows diffuse homogeneous intravoxel lipid, and I-HCA typically shows marked hyperintensity on T2-weighted images and persistent enhancement on delayed phases. In comparison, B-HCA and U-HCA lack characteristic conventional MRI features, further raising concern for the use of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for their differentiation from FNH. Table 5 summarizes characteristic conventional MRI features [47], as well as the proportion of HBP iso- or hyperintensity based on the present meta-analysis, of FNH and of the HCA subtypes.

Traditionally, HBP hypointensity has been considered to be a hallmark of HCA and attributed to a reduction in HCA of membrane carrier molecules, such as organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B3 (OATP1B3), that transport gadoxetic acid into hepatocytes during the HBP [29]. The variation in HBP signal intensity among HCA subtypes may reflect differences in molecular backgrounds [7]. For example, the uniform lack of HBP iso- or hyperintensity in H-HCA can be attributed to a uniform reduction in OATP1B3. On the other hand, in B-HCA, β-catenin mutations (which are present in 30–40% of HCCs [4]) lead to activation of the β-catenin/Wnt pathway, which in turn leads to OATP1B3 over-expression and thus HBP iso- or hyperintensity. Approximately 10–20% of I-HCAs also have β-catenin mutations [48], which may account for potential HBP iso- or hyperintensity in such HCAs as well. These observations support a role for HBP iso- or hyperintensity as an imaging surrogate for malignant potential among HCAs [9, 29]. Indeed, Sciarra et al. [29] reported both HBP iso- or hyperintensity and increased expression of OATP1B3 on pathologic assessment in HCAs with either focal HCC transformation or with a high risk of malignant transformation (i.e., B-HCA).

Gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI is typically used clinically to differentiate FNH from HCA rather than to determine HCA subtype. However, this application of gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI is challenged by the observed association of HBP iso- or hyperintensity with B-HCA (the HCA subtype with highest malignant potential) [15]. Studies have portrayed gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI as a simple and reliable tool for differentiating FNH from HCA because of an expectation of FNH typically showing HBP iso- or hyperintensity and HCA typically showing HBP hypointensity [49–51]. However, such studies did not account for HCA subtypes and may have had high proportions of H-HCA and I-HCA, leading to the high reported diagnostic performance. In the current meta-analysis, after exclusion of HCA subtypes that have characteristic conventional MRI features (H-HCA and I-HCA), HBP iso- or hyperintensity had a specificity of only 65% for differentiating FNH from HCA, which is insufficient to support the clinical use of gadoxetic acid–enhanced HBP MR images for this purpose.

Radiologists should avoid using findings from HBP images in isolation to render a specific diagnosis but rather interpret the HBP signal intensity in combination with other MRI sequences and in consideration of the clinical context to guide recommendations for biopsy of indeterminate lesion. For instance, in a patient without cirrhosis or with a history of cancer, a lesion showing HBP iso- or hyperintensity that also shows hyperenhancement in the arterial phase and isointensity on other sequences is likely FNH and can be observed on follow-up imaging. In comparison, in a patient with cirrhosis, a lesion that shows HBP iso- or hyperintensity and arterial phase hyperenhancement may represent well-differentiated HCC and warrants consideration of biopsy [52].

This study has limitations. First, despite including 28 articles, the study may have been underpowered for some comparisons (e.g., comparison of HBP iso- or hyperintensity between B-HCA and I-HCA), in part reflecting the uncommon nature of some HCA subtypes. Second, all 16 case reports and case series were considered to have high risk of bias in the patient selection domain, and some of the original investigations also showed high risk of bias or high concern of applicability in the evaluated domains. Third, the proportion of HBP iso- or hyperintensity was based on a dichotomous qualitative assessment, although this assessment may be affected by lesion size and heterogeneity. Several studies included in the meta-analysis also explored the use of quantitative assessment of HBP signal intensity to differentiate HCA subtype [26, 27, 31]. Other studies not included in this meta-analysis explored the use of the HBP enhancement pattern to differentiate FNH from either HCA or HCC [53, 54]. For example, Auer et al. [53] reported that 93% of FNHs showed homogeneous HBP hyperintensity, whereas 70% of HCAs that showed HBP iso- or hyperintensity were heterogeneous on HBP images. In addition, Kitao et al. [54] reported that a hyperintense rim with central hypointensity on HBP was exhibited by FNH but not by HCC with HBP hyperintensity. Moreover, a study of gadobenate dimeglumine–enhanced MRI found that apparent HBP iso- or hyperintensity may not reflect hepatocyte-specific contrast agent uptake in the setting of liver steatosis given the liver parenchyma’s altered T1 signal [55], indicating the importance of considering the background liver parenchyma when performing qualitative assessment of HBP signal intensity. Finally, additional HCA subtypes that were incorporated in the 2017 updated HCA classification (i.e., exon 3–mutated B-HCA, exon 7/8–mutated B-HCA, and sonic hedgehog HCA) were not evaluated, reflecting the paucity of relevant literature [3].

In conclusion, the pooled proportion of HBP iso- or hyperintensity was 0% for H-HCA, 11% for U-HCA, 14% for I-HCA, and 59% for B-HCA. HBP iso- or hyperintensity had sensitivity and specificity of 99% and 89%, respectively, for differentiating FNH from all HCA, but these values were 99% and 65%, respectively, for differentiating FNH from U-HCA or B-HCA. A low prevalence of HCA subtypes that most commonly exhibit HBP iso- or hyperintensity has contributed to prior reports of high diagnostic performance of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for differentiation of FNH from HCA. A low specificity of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for differentiating FNH from HCA subtypes other than H-HCA and I-HCA is an important limitation for radiologists to recognize when using HBP images on gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI for liver lesion characterization.

HIGHLIGHTS

Key Finding

  • The proportion of HBP iso- or hyperintensity was 0% among HNF1a-inactivated HCA, 11% among unclassified HCA (U-HCA), 14% among inflammatory HCA, and 59% among β-catenin–activated HCA (B-HCA). HBP iso- or hyperintensity had sensitivity of 99% and specificity of 65% for differentiating FNH from B-HCA and U-HCA.

Importance

  • The low specificity of HBP iso- or hyperintensity for differentiating FNH from certain HCA subtypes is a limitation when applying HBP images for lesion characterization.
