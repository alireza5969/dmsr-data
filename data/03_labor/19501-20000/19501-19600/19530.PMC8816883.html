Abstract

Background:

The use of CRRT for renal support has increased substantially in critically ill children compared to intermittent modalities due to its preferential effects on hemodynamic stability. With the expanding role of CRRT, the quantification of extracorporeal clearance (CLRRT) and the effect on primary pharmacokinetic parameters is of the utmost importance. Within this review we aimed to summarize the current state of the literature and compare published pharmacokinetic analyses of commonly used medications in children receiving CRRT to those who are not.

Methods:

A systematic search of the literature within electronic databases PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library and Web of Science was conducted. Published studies that were included contained relevant information on use of commonly administered medications to children, from neonates to adolescents, receiving CRRT. Pharmacokinetic parameters that were analyzed included Vd, CLtotal, CLRRT, AUC and elimination half-life. Information regarding CRRT circuit, flow rates and membrane components were analyzed to investigate differences in pharmacokinetics between each modality.

Results:

45 studies met final inclusion within this systematic review, totaling 833 pediatric patients, with 586 receiving CRRT. Antimicrobials were the most common pharmacological class represented within the literature, representing 81% (35/43) of studies analyzed. Children receiving CRRT largely had similar Vd and CLtotal to critically ill children not receiving CRRT, suggesting reno-protective dose adjustments may lead to subtherapeutic dosing regimens in these patients. Overall, there was a tendency for hydrophilic agents, with low protein binding to undergo elevated CLtotal in these children. However, results should be interpreted with caution due to the large variability amongst patient populations and heterogeneity with CRRT modalities, flow rates and use of ECMO within studies. This review was able to identify that variation in solute removal, or CRRT modalities, properties (i.e. flow rates) and membrane composition may have differing effects on the pharmacokinetics of commonly administered medications.

Conclusion:

The current state of the literature of medications administered to children receiving CRRT largely focuses on antimicrobials. Significant gaps remain with other commonly used medications such as sedatives and analgesics. Overall reporting of patient clinical characteristics, CRRT settings and circuit composition was poor, with only 10% of articles including all relevant information to assess the impact of CRRT on CLtotal. Changes in pharmacokinetics because of CRRT often required higher than labelled doses, suggesting renally adjusted, or reno-protective doses may lead to subtherapeutic dosing regimens. A thorough understanding of the interplay between patient, drug and CRRT-circuit factors are required to ensure adequate delivery of dosing regimens to this vulnerable population.

BACKGROUND:

1

The incidence of pediatric patients admitted to intensive care units continues to rise in North America, with approximately 200,000 annual admissions within the US.[1,2] Approximately 20% of critically ill children may experience various degrees of multiple organ dysfunction.[1] Multiple organ dysfunction, or MODS, encompasses failure of organ systems such as the respiratory, renal, neurological, hepatic and cardiovascular systems.[3] MODS is largely associated with a severe inflammatory response that is commonly triggered by conditions such as shock, infection, or trauma.[3,4] Of these, renal dysfunction has been shown to be one of the most common and one of the strongest predictors of mortality within a pediatric intensive care unit (PICU).[4]

Recent epidemiological data suggests that renal dysfunction amongst pediatric patients may be as high as 30% and associated with a mortality rate of nearly 15% within a PICU.[4–6] To prevent further complications and reduce mortality associated with renal dysfunction, many of these children will require extracorporeal technologies such as continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT).

Renal replacement therapy (RRT) was originally developed as a method of fluid and solute removal in patients with chronic kidney disease. Due to preferential effects on hemodynamics compared to intermittent modalities, CRRT has become the treatment of choice for patients requiring renal support.[7] Multiple CRRT techniques have been used to aid in fluid and solute removal, including continuous veno-venous hemofiltration (CVVH), continuous veno-venous hemodialysis (CVVHD), and continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF). Depending on the modality chosen, drugs will be cleared differently.

Clearance through a convective process, as seen with CVVH, is largely dependent upon the ultrafiltration rate and independent of molecular size. [8,9] This differs from the diffusion process seen within CVVHD, as efficient clearance depends on molecular weight and the utilization of a concentration gradient for molecules to move from blood to dialysate.[10,11] Where as, CVVHDF combines both mechanisms (diffusion & ultrafiltration) to aid in solute removal.[10,11] The efficiency of drug removal is expected to be CVVHDF>CVVH>CVVHD.[7,8]

With the expanding data supporting the impact of CRRT on survival of critically ill children, the quantification of extracorporeal clearance (CLRRT) has significant implications for alteration in the pharmacokinetic behaviour of medications.[12] Differences in method of RRT, circuit properties such as flow rates or membrane composition, in conjunction with patient clinical status and the physicochemical properties of a drug, may lead to suboptimal pharmacodynamic responses or toxicity based on alterations in volume of distribution (Vd) or total clearance (CLtotal). [9,10,13] For example, the degree of CLRRT is of particular importance for patients experiencing life-threatening infections as the consequences of suboptimal dosing has been shown to negatively impact mortality and increase antimicrobial resistance.[14–16] Current dosing recommendations are guided by a one-size-fits all approach, lacking patient, and circuit specificity.[17] To date there are no systematic reviews investigating the pharmacokinetic parameters for commonly used medications within pediatric patients receiving CRRT. The goal of this systematic review is to summarize the pharmacokinetic data and investigate the quality and validity, with the objective of providing a guide for clinicians to develop optimal dosing regimens for pediatric patients and to identify knowledge gaps for future research.

METHODS

2

The systematic review protocol has been previously registered on PROSPERO, CRD42021250070. [18]

Search Strategy

2.1

A systematic search of the literature within electronic databases PubMed(MEDLINE), Ovid EMBASE, Cochrane Library and Web of Science was conducted with assistance from an information specialist (CC). The search strategy was developed using keywords: pharmacokinetics, drug dosing, pediatrics, and renal replacement therapy within the pediatric patient population from birth to 18 years. Further refining was done to include articles published from 1990 to May 3, 2021. Researchers were alerted to additional results for the search after May 2021 to September 15th, 2021 by automatic email alerts. Articles during this period were screened and selected in a similar manner to the original search. Limitation of publication date was to ensure the search results only included pharmacokinetic analyses with widely used veno-venous CRRT circuits. To maximize inclusion, references of selected publications were reviewed, and all relevant articles were included. The full search strategy for PubMed(MEDLINE)can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Study Selection

2.2

The final search results from each database were imported within the software Covidence (Melbourne, Australia).[19] Two reviewers (SS + BA) independently screened each title and abstract to assess eligibility for inclusion. The systematic review process was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for systematic reviews and interventions.[20] All discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by a third independent reviewer (SD). Studies were included if they met the following pre-defined inclusion criteria: 1) study population of ages 18 years and below, including neonates (0–28 days), infants (29 days – 1 year of age), children (>1–12 years) and adolescents (>12–18 years), 2) those receiving CRRT such as: CVVH, CVVHD, and CVVHDF, 3) studies reporting results of primary pharmacokinetic parameters such as CL (CLtotal & CLRRT), Vd or AUC in patients receiving CRRT. No restrictions on the use of additional extracorporeal technologies such as ECMO were included within this review. Ex-vivo studies investigating the drug-CRRT circuit interaction were included to illustrate the impact on pharmacokinetics. All study designs, including case reports and case series were incorporated within this review. Studies that were excluded were: 1) Non-English publications, 2) animal studies and 3) studies including predominantly adult populations with no clear separation of pediatrics.

Data Extraction

2.3

All extracted data was contained within a pre-populated data extraction sheet developed using Microsoft Excel. Extracted data included study design, sample size, drug, dose, administration route (i.e. enteral or intravenous), and patient demographics including age, body weight (kg), and height (cm). Indicators of clinical status were also extracted if applicable, including serum creatinine (SCr), albumin, bilirubin, liver enzymes and presence or absence of residual renal function. As the study population included children receiving CRRT, all relevant CRRT circuit components were recorded such as: CRRT machine, circuit filter/tubing, including membrane porosity, flow rates (i.e. blood, dialysate, ultrafiltrate) and replacement fluid flow rate (i.e. prefilter vs. post-filter replacement). Additionally, the sieving coefficient (SC) or dialysate saturation constant (SD) was also extracted. This may simply be defined as the ratio between the drug concentration in the effluent fluid to plasma for a CVVH and CVVHD circuit respectively. The presence or absence of ECMO was also noted.

The pharmacokinetic parameters that were extracted included: CLtotal, Vd, AUC, half-life (t ½), elimination rate constant (ke) and peak/trough concentrations (Cmax & Cmin). Pharmacokinetic parameters were recorded as mean/median values as presented in each respective study. Additionally, the method of pharmacokinetic analysis and model structure was included within the extraction. For population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) models, covariates and final model equations were documented if applicable.

Probability of target attainment (PTA) was also recorded. PTA included time above the minimum inhibitory concentration (fT>MIC) for time-dependent antimicrobials, peak-concentration to MIC ratio (Cmax/MIC) for concentration-dependent antimicrobials and AUC/MIC ratio for exposure-dependent antimicrobials. Pharmacodynamic targets for non-antimicrobials were also investigated if reported.

Quality Assessment

2.4

One of the main objectives of this review was to assess the quality of the current literature of pharmacokinetic analyses in children receiving CRRT. The ClinPK quality assessment tool constructed by Kanji et al. was developed to aid clinicians’ ability to assess validity and quality of reporting for pharmacokinetic studies. [21] This 24-item checklist includes criteria such as study rationale, eligibility criteria, coadministration of study drugs, validation of quantitative bioanalytical methods, use of pharmacokinetic software, transparency of extracorporeal technology components and quality of reported results. [21] As a result, this tool was incorporated within the review to allow for better assessment of clinical application.

RESULTS

3

Characteristics of Included Studies and Study Population

3.1

The literature search strategy identified 1713 non-duplicate records. After title and abstract screening, 129 were determined to be eligible for full-text review. Among these, 45 studies met inclusion criteria and included a total of 833 pediatric patients, with 586 receiving CRRT. Only one patient included within the review received peritoneal dialysis. Retrieval process of included studies is shown in Figure 1, in accordance with the PRISMA statement. [22]

Of the 45 included studies, 16 (36%) were prospective, [23–37] 6 (13%) were retrospective, [38–43] 19 (42%) were case reports or case-series, [44–62 24,35,39,42,43] and 4 (9%) were ex-vivo studies.[63–67] Of these 12 (27%) included a PopPK analysis. [24,25,28,30,34–39,42,43] Data on 33 different medications were reported within this analysis, including: 14 antibiotics (vancomycin, tobramycin, daptomycin, meropenem, doripenem, ticarcillin-clavulanic acid, teicoplanin, amikacin, clindamycin, piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, ceftriaxone, ceftolozane-tazobactam, linezolid), 4 antivirals (ribavirin, acyclovir, oseltamivir, peramivir), 4 antifungals (voriconazole, fluconazole, liposomal amphotericin B, caspofungin), 2 anticonvulsants (phenobarbital, phenytoin), 4 vasoactive agents (vasopressin, milrinone, dobutamine, dopamine), and others (clonidine, digoxin, theophylline, midazolam, propafenone). Within this analysis, 20 out of 45 studies (44%) included children <1 year of age. [24,25,27,29–31,34,35,36,37,39–45,47–49,55] The clinical and methodological characteristics of included studies may be found in Table 1.

The specific modality of CRRT used in the patient population was reported in 88% of included studies. All three methods of CRRT (i.e. CVVH, CVVHD, CVVHDF) were represented, with CVVH being the most common in 47% of studies. Information on specific CRRT modality, flow rates and filter components may be found in Table 2. At least one patient received ECMO in approximately half (53%) of the studies, with VA-ECMO being the most common method. To fully assess the impact of CRRT on clearance (CLRRT), an understanding of blood, replacement fluid, dialysate and/or ultrafiltrate fluid flow rates is required. Within the studies incorporating CRRT, blood flow rate was reported in 24 (53%), replacement fluid was reported in 14 (31%), dialysate flow rate was reported in 19 (42%) and ultrafiltrate flow rate was reported in 25 (56%) studies respectively. Only 18 (40%) studies reported all necessary mechanistic properties of CRRT to assess impact on CLRRT. The specific filter used within the CRRT machine was reported in 27 (60%) studies, with the AN 69 polyacrylonitrile filter being the most common.

To assess certain confounding characteristics on the pharmacokinetic alterations within this patient population, information on baseline laboratory values (i.e. creatinine, albumin, liver enzymes), residual renal function and concurrent medications was analyzed. Baseline SCr, albumin and liver enzymes were reported in 53%, 24%, and 29% of studies, respectively. Reported information on residual renal function or urine output was included in 40% of the studies. The inclusion of a comparator group, children not receiving CRRT, was included in 4 (9%) out 45 studies. [29,36,41,56] As a result, to assess comparison of pharmacokinetic parameters in those studies that did not include a comparator, pharmacokinetic studies of age-matched historical controls not receiving CRRT were investigated.

QUALITY ASSESMENT

4

Of the 24-items within the ClinPK checklist, >50% provided an explanation of the study rationale, objectives and hypotheses, eligibility criteria, drug preparation characteristics, pharmacokinetic modelling techniques and formulas for calculating pharmacokinetic variables. All detailed percentages of included studies within this review may be found in Supplementary Table 1. Only 26 of the 45 included studies (58%) provided background information on pharmacokinetic data of the drug used within each respective study. Additionally, 15 out of 45 studies (33%) reported a description of concurrent medications used within study population, therefore limiting the ability to assess for confounding drug-drug interactions. One of the main findings within this review was the paucity of reporting of patient demographics that may account for inter- and intra-patient pharmacokinetic variability. Of the 45 studies, only 16 (38%) described relevant patient demographics within this review. As this review was focused on the pharmacokinetic changes associated with CRRT, reporting of CRRT modality and settings were assessed. Eighteen (40%) studies reported all necessary mechanistic properties of CRRT modality to assess impact on CLRRT and to allow for reproduction of methodology.[26,29,34,39,40,47,49,52–54,57,58,60,63–67]

ALTERATIONS IN PHARMACOKINETIC PARAMETERS

5

Antibiotics

5.1

Penicillins

5.1.1

Ticarcillin-Clavulanic Acid

5.1.1.1

One study within this review included a case-series (n=3) investigating the pharmacokinetics of ticarcillin-clavulanic acid in pediatric patients receiving CRRT. [50] Study investigators demonstrated ticarcillin-clavulanic acid pharmacokinetics with a one-compartment, first order model. [50] Each patient received CVVH with an average ultrafiltration flow rate of 890 mL/h.[50] Two patients within this study received ECMO for a portion of study duration. Mean weight normalized CLtotal was determined to be 0.038 L/h/kg and 0.184 L/h/kg for ticarcillin and clavulanic acid, respectively.[50] Weight normalized CLRRT for ticarcillin and clavulanic acid was 0.022 L/h/kg and 0.049 L/h/kg.[50] The differences in CLRRT between the two molecules may be explained by the increased SC of clavulanic acid compared to ticarcillin (1.69 vs. 0.83). [50]

Total weight normalized clearance for ticarcillin within this population was lower than prior reports of critically ill premature infants (0.047–0.074 L/h/kg) [68–70] and full-term infants and children (0.126–0.150 L/h/kg) not receiving CRRT.[71,72] The recorded Vd is similar to what has been reported in the literature within pediatric patients not receiving CRRT (0.22–0.48 L/kg). [68,70–72] No dosing recommendations were provided by study investigators.

Piperacillin-Tazobactam

5.1.1.2

We identified one case-report analyzing the pharmacokinetics of piperacillin-tazobactam in a 13-year-old pediatric patient receiving CRRT.[56] Due to pre-existing liver failure, molecular adsorbent recirculating system (MARS) therapy was administered in conjunction with CVVHD to aid in fluid and albumin filtration.[56,73] Piperacillin-tazobactam was administered as an intermittent infusion over 30 minutes with a dose of 48 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours. [56] CLtotal of piperacillin-tazobactam was determined prior to MARS and CVVHD, while on MARS/CVVHD and during CVVHD only. CLtotal was 0.13 L/h/kg, 0.39–0.45 L/h/kg and 0.18 L/h/kg during each analytical period.[56] Interestingly, CLtotal during MARS/CVVHD within this study was similar to prior analyses of critically ill patients with augmented renal clearance (ARC) or burns (0.18–0.299 L/kg/h).[74,75] CLtotal of piperacillin-tazobactam in this patient receiving MARS/CVVHD was also similar to healthy infants and children (0.198–0.33 L/h/kg).[76] Investigators suggested that a piperacillin-tazobactam dose of 80 mg/kg/dose every 6 hours would be required to ensure 50% fT> 4xMIC while receiving MARS/CVVHD and 100% fT> 4xMIC while receiving CVVHD alone for the treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MIC 16 μg/mL). [56]

Cephalosporins

5.1.2

Cefepime

5.1.2.1

The investigation by Stitt et al. was a retrospective study analyzing the pharmacokinetics of cefepime in pediatric patients receiving CVVHDF.[40] Cefepime was administered as an intermittent infusion with doses of 48–64 mg/kg/dose every 6–12 hours.[40] CVVHDF settings may be found in Table 2. CLtotal within this study was determined to be 0.056 L/h/kg, with a Vd of 0.14 L/kg and an elimination t ½ of 1.93 h.[40] The weight normalized CLtotal in this study was slightly reduced compared to previous investigations in neonates (0.07–0.20 L/h/kg) [77–80], and critically ill infants and children not receiving CRRT (0.19 L/h/kg).[81] In comparison, the Vd determined within this study was significantly lower than those recorded in neonates (Vd 0.41–0.43 L/kg),[78,79] and infants and children (Vd 0.33 L/kg).[81] Study investigators suggested that doses > 50 mg/kg/dose every 12 hours are required to achieve a pharmacodynamic target of 100% fT> 1–4xMIC, assuming MIC of 8 μg/mL for Pseudomonas aeruginosa.[40]

Ceftolozane-Tazobactam

5.1.2.2

Butragueño-Laiseca et al. reported the results of a pharmacokinetic analysis of 3 pediatric patients receiving ceftolozane-tazobactam.[36] Within this analysis, an intra-study comparison was conducted between patients with sepsis and no AKI, sepsis with AKI and sepsis requiring CVVHDF support. Ceftolozane-tazobactam was administered as an IV intermittent infusion of 40 mg/kg every 6 hours, 36 mg/kg every 8 hours and 30 mg/kg every 8 hours for each respective patient.[36] CLRenal was 0.88 L/h, 0.27 L/h and 0.39 L/h in each patient. VSS was 2-fold higher in the patient without renal dysfunction compared to CVVHDF (4.39 L vs. 1.91 L).[36] Interestingly, elimination t ½ was consistent between the patient receiving CVVHDF and that who didn’t. Ceftolozane-tazobactam was determined to have a SC of 1.06 within the CVVHDF circuit.

Pharmacokinetic parameters reported in this study differed slightly than those determined in a single IV dose pharmacokinetic study conducted by Bradley et al.[82] Study authors determined that doses of 35 mg/kg IV Q8H, 10 mg/kg IV Q8H and 30 mg/kg IV Q8H would be sufficient to ensure 40% fT>MIC, assuming a MIC of 4 μg/mL for Pseudomonas aeruginosa.[36] However, due to the small sample size included within this analysis, further research is needed to confirm these findings.

Carbapenems

5.1.3

Meropenem

5.1.3.1

Meropenem was the most studied drug identified within the literature, with 7 studies included within this review. Within these 7 studies, 5 were prospective pharmacokinetic analyses [26,28,29,34,35] and 2 were case reports. [47,53] A total of 363 patients were analyzed, with each study including various diagnoses with sepsis being the most common reason for admission. CLtotal of meropenem ranged from 0.09–0.474 L/kg/h within these patients across all CRRT modalities. In the studies that reported Vd it was quite heterogenous, ranging from 0.194 to 0.59 L/kg. Cies et al. reported the pharmacokinetics of meropenem administered as a 10 mg/kg continuous infusion to a 10-day old neonate weighing 2.8 kg receiving CVVHDF.[47] The authors concluded that a loading dose of 40 mg/kg, followed by a continuous infusion of 10 mg/kg/h would provide 100% fT>MIC up to the Pseudomonas aeruginosa breakpoint of 16 μg/mL.[47] The need for extended interval administration of meropenem in patients receiving CRRT was also reported by Saito et al. [53] The child within this report, received CVVHD+ECMO with a blood flow rate of 50–80 mL/min, and a dialysate flow rate of 820–1500 mL/h. [53] It was determined that meropenem 200–300 mg/kg/day every 8 hours as a 1-hour infusion or meropenem 200–300 mg/kg/day every 8 hours as a 3-hour infusion would be necessary to achieve a pharmacodynamic target for Escherichia coli (MIC= 1μg/mL).

A popPK model of meropenem was simulated for pediatric patients receiving CRRT and then further validated by a prospective, pharmacokinetic analysis by Nehus et al. [34] Within this simulation, meropenem was administered as an intermittent infusion of 20 mg/kg/dose every 8–12 hours to patients <1–18 years of age. All modalities of CRRT were simulated, with 49% receiving CVVHD, 27% receiving CVVH and 24% receiving CVVHDF.[34] A popPK, four-compartment model was used in dosing simulations, final popPK model may be found in Table 2. To obtain the pharmacodynamic target, it was determined that children (<5kg) receiving CRRT should receive meropenem 20 mg/kg every 8 hours and children (>5 kg) should receive meropenem 20 mg/kg every 12 hours.

These results were further validated in a prospective pharmacokinetic study (n=7) with participants receiving an average meropenem dose of 18.6 mg/kg/dose.[26] Two subjects received CVVH, while the remaining received CVVHDF for renal dysfunction and fluid overload.[26] Administering a dose of meropenem 20 mg/kg every 12 hours achieved a pharmacodynamic target of 100% fT>MIC in 5/7 patients for Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MIC= 4 μg/mL.[26]

Rapp et al. conducted a prospective, pharmacokinetic analysis of 40 critically ill pediatric patients receiving meropenem. [35] A two compartment, first order elimination popPK model was used to describe the pharmacokinetics.[35] In those who did not receive CRRT, more than half were determined to have ARC (eGFR >130 mL/min/1.73 m2).[35,83] CRRT was determined to be an influential covariate for CLtotal of meropenem in those children receiving therapy.[35] Upon popPK simulations, the authors concluded that intermittent infusions of meropenem 20 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours is adequate for treating bacteria with a MIC <2 μg/mL. [35] However, for bacteria with an elevated MIC (>4 μg/mL), meropenem should be administered as a continuous infusion of 60 mg/kg/day in pediatric patients receiving CRRT.[35]

The fourth prospective, pharmacokinetic analysis of meropenem in pediatrics receiving CRRT identified within this review was conducted by Tan et al.[28] A two-compartment model was used to determine meropenem pharmacokinetics.[28] Meropenem was administered as an intermittent infusion of 20–40 mg/kg/dose every 12 hours. Four children received CVVH, while 5 received CVVHDF.[28] Additionally, 4 children received concurrent ECMO during the study period.[28] Mean CLRRT of 1.4 L/h was determined in these patients, which was similar to that recorded by Nehus et al. (1.9 L/h). [28] The average SC of meropenem for CRRT was 0.96, demonstrating the ability for meropenem to pass across the CRRT circuit membrane.[28] The authors concluded that meropenem 20 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours administered as a 4 hour intermittent infusion or meropenem 40 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours as a 2 hour infusion would be sufficient to attain pharmacodynamic target in these patients receiving CRRT with an ultrafiltration rate of 1952 mL/1.73m2/h.[28]

Wang et al. analyzed 27 pediatric patients receiving meropenem 20 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours as a 1-hour intermittent infusion.[29] Analyses were conducted in patients receiving CVVHDF, ECMO or neither.[29] CLtotal was similar between the 3 groups, 15.73 L/h in those children receiving CVVHDF, 11.59 L/h in those children on ECMO, and 13.51 L/h with no extracorporeal therapy.[29] Interestingly, Vd was decreased in patients receiving CVVHDF (19.42 L) compared to those on ECMO (31.67 L) and those not receiving ECLS (34.41 L). [29] Elimination t ½ of meropenem was similar between the 3 cohorts. The authors concluded that meropenem 20 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours administered as a 1-hour infusion is appropriate for critically ill children receiving CVVHDF, ECMO or neither. [29]

Several pharmacokinetic analyses have been conducted with meropenem in critically ill children not receiving CRRT. There is substantial heterogeneity within the literature in terms of primary pharmacokinetic parameters. CLtotal for meropenem in neonates has been shown to be 0.24 L/kg/h, [84] and 0.33–0.43 L/kg/h in critically ill children. [85–90] While Vd of meropenem in neonates has been shown to be 0.55 L/kg, [84] and 0.20–0.78 L/kg in critically ill children. [85–90] CLtotal and Vd of meropenem in critically ill children appear to be similar regardless if CRRT is administered or not, suggesting higher than labelled doses may be required to ensure pharmacodynamic target attainment.

Doripenem

5.1.3.2

The use of doripenem in an 11-year-old pediatric patient was investigated by Cies et al. [62] Dorpienem was administered as a 2.5 mg/kg/h continuous infusion for the treatment of pneumonia due to multi-drug resistant organisms. At the time of CVVH administration, this patient maintained residual renal function with a urine output of 0.46 mL/min/kg. CLtotal of doripenem in this patient was determined to be 10.5 mL/kg/min.[62] Administering doripenem as a 2.5 mg/kg/h continuous infusion ensured 100% fT>MIC for bacteria with a MIC <4 mg/L.[62] CLtotal of doripenem in this patient was over 2-fold of what has been previously reported in pediatric patients not receiving CRRT (4.4–4.8 mL/min/kg). [91] The results of this case report demonstrate that CLtotal is substantially increased with CVVH and residual renal function, therefore alterations in dosing administration may be needed to ensure pharmacodynamic target attainment. Further studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

Aminoglycosides

5.1.4

Tobramycin

5.1.4.1

Despite the widespread use of tobramycin in neonates and infants, only one case-report was included within this review analyzing the pharmacokinetics in a 4-month-old anuric infant.[45] Tobramycin was administered as 1.8–2.5 mg/kg/dose, with the interval guided by therapeutic drug monitoring. As a result of renal dysfunction, CVVH was started with a blood flow rate of 15 mL/min and an ultrafiltration flow rate of 20–30 mL/min.[45] CLRRT of tobramycin was determined to be 0.32–0.91 mL/min, while elimination t ½ decreased from 40–44 hours to 20–23 hours during CVVH.[45] The authors determined that 48–66% of the tobramycin dose was removed during a 24-hour CVVH study period.[45] Tobramycin elimination t ½ in healthy pediatrics has been reported to be 2.37 h,[92] and 1.2–2.32 h in adults. [92–94] Elimination t ½ of tobramycin has also been shown to be increased in critically ill adults not receiving CRRT (2.9–5.4 h). [95,96]

Glycopeptides

5.1.5

Vancomycin

5.1.5.1

Four studies within this review analyzed the pharmacokinetics of vancomycin in pediatrics receiving CRRT. These 4 studies included 179 patients, ranging from ages 4 months to 14.5 years.[39,41,45,49] Two studies were retrospective, [39,41] while the other two were case-reports/case-series.[45,49] Only one study within this analysis included patients receiving ECMO concurrently with CRRT.[41] Three studies included pharmacokinetic analyses, [39,41,45] while one study results were based on data from trough concentrations. [49]

Armstrong et al. conducted a pharmacokinetic analysis in a 4-month old neonate receiving vancomycin 11–15 mg/kg/dose as an intermittent infusion.[45] Due to renal dysfunction, CVVH was initiated with a blood flow rate of 15 mL/min and an ultrafiltration rate of 20–30 mL/min.[45] CLRRT of vancomycin was determined to be 0.27–0.80 mL/min with an elimination t ½ of 20–24 h. [45] The SC of vancomycin within the CVVH membrane filter was 0.86–1.55.[45] The authors determined that 35% of the vancomycin dose was removed by CVVH. [45]

A novel vancomycin administration approach was investigated in a case-series of 11 pediatric patients by Cies et al. [49] Within this study, a 15–20 mg/kg loading dose followed by the addition of vancomycin into the CRRT solution at a final concentration of 18–30 mg/L was administered continuously. All three CRRT modalities were used within the analysis, with 4 patients receiving CVVH, 2 patients receiving CVVHD and 8 patients receiving CVVHDF. [49] Average vancomycin concentration at the time of analysis was 22.3 (±3.3) μg/mL, with 8/11 patients meeting therapeutic target within 8 hours.[49] The authors concluded that the application of administering vancomycin within the CRRT solution at a final concentration of 18–30 mg/L may be safe and effective in pediatric patients receiving CRRT, however further studies are needed to confirm these results. [49]

Moffet et al. conducted a retrospective, pharmacokinetic analysis of 138 patients receiving vancomycin while on CVVHDF.[39] Within this analysis, the mean vancomycin dose administered as a 1-hour intermittent infusion was 14.3 mg/kg/dose. A popPK, two compartment model was used to describe vancomycin pharmacokinetics within this population. [39] CLtotal of vancomycin was 2.29 L/h, with Vd of central and peripheral compartments 81 L and 550 L, respectively. [39] Dialysate and ultrafiltration rates were found to be significant covariates for CLtotal upon popPK analysis. It was determined that vancomycin 40–50 mg/kg/day every 8–12 hours dosed upon fat-free mass (FFM) would be necessary to achieve an AUC/MIC ratio ≥400 and a vancomycin trough < 20 mg/L in pediatric patients receiving CVVHDF. [39] Final popPK model and significant covariates for CL and Vd may be found in Table 2.

The final vancomycin pharmacokinetic study included within this review investigated 29 critically ill pediatric patients receiving extracorporeal therapy.[41] This analysis included an intrastudy comparison of 3 groups, those receiving ECMO with no AKI/CRRT (1), those with AKI receiving ECMO ± CRRT (2) and those receiving ECMO and CRRT without AKI (3). [41] Vancomycin was administered as 40 mg/kg/day every 6 hours, 40 mg/kg/day every 8 hours and 40 mg/kg/day every 8 hours for the 3 respective groups.[41] CLtotal of vancomycin was determined to be 1.67 mL/min/kg, 0.83 mL/min/kg and 1.17 mL/min/kg in the 3 groups, while Vd was 0.73 L/kg, 1.16 L/kg, 0.88 L/kg.[41] Final adjusted doses of vancomycin 40 mg/kg/day every 6 hours, 20 mg/kg/day every 12 hours and 40 mg/kg/day every 6 hours for each respective group to ensure an AUC/MIC ≥ 400. [41]

The pharmacokinetics of vancomycin in critically ill pediatric patients has been widely studied in the literature due to its broad spectrum of activity against gram-positive bacterial infections. CLtotal in critically ill pediatric patients has been shown to be quite heterogenous. With a CLtotal of 0.07–0.117 L/kg/h in patients with septic shock, [97,98] 0.11–0.19 L/kg/h in patients with cardiogenic shock and/or post-cardiac surgery, [99–101] 0.05–0.18 L/kg/h in pediatric oncology patients, [102,103] and 3.24 L/kg/h in critically ill patients with ARC.[104] Vd of vancomycin may also change depending on patient population, as for critically ill pediatric patients the Vd may range from 0.40–0.91 L/kg, [97–100,104] with a slightly higher Vd in critically ill oncology pediatric patients of 1.03–1.04 L/kg. [102,103] The identified studies within this analysis suggest that CLtotal and Vd of vancomycin may be similar among critically ill pediatric patients, regardless of CRRT. Use of vancomycin should be guided by TDM to ensure efficacy and minimize toxicity.

Lincosamides

5.1.6

Clindamycin

5.1.6.1

This review identified only one case-report analyzing the pharmacokinetics of clindamycin administered to a 14-year-old pediatric patient receiving CRRT. [59] Within this study, clindamycin, at an unrecorded dose, was administered to a patient with septic shock receiving concomitant CVVH and VV-ECMO. The pharmacokinetics were described with a one-compartment model, with a CLtotal of 3.6–15.5 L/h.[59] The authors concluded that no dose adjustments for clindamycin are necessary for pediatric patients receiving CVVH + ECMO. [59] CLtotal within this analysis is consistent to what has been shown in pediatric pharmacokinetic studies of the same age not receiving extracorporeal therapy (4.17–14.25 L/h). [105]

Oxazolidinones

5.1.7

Linezolid

5.1.7.1

A recent popPK analysis by Yang et al. investigated the pharmacokinetics of IV linezolid in pediatric patients with suspected or confirmed infection due to multi-drug resistant gram-positive cocci.[37] 63 children were included, with 15 receiving CRRT and 2 on ECMO. No information was reported on CRRT modality, flow rates, or filters used within this study. A dose of 10 mg/kg three times per day was given to patients <12 years of age (n=57) and 600 mg twice daily for children 12–18 years (n=6). The pharmacokinetics of linezolid were described with a two-compartment, first order model, with weight and AST being significant covariates for CLtotal. CRRT and ECMO were included within popPK model analysis, however, were shown to be insignificant covariates on linezolid pharmacokinetics. The final popPK model may be found in Table 2. CLtotal and VC were estimated to be 2.34 L/h and 5.22 L, respectively.[37] CLtotal and Vc estimates within this study were increased compared to those reported by an alternative popPK analysis in children of similar age.[106] Within this study, CLtotal and Vd were 1.31 L/h and 4.24 L, respectively.[106] Characteristics of critical illness were not considered within this analysis, as such future studies are needed to determine the influence of critical illness, CRRT and/or ECMO on the pharmacokinetic parameters of linezolid within pediatric patients.

Antivirals

5.2

Ribavarin

5.2.1

Two case-reports were identified within this review describing the pharmacokinetics of ribavirin in pediatric patients receiving CRRT.[44,55] In each case, CVVH and ECMO were administered to the patient for renal and respiratory support.[44,55] Ribavarin was administered as 5 mg/kg/dose intermittent infusion every 6 hours to a 10 year old pediatric patient within Aebi et al. [44] CLRRT of CVVH was determined to be 0.046 L/kg/h with a Vd at steady state of 1.37 L/kg.[44] As 39% of the ribavirin dose was eliminated by CVVH+ECMO, the authors concluded that no dosing adjustments are necessary in patients receiving extracorporeal therapy.[44]

Within the case-report conducted by Sargel et al., a higher dose of ribavirin was administered to this 5-week-old patient. [55] CLtotal within this analysis was 0.31–0.76 L/kg/h.[55] CLRRT, Vd and elimination t ½ were calculated to be 0.12 L/kg/h, 2.92–5.1 L/kg, and 2.68–10.7 h respectively.[55] The sieving coefficient of ribavirin was 1.17, slightly higher than the value recorded by Aebi et al.[55]

There is a paucity of pharmacokinetic data within the literature analyzing ribavirin in critically ill pediatric patients. CLtotal for ribavirin in pediatric patients being treated for viral encephalitis and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infections were shown to be 0.29 L/kg/h and 0.39 L/kg/h respectively.[107,108] Vd in patients receiving CRRT + ECMO in the analysis by Sargel et al. were consist with children not receiving extracorporeal therapy (5.1 L/kg vs. 4.64–7.7 L/kg). [55,107,108] Elimination t ½ was also similar in the analysis by Sargel et al. compared to young children not on extracorporeal therapy (10.7 h vs. 10.8–18.6 h). [55,107,108]

Acyclovir

5.2.2

One case-report investigated the pharmacokinetics of acyclovir in a critically ill pediatric patient receiving CVVH/CVVHDF in addition to ECMO. [48] Acyclovir was initially started as an intermittent infusion of 30 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours, then subsequently changed to be administered continuously within the CRRT dialysate solution to a final concentration of 5.5 μg/mL.[48] Plasma concentrations of acyclovir were determined to be 8.4 mg/L and 5.3 mg/L at 24 h and 72 h respectively.[48] Administering acyclovir as a continuous infusion within the dialysate solution may be safe and effective in pediatric patients receiving CRRT + ECMO, however further studies are needed to confirm these findings.[48]

Oseltamivir

5.2.3

Two case-reports and one prospective, pharmacokinetic study were included within this analysis. [32,51,61] Karsch et al. investigated the use of oral (75 mg twice daily) and intravenous (100 mg twice daily) oseltamivir in a 9-year old patient receiving CVVHDF and ECMO.[51] The ultrafiltration rate for CVVHDF administered to this patient was 1.2 L/m2/h.[51] AUC0–12 was 1070 ng∙h/mL for oseltamivir and 47,250 ng∙h/mL for oseltamivir carboxylate (OSC).[51] The average SC during the sampling period of oseltamivir was 1.39 and 1.44 for OSC.[51] Despite target concentrations for oseltamivir not being well defined, the authors concluded that oral doses of 75 mg twice daily and intravenous doses of 100 mg twice daily for oseltamivir may be necessary to ensure antiviral activity in children receiving extracorporeal support.[51]

A similar analysis was conducted by Eyler et al. in a 6-year-old pediatric patient receiving CVVHD and ECMO.[61] Oseltamivir was administered as an oral dose of 90 mg (4 mg/kg) twice daily. CVVHD was administered using the AN-69 filter membrane with a dialysate flow rate of 1000 mL/h and a mean ultrafiltration rate of 304 mL/h.[61] Non-compartmental methods were used to describe the pharmacokinetics of oseltamivir and OSC. CLtotal of oseltamivir and OSC was 0.33 L/kg/h and 0.23 L/kg/h respectively.[61] CLRRT was 0.07 L/kg/h and 0.06 L/kg/h for oseltamivir and OSC.[61] AUC0–12 for oseltamivir was calculated to be 30.5 ng∙h/mL, while OSC was 905 ng∙h/mL, significantly lower than that analyzed by Karsch et al. and Wildschut et al.[32,51,61] Oseltamivir and OSC saturation coefficients for CVVHD were 0.99 and 0.85 respectively.[61] Based these results, it was recommended that oral oseltamivir 90 mg twice daily should be given to pediatric patients receiving CVVHD and ECMO.[61]

The pharmacokinetics of oseltamivir in patients receiving organ support identified within this review mirror previous pediatric pharmacokinetic reports. An analysis conducted by Oo et al. investigated the pharmacokinetics of oseltamivir suspension (2 mg/kg) administered to children (1–12y) and adolescents (13–18y). [109] CLtotal in this analysis was 0.63 L/kg/h for OSC in children and 0.32 L/kg/h in adolescents, consistent with Eyler et.al.[61,109] Elimination t ½ of OSC in pediatrics not receiving organ support are also similar to those that do, with an elimination t½ of 7.8 h in children and 8.1 h in adolescents. [109]

Peramivir

5.2.4

Dillon et al. reported the pharmacokinetics of peramivir in a 17-year old pediatric patient receiving CVVHDF. [58] Throughout hospital stay the patient experienced significant renal and respiratory dysfunction due to septic shock associated with H1N1 influenza.[58] CLtotal in this patient was 0.032 L/kg/h, with a Vd 0.32 L/kg.[58] Elimination t ½ of peramivir was 11.6 h.[58] The case series conducted by Cies et al. analyzed the pharmacokinetics of peramivir in 11 patients admitted to the PICU. [110] Median CLtotal was 0.31 L/kg/h, with a 0.58 L/kg Vd and a 1.7 h elimination t ½. [110] These results are slightly different than those determined by a popPK analysis in infants and children by Sugaya et.al. [111] CLtotal, and Vd from the final model were 0.516 L/kg/h and 0.194 L/kg, respectively, [111] suggesting CLtotal of peramivir is significantly lower in pediatric patients receiving CVVHDF. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Antifungals

5.3

Azoles

5.3.1

Voriconazole

5.3.1.1

Brüggeman et al. reported the pharmacokinetics of voriconazole in a 5-year old patient receiving concurrent CVVH and VV-ECMO for an invasive aspergillus infection.[46] Voriconazole (IV) 7 mg/kg twice daily was started and further increased to 14 mg/kg twice daily based on TDM.[46] Information regarding CVVH prescription was not reported within this analysis. Based on the plasma concentrations, a non-compartmental pharmacokinetic analysis of voriconazole was conducted. CLtotal was shown to be 0.22 L/h/kg, within an elimination t ½ of 4.7 h. [46] AUC0–12 of voriconazole in this patient was 64 mg∙h/L.[46] The authors determined that pediatric patients receiving CRRT+ECMO should receive a voriconazole dose of 10–14 mg/kg twice daily to achieve target trough (Cmin) concentrations > 1mg/L.[46] TDM is recommended to ensure efficacy and safety.

Several PopPK models have been constructed to describe the pharmacokinetics in pediatric patients, including critically ill and immunocompromised.[112–114] CLtotal identified within these analyses are quite heterogenous, ranging from 0.09–0.582 L/h/kg.[112–114] Elimination t ½ closely resembled that determined by Brüggeman et al., with a range of 2.41–2.45 hr for voriconazole. [112,113] As Vd was not reported by Brüggeman et al., no comparison can be made, however due to the use of ECMO in this patient one would assume an increased Vd due to the effects of ECMO on lipophilic agents (logP voriconazole = 1.82). [115] Further analyses in pediatric patients receiving organ support are needed to confirm if higher doses are required to achieve pharmacodynamic targets.

Fluconazole

5.3.1.2

Fluconazole was the most studied antifungal agent in pediatric patients receiving CRRT identified within this review. One case report, [52] and 2 prospective analyses, [30,31] totaling 51 patients were analyzed. Oualha et al. reported the pharmacokinetics of a 17-year old patient receiving fluconazole IV 600 mg once daily as a 30-minute intermittent infusion.[52] As the patient developed anuric renal dysfunction, CVVHD was started with an ultrafiltration rate of 90 mL/h.[52] CLtotal and CLRRT of fluconazole in this patient were 2.79 L/h and 1.64 L/h, respectively. [52] Vd at steady state was shown to be 30 L, with an elimination t ½ of 7.4 h. [52] The authors concluded that a fluconazole loading dose of 900 mg IV, followed by maintenance doses of 600 mg IV twice daily would be required to achieve target attainment in pediatric patients receiving CVVHD for Candida infections with an MIC >2 mg/L. [52]

The first prospective, pharmacokinetic analysis conducted by Watt et al., investigated fluconazole use in infants (<120 post-natal days; median GA 37 w). [31] Fluconazole was administered as a 25 mg/kg IV dose over 2-hours for prophylaxis and 12 mg/kg IV once daily over 1-hour for known or suspected fungal infection.[31] All patients were treated with ECMO, while only 2 received CVVH and 1 received CVVHD. Median CLtotal of fluconazole in those who received one dose was 0.017 L/h/kg, and 0.022 L/h/kg in those were received multiple doses.[31] Vd and elimination t ½ were similar between the two groups.[31] The pharmacokinetics of fluconazole were described with a one-compartment model, with 7/9 infants achieving 50% T>MIC for prophylaxis.[31] The authors concluded that fluconazole 25 mg/kg IV weekly would be sufficient for pharmacodynamic target attainment in prevention of fluconazole infections in patients receiving organ support. [31]

The second prospective, pharmacokinetic analysis conducted by Watt et al., utilized prospective data from 3 open-label pharmacokinetic studies (n=40) in patients receiving ECMO ± CRRT.[30] Fluconazole was administered as a 25 mg/kg IV dose for prophylaxis, and a 25 mg/kg loading dose, followed by a 12 mg/kg daily maintenance IV dose for treatment of confirmed or suspected fungal infections. [30] Twenty-one patients received ECMO support, while only 7 received CRRT. A one-compartment popPK model was used to describe fluconazole pharmacokinetics within this population. [30] The final model may be found in Table 2. CLtotal (0.018 L/kg/h) was similar in children irrespective of ECMO use.[30] Patients who received ECMO within this analysis had a slightly larger Vd compared to those who didn’t (1.3 L/kg vs. 0.93 L/kg). [30] The authors concluded that a loading dose of 35 mg/kg, followed by maintenance doses of 12 mg/kg once daily would be required to achieve pharmacodynamic target attainment within this population. [30] Caution should be made when extrapolating these results to children receiving CRRT±ECMO due to the low number of children receiving CRRT support.

Several analyses have reported the pharmacokinetics of fluconazole in neonates, infants and children. CLtotal ranges from 0.011–0.0312 L/kg/h in neonates, [116] 0.015–0.0378 L/kg/h in infants, [117–119] and 1.164–1.368 L/kg/h in children.[119] The Vd of fluconazole also ranges depending on age, with 1.17–1.84 L/kg in neonates, [116,119] 0.95–1.05 L/kg in infants, [116–119] and 0.70–0.95 L/kg in children. [116,119] Caution should be applied when interpreting these comparisons due to the low number of children receiving CRRT identified within this review.

Anticonvulsants

5.4

Phenobarbital

5.4.1

Phenobarbital was the only anticonvulsant identified within this review administered to patients receiving CRRT. This review included 2 case-reports, [57,60] 2 retrospective analyses, [42,43] and 1 prospective analysis,[25] including a total of 87 patients. CVVHDF was the most common CRRT modality used, while 3 studies included patients receiving concurrent ECMO. [25,42,43,57,60] Suzuki et al. presented the pharmacokinetics of a 9-year old patient receiving phenobarbital for acute encephalopathy.[60] CVVHDF was administered to this patient for renal dysfunction with a blood flow rate of 60 mL/min, replacement fluid flow rate of 240 mL/h, a dialysate flow rate of 1000 mL/h and an ultrafiltration rate of 1300 mL/h.[60] CLtotal and CLRRT in this patient were 1.11 L/h and 0.85 L/h, respectively.[60] These results are similar to those determined by Rosenborg et al. in a patient receiving CVVHD.[57] The Vd of phenobarbital was 0.95 L/kg, with an elimination t ½ of 14.8 h. [60] The authors recommended that a phenobarbital dose of 20 mg/kg/day was needed to maintain therapeutic concentrations of 10–30 μg/mL in this patient receiving CVVHDF.

Thibault et al. conducted a retrospective, pharmacokinetic analysis of phenobarbital administered to neonates and infants receiving ECMO±CRRT. [43] Thirty-five patients were included, with a median PNA of 14 days and a median PMA of 40.4 weeks.[43] Only 5 patients received CVVHDF. Phenobarbital was administered with a loading dose of 10–60 mg/kg, followed by a maintenance dose of 3–8 mg/kg/day every 12 hours.[43] A one-compartment, popPK model was constructed to describe the pharmacokinetics of phenobarbital.[43] CLtotal was calculated to be 0.011 L/h/kg in patients receiving ECMO and 0.069 L/h/kg in those receiving ECMO + CVVHDF.[43] Vd of phenobarbital in this population was 1.12 L/kg, similar to neonates post-cardiac surgery receiving ECMO±CVVHDF (0.95L/kg).[42,43] Based on popPK dosing simulations, to achieve a phenobarbital trough (Cmin) concentration of 20–40 mg/L, loading dose of 40 mg/kg, followed by a maintenance dose of 40 mg/kg/day divided every 6 hours is required in patients receiving ECMO+CVVHDF. [43]

The only prospective analysis identified within this review was conducted by Michaličková et al. [25] Only one patient received CRRT, while all 13 received ECMO therapy.[25] A one-compartment, popPK model was constructed to illustrate the pharmacokinetics of phenobarbital within this population.[25] CLtotal showed a time-dependent increase in CLtotal, ranging from 0.003–0.01 L/h/kg with a Vd of 0.85 L/kg for a typical neonate with median body weight 3.21 kg and median PNA of 2 days while on ECMO. [25] A loading dose of 20 mg/kg followed by a maintenance dose of 4 mg/kg/day divided twice daily, increasing by 0.25 mg/kg every 12 hours was required to maintain phenobarbital concentrations of 15–40 mg/L. [25]

The pharmacokinetics of phenobarbital in pediatric patients receiving CRRT±ECMO demonstrate that CLtotal may increase in comparison to those who are not. CLtotal in hospitalized pediatrics has been shown to be 0.02 L/h in neonates, [120,121] and 0.37–0.67 L/h in young infants.[122,123] Vd of phenobarbital in infants not receiving organ support (0.64–0.98 L/kg) is similar to pediatric patients within this analysis.[122,123] Caution should be applied when interpreting these results due to the limited number of patients receiving CRRT. Dosing of phenobarbital should be guided by TDM, however more frequent and higher dosing may be needed in some cases to achieve therapeutic targets due to the impact of CRRT±ECMO on the pharmacokinetics.

Vasoactive Agents

5.5

Vasopressin

5.5.1

Baird published the only study of vasopressin administered to pediatric patients receiving CRRT.[23] Vasopressin was administered to these patients as a continuous infusion of 0.0003–0.0008 iu/kg/min.[23] Three patients received CVVH, while the remaining received CVVHDF.[23] The average effluent flow rate of CRRT used within this study was 30 mL/min. [23] CLRRT for vasopressin was 48 mL/min, similar to the effluent flow rate administered to this study cohort. [23] The SC of vasopressin was 1.1.[23] This analysis suggested vasopressin CLtotal mimics that of the effluent flow rate delivered to pediatric patients receiving CRRT. Higher doses may be required to elicit pharmacodynamic response based on increasing effluent flow rates.

Milrinone

5.5.2

The pharmacokinetics of milrinone were described in one retrospective analysis conducted by Gist et al.[38] Eleven patients with AKI were included within this analysis, however only 3 received CRRT.[38] Children within this cohort received a continuous milrinone infusion of 0.25–0.75 μg/kg/min.[38] There were no significant differences of CLtotal between those children receiving CRRT and those who did not (5.3±3.4 L/h/70kg vs. 5.3±1.4 L/h/70kg; p=0.20). [38] CLtotal was significantly reduced compared to previously published reports of critically ill children without AKI/CRRT (15.9–18.9 L/h/70kg), [124,125] demonstrating the impact of renal dysfunction on milrinone CLtotal.

Dopamine & Dobutamine

5.5.3

The study published by Freter et al. investigated the pharmacokinetics of dopamine and dobutamine in 3 critically ill pediatrics with renal dysfunction and cardiac instability.[33] Each patient received CVVH, with an ultrafiltration flow rate of 10–100 mL/h.[33] Dopamine and dobutamine were administered as continuous infusions of 5.0–25.0 μg/kg/min. Mean CLtotal for dopamine and dobutamine in this study was 25.30 mL/min/kg and 48.20 mL/min/kg respectively.[33] While CLRRT for dopamine was 0.078 mL/min/kg and 0.036 mL/min/kg for dobutamine.[33] Minimal drug was extracted by CVVH within this analysis (0.56% and 0.13%). [33] The SC within the CVVH system was 0.92 for dopamine and 0.59 for dobutamine.[33]

CLtotal of dopamine within this study was significantly decreased to that observed by Eldadah et al.[126] Within this study, CLtotal of dopamine in children admitted to a PICU ranged from 151 mL/kg/min in those with renal and hepatic dysfunction to 949 mL/min/kg in those with normal organ function.[126] CLtotal of dobutamine identified by Freter et al. was similar to what has been reported in pediatric patients not receiving CRRT (40–130 mL/min/kg). [127]

α-Adrenergic Receptor Agonist

5.6

Clonidine

5.6.1

Kleiber et al. conducted a prospective, pharmacokinetic analysis of 22 pediatric patients requiring clonidine for sedation within the PICU.[24] Clonidine was administered as a 1–2 μg/kg bolus, followed by a continuous infusion of 0.1–1.0 μg/kg/h. [24] All patients included within this analysis received VV-ECMO, with 20/22 children receiving concurrent CRRT.[24] CLtotal and Vd were determined to be 30 L/h/70 kg and 454 L/70kg respectively.[24] These results suggest CLtotal and Vd may be increased for clonidine in pediatric patients receiving organ support compared to those who don’t, as previous literature has shown a CLtotal of 14.6–15.2 L/h/70kg, [128,129] and a Vd of 391 L/70kg. [129] Dosing recommendations presented within this study may be found in Table 2.

Benzodiazepines

5.7

Midazolam

5.7.1

Midazolam pharmacokinetics were described in one prospective analysis by Mulla et al.[27] Within this study, 20 patients received a continuous infusion of midazolam 50–2200 μg/kg/h, with additional bolus doses of 50–100 μg/kg as needed to target a sedation score of 4 defined by study authors.[27] All children received ECMO, with only 7 receiving concurrent CVVH. CLtotal was 3.5 mL/min/kg [0.4–17.5] within these children.[27] CLtotal of midazolam in healthy pediatric patients not receiving organ support or with hepatic dysfunction, have been shown to be 9.11–11.3 mL/min/kg in young infants,[130,131] and 9.33–10.0 mL/min/kg in children.[131] de Wildt et al. demonstrated within a popPK study that CLtotal of midazolam in critically ill children, including those with hepatic dysfunction, was 5.0 mL/min/kg.[132] This study demonstrates that CLtotal and Vd of midazolam are significantly altered in pediatric patients receiving organ support within the first 24 hours.[27] Beyond 24 hours, there appears to be a reduction in CLtotal and/or a change in Vd.[27] Higher doses of midazolam may be required acutely (~24 h) upon the initiation of organ support to achieve sedation targets within these children.

Anti-Arrhythmic Agents

5.8

Propafenone

5.8.1

Seto et al. presented a case-report of a 3-year-old patient receiving propafenone as a 1 mg/kg loading dose, followed by a maintenance continuous infusion of 2–4 μg/kg/min for complex congenital heart disease.[54] CLtotal in this child was 0.94–1.08 L/h/kg.[54] Propafenone was undetectable within the ultrafiltration fluid, suggesting minimal clearance by CVVH.[54] CLtotal in this patient was similar to what has been previously reported in pediatric patients not receiving CRRT, with 1.18 L/h/kg after oral propafenone and 1.21 L/h/kg after IV propafenone.[133] The results of this report suggest propafenone CLtotal is not significantly affected by acute renal and hepatic impairment. [54]

EX-VIVO STUDIES

6

This review incorporated 4 studies of 14 different medications administered within an ex-vivo CRRT circuit. All three methods of CRRT were identified within these analyses. The purpose was to investigate the SC of medications and to determine the effects of flow rates and membrane circuit components (i.e. filter membranes) on CLRRT. Purohit et al. demonstrated that increasing ultrafiltration rates lead to an increase in CLRRT and SC for hydrophilic molecules with a high unbound fraction such as piperacillin and meropenem within a CVVHDF circuit.[65] This finding was further replicated with vancomycin in the study by Kroh et al., [64] illustrating a positive correlation with ke and increasing ultrafiltration rates. Shiraishi et al. concluded that drug-loss due to filter membrane adsorption differs based on membrane circuit composition as polysulfone (PS) and polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) resulted in higher teicoplanin CLRRT compared to a polyacrylonitrile membrane (PAN).[66] Differing albumin concentrations were also shown to affect CLRRT of ceftriaxone, a highly protein bound antibiotic, as CLRRT increased with lower albumin concentrations.[64,67] These results suggest that differing albumin concentrations, often seen in critically ill patients, may impact CLRRT for highly protein bound molecules. DISCUSSION

7

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review focusing on drugs of all therapeutic classes administered to children across the age spectrum, from neonates to adolescents, receiving CRRT. A previous review conducted by Veltri et al.[17] provided an overview of medications administered to this population, however dose recommendations were largely based on adult extrapolations. Using a comprehensive search strategy, this review identified 45 studies evaluating 33 different medications. The current state of the literature largely focuses on the use of antibiotics, as 14 out of 45 studies analyzed the pharmacokinetics of antibiotics administered to pediatric patients receiving CRRT.

Most studies identified that Vd and CLtotal may be similar in critically ill pediatric patients receiving CRRT compared to critically ill pediatric patients who did not. This suggests renally adjusted or reno-protective dose adjustments may lead to subtherapeutic dosing regimens. In the studies that included a comparator, CLtotal was increased in those receiving CRRT compared to those who did not, especially in the presence of residual renal function.[29,41,56] These results were demonstrated with antibiotics (meropenem, piperacillin, vancomycin) where CLtotal is largely dependent on renal clearance.[134–136] The SC of such medications approximated 1.0, suggesting free passage across the CRRT circuit membrane from blood to ultrafiltrate/dialysate fluid.[29,41,56,137] Meropenem, piperacillin and vancomycin all demonstrate similar physicochemical properties as they are hydrophilic agents with a relatively high unbound fraction (~50–98%). [134–136] Drugs with similar physicochemical properties result in high SC within CRRT circuits, therefore being subjected to increased CLRRT.[138] Higher SC and CLRRT were also recorded for these molecules within the included ex-vivo analyses. [64,65] However, further studies with comparator arms would be needed to confirm the relationship between hydrophilicity and fraction unbound with CLtotal in patients on and off CRRT. Furthermore, often higher doses were needed within these studies to achieve adequate pharmacodynamic target attainment.[29,41,56]

One of the objectives of this review was to investigate the pharmacokinetics of molecules administered to pediatric patients receiving CRRT and how these may differ between circuits. Only one such comparison was made due to the paucity of studies and poor quality of reporting. CLtotal of meropenem was greatest with CVVHDF (0.13 mL/kg/hr) and lowest with CVVHD (0.037 mL/hr/kg) when similar blood flow rates (50 mL/min) and membrane filters were used (AN 69).[47,53] Although these results do support greatest CLRRT with CVVHDF within pediatrics, there may be high variability between compounds based on pharmacokinetic behaviour and physicochemical properties.

CRRT membrane composition have been shown to impact CLtotal of medications due to the differences in adsorptive potential and surface area. [139,140] Commonly used semi-synthetic membranes such as PS, PAN, and PMMA contain different degrees of charge which affect adsorptive capacity based on the charge of molecule.[139,140] This concept was shown in the analysis by Shiraishi et al., indicating a higher adsorption potential of the PAN filter, leading to a reduction in CLRRT for teicoplanin.[66] The effects of membrane potential may differ based on the physicochemical properties of a drug as indicated by Lindsay et al. [50] Within this study, CLtotal and CLRRT was significantly greater for clavulanic acid, a negatively charged molecule, compared to piperacillin.[50] Increases in membrane porosity have the potential to increase CLRRT of certain molecules as demonstrated by Harvey et al.[67] The surface area of the membrane filter is of particular importance when designing drug dosing regimens for pediatric patients receiving CRRT due to the smaller total blood volume seen in children. A thorough understanding of the physicochemical properties of both circuit membranes and molecules is needed to ensure adequate dosing regimens for pediatrics receiving CRRT.

This review had several limitations, first there was substantial heterogeneity in the patient populations identified. This heterogeneity may explain the large variability in primary pharmacokinetic parameters identified and may limit the ability to draw significant conclusions. CLtotal of medications administered to patients receiving CRRT may also depend on non-renal CL mechanisms. Non-renal clearance may also be affected by critical illness due to inflammatory responses downregulating CYP-mediated enzymes, hepatic dysfunction, drug-drug interactions and AKI-mediated uremic toxins affecting metabolic enzymes and drug transporters.[141] Reporting of relevant clinical variables associated with renal and hepatic function was poor within the identified studies, therefore minimal conclusions can be drawn as to how acute illness may affect non-renal CL in pediatrics. Secondly, nearly half (42%) of the studies identified were case-reports or case-series. Due to the high variability in pharmacokinetic parameters within critically ill children, the small sample sizes limit the generalizability of the results and should be interpreted with caution. Thirdly, >40% of studies included children <1-year of age. Age-related changes in metabolism and excretion of pharmacokinetics affecting Vd and CLtotal has been well established.[142,143] Changes in body water composition in neonates and young infants results in higher percentages of total body water, affecting Vd. [142,143] Additionally, due to maturation in kidney size and metabolic enzymes, CLtotal may be decreased in young infants compared to more mature children or adolescents. [142,143] As a result, more robust studies are warranted to describe the pharmacokinetics in pediatrics receiving CRRT across the age spectrum. Finally, 53% of studies included children receiving ECMO, accounting for 24% of identified population (197/833) included within this review. As previously mentioned, ECMO may induce alteration in pharmacokinetic parameters of certain medications, therefore a patient specific approach should be utilized when applying these results.

One of the primary objectives of this review was to assess the quality of reporting of pharmacokinetic research within this patient population. To fully assess the validity of such results, completeness and transparency in reporting is of the utmost importance. Overall, reporting of necessary information was generally poor as described by the ClinPK statement.[21] Significant under reporting was seen with the use of concurrent medications and patient demographics that may explain intra- and inter-patient pharmacokinetic variability. Although no formal validated tool has been developed to assess the quality of reporting of pharmacokinetic studies incorporating CRRT, Choi et al. illustrate suggestions for a complete data set that would allow clinicians to incorporate CRRT properties to bedside dosing recommendations.[10] Within this review approximately 10% of studies contained all necessary data required to describe mechanistic effects of CRRT, in conjunction with patient clinical characteristics on the pharmacokinetics of administered drugs. It is imperative that future pharmacokinetic studies in pediatrics receiving CRRT include specific details such as modality, flow rates and filters used, in addition to clinical patient characteristic to further enhance the applicability of such results.

Pediatric pharmacokinetic studies present significant challenges due to logistical and ethical considerations. Often challenges arise with obtaining informed consent, withdrawing blood and urine samples, and recruiting participants within studies.[144,145] Predictive simulations through the use of physiologically-based pharmacokinetic modelling (PBPK) may address these limitations with compartmental modelling by translating CRRT ex-vivo experiments into bedside dosing recommendations. Modelling of drug exposure in pediatrics on CRRT may occur through linking a CRRT “organ” within the PBPK model and parameterized with data from ex-vivo studies.[146] With the incorporation of volume, blood flow, and drug clearance of the CRRT circuit, the impact of CRRT on drug pharmacokinetics can be predicted in-vivo and the CRRT-PBPK model may be used to determine optimal dosing regimens within this patient population.

CONCLUSION

8

With the use of a comprehensive search strategy, this review was able to identify that primary pharmacokinetics in pediatric patients receiving CRRT are largely similar to critically ill children who are not. Over 50% of studies provided a dosing recommendation based on patient specific pharmacokinetics, however due to the substantial amount of heterogeneity within the patient populations, drugs analyzed, and doses administered, these results should be interpretated with caution. Renally adjusted or reno-protective doses often led to subtherapeutic dosing regimens within these patients, illustrating the impact of CRRT on primary pharmacokinetic parameters. However, upon future well designed, pharmacokinetic studies within this population, the use of TDM to guide prescribing practices, when applicable, will optimize PTA and minimize adverse events.

This review identified several ex-vivo CRRT analyses that provide mechanistic insight as to how extracorporeal clearance may be affected by variation in circuit settings (i.e. flow rates, membrane composition). Utilization of these analyses may provide a glance of the pharmacokinetics of certain medications administered to patients receiving CRRT. As such, further well-designed ex-vivo analyses incorporating commonly used CRRT circuit characteristics are required as a starting point to understand the pharmacokinetics within this population. However, a thorough understanding of the interplay between CRRT circuit components, patient clinical status and drug physicochemical properties are required when designing safe and effective dosing regimens. Significant gaps remain within the literature, as a large majority of medications studied were antimicrobials. Minimal studies have been conducted on the use of analgesics or sedatives within this population despite widespread use within a PICU. This review highlights the urgent need for well-designed pharmacokinetic studies in children receiving CRRT and the incorporation of ex-vivo analyses in conjunction with PBPK modelling to provide a complete mechanistic understanding of the interplay between drug, circuit, and patient interactions.

KEY POINTS

  • This systematic review provides a complete and comprehensive analysis of all studies investigating the pharmacokinetics of medications administered to pediatric patients receiving CRRT.

  • The pharmacokinetics of several commonly used medications were analyzed within this patient population and compared to historical controls not receiving CRRT.

  • Overall, reporting of pharmacokinetic results and CRRT settings were poor within the literature. As a result, future well-designed research is needed to address the current state of the literature to ensure adequate dosing regimens for this vulnerable population.
