Prevalence and prognostic importance of lung ultrasound findings in acute coronary syndrome: A systematic review

Abstract

Background:

Heart failure (HF) complicating acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is a herald of adverse outcomes. In this systematic review, we investigated the prevalence of lung ultrasound (LUS) findings and their prognostic utility among patients with ACS.

Methods:

We searched the online databases PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science for studies (full-text articles, published in English) that used LUS in adult patients with ACS [ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina].

Results:

Of 462 studies screened, five prospective, observational investigations published between 2010 and 2021 including 1087 patients met our inclusion criteria. Two studies employed 28-zone imaging protocols whereas three used eight-zone protocols. The proportion of patients with a prior HF diagnosis was ≤ 5% in all studies. The prevalence of B-lines was examined prior to or within 12 hours after coronary angiogram and reporting varied between studies due to different imaging protocols or quantification methods. A higher number of B-lines on admission was associated with an increased risk for developing symptomatic HF during the baseline hospitalization and with a higher in-hospital mortality rate using either 8 or 28-zone protocols. A higher number of B-lines at baseline was also associated with an increased risk of subsequent HF hospitalization or all-cause death.

Conclusions:

Pulmonary congestion by LUS performed on admission appears to be a common finding among patients hospitalized for ACS and is associated with adverse in-hospital and long-term outcomes. Further investigations using standardized LUS protocols are warranted and have the potential to improve risk stratification in ACS.

INTRODUCTION

1 |

Concomitant heart failure (HF) is reported in up to one third of patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and associated with an increased risk for all-cause mortality.1,2 HF signs and symptoms can range from only a subtle degree of pulmonary congestion to cardiogenic shock and may be present on admission or develop during the hospital course.3 Risk stratification of patients with HF in the context of ACS is traditionally performed by applying the Killip classification which is based on clinical examination findings.4 Despite its simplicity, the Killip classification provides prognostic information and is part of established risk scores, such as the Global Registry in Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) and the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) scores.

While cardiogenic shock and overt pulmonary edema can be diagnosed at the bedside, the assessment and detection of less obvious degrees of HF remains challenging. Traditional methods for the detection of pulmonary congestion, such as clinical examination or chest radiography, are relatively insensitive in undifferentiated patients presenting with dyspnea.5,6 Lung ultrasound (LUS) is an emerging tool for the diagnosis and quantification of pulmonary congestion. B-lines on LUS are vertical reverberation artifacts that indicate extravascular fluid. Sonographic B-lines have been identified as a more sensitive method than lung auscultation and chest radiography for the detection of pulmonary congestion among patients with undifferentiated dyspnea.7 Furthermore, the prognostic value of B-lines with respect to HF hospitalizations and mortality has been demonstrated in various studies in both acute and chronic HF cohorts.8,9

The purpose of this systematic review was to examine the prevalence of B-lines on LUS and their prognostic implications for in-hospital and long-term outcomes in patients with ACS. Our hypothesis was that patients with a higher number of B-lines were at a greater risk for adverse outcomes.

METHODS

2 |

Literature search, study selection, and data synthesis

2.1

We identified studies examining the utility of LUS in adults with ACS by searching PubMed (NCBI), Embase (Elsevier), and the Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate Analytics). Search strategies are presented in detail in the Supplementary Material. Applicable controlled vocabulary terms were included when available; no date restrictions were applied. Meeting abstracts were excluded from the Embase and Web of Science search results. The search was carried out on April 13, 2021. Inclusion criteria for this systematic review were full-text articles written in English, in humans, enrolling adults with ACS [comprising ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), or unstable angina], using lung or pleural ultrasound to examine the prognostic value of pulmonary congestion in this cohort. We excluded case reports/case series, studies available in abstract form only, editorials, and review articles. Study selection was independently performed by two investigators based on title and abstract screening with discrepancies resolved by consensus or a third investigator.

Study characteristics including B-line quantification methods, relevant baseline characteristics, interventions and outcomes were independently extracted by two investigators and are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Values are expressed as mean (standard deviation), median (interquartile range), or count (percentage). Due to the heterogeneity of the data, descriptive data synthesis was used to summarize our findings.

For the writing of this manuscript, we applied the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist to describe our methodology and findings (see Table in the Supplements). We registered the protocol for this systematic review on PROSPERO (registration number CRD42021242800). The NIH “Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies” was used independently by two investigators to identify potential biases in the included studies and can be found in the Supplementary Material. We found that the included studies had good or fair risk of bias.

RESULTS

3 |

Study populations

3.1 |

Of 462 unique reports identified through database searching, four met our inclusion criteria. An additional study was identified by manual searching. A detailed overview of the study selection is summarized in Figure 1. The included studies were conducted between 2010 and 2021 in Argentina, Brazil, China, and Italy and included a total of 1199 patients with ACS. Three studies enrolled patients with STEMI,10–12 one patients with STEMI or NSTEMI13 and one patients with STEMI, NSTEMI, or unstable angina.14 One publication included 112 (51%) of patients that were also included in another analysis by this study group that is included in this review.11,12 Therefore, the total number of unduplicated patients in this systematic review is 1087. The proportion of patients with a prior diagnosis of HF was ≤ 5% in all studies. There were important differences in Killip class on admission ranging from one study including only patients without any signs of HF13 and one study including up to 16% of patients with Killip class IV.12 Only one study specifically excluded patients with relevant comorbidities like pulmonary infection or pulmonary fibrosis, which are known to be associated with B-lines in the absence of HF.10 A summary of the study characteristics and findings is presented in Tables 1 and 2.

Lung ultrasound protocols

3.2 |

The timing of LUS examinations ranged from immediately prior to the urgent coronary angiogram to up to 12 hours after admission. Investigators used either phased array or curvilinear transducers for image acquisition. The number of LUS zones investigated was 28 zones in two studies10,14 and eight zones in the three most recent publications.11–13 None of the studies reported offline image analysis, that is, without blinding to clinical findings. B-line quantification was assessed by summing B-lines of each zone to a total value in two studies10,13 and by calculating a score in three studies.11,12,14 Only one study reported the presence of pleural effusions which were present in two patients (2%).10 The mean LUS examination time was reported in three studies and ranged from less than 3–7 minutes.10–12 In one study, serial LUS examinations were performed during the baseline hospitalization,10 while no follow-up LUS examinations were performed after hospital discharge in any of the reviewed studies.

Prevalence of B-Lines on admission

3.3 |

The prevalence of B-lines was reported inconsistently among the included studies due to differences in imaging protocols and quantification methods and in two publications presented by subgroups (e.g., Killip class) only.10,13 In a study in 470 patients with STEMI, NSTEMI or unstable angina, the median number of B-lines in 28 zones was 7 (IQR 0–31) in LUS examinations performed within 1–12 hours after admission.14 In 215 patients with STEMI and eight-zone LUS performed immediately before percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), 51% of patients presented with at least one positive zone (≥ 3 B-lines).11 Similarly, in a subsequent study by this group in patients with STEMI (n = 218) the median number of positive zones on LUS was 1 (IQR 0–5).12

Association between B-lines and in-hospital outcomes

3.4 |

Two publications investigated the association between the number of B-lines on admission and development of HF during a hospitalization for ACS. In patients with anterior STEMI without a prior diagnosis of HF and Killip class ≤ II on admission, a higher number of B-lines assessed within 5 hours after primary PCI was associated with symptomatic HF development (Killip class ≥ III) during the baseline hospitalization.10 The median number of B-lines on 28-zone LUS was 32 (IQR 24–37) in patients that developed symptomatic HF during the baseline hospitalization and 5 (IQR 2–10) in patients who did not. A B-line cutoff ≥ 18 in 28 zones predicted HF events with a sensitivity and specificity of 94.8% and 94.7%, respectively. There was no statistical difference with respect to lung auscultation findings between the two groups. Furthermore, 62% of patients with ≥ 18 B-lines had no rales on physical examination.

In a study including patients with either STEMI or NSTEMI ≥ 5 B-lines on eight-zone LUS on admission predicted the development of HF (Killip class ≥ II) during the baseline hospitalization with a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 81%.13 The median number of B-lines was 14 across eight zones in patients that developed signs of HF in contrast to two B-lines in patients who did not.

Two studies assessed the association between B-line number on admission and in-hospital mortality. In patients presenting with STEMI, the absence of pulmonary congestion (< 3 B-lines in any zone) on eight-zone LUS performed immediately before primary PCI demonstrated a negative predictive value for in-hospital mortality of 98.1% (93.1–99.5%) and a sensitivity for in-hospital survival of 93.3% (77.9%–99.1%).11 In a subsequent study from these investigators, the presence of ≥ 3 B-lines in any zone was associated with in-hospital major adverse cardiac events (MACE, defined as in-hospital mortality, new MI, stroke, or new cardiogenic shock) (OR 1.19 (95% CI, 1.11 –1.27)).12

Association between B-lines and long-term outcomes

3.5 |

Three studies investigated the impact of B-line number on admission on long-term adverse outcomes. During a median follow-up of 25 months, a higher number of B-lines on 28-zone LUS was an independent predictor of the composite endpoint of HF-hospitalizations or all-cause death (HR 1.06 (95% CI: 1.004–1.111)) in patients with anterior STEMI.10 In a cohort of patients with STEMI, NSTEMI, or unstable angina, a higher number of B-lines on 28-zone LUS (stratified by B-line score categories) was an independent predictor of the composite endpoint of all-cause death or nonfatal MI during a median follow-up of 5 months (adjusted HR 1.97 (95% CI, 1.66–2.33)).14 In a study among patients with STEMI, one positive zone (≥ 3 B-lines) on eight-zone LUS was an independent predictor of 30-day MACE (OR: 1.15 (95% CI, 1.06–1.24).12

DISCUSSION

4 |

In this systematic review of prospective, observational studies in adult patients hospitalized for ACS, we found that B-lines as an indicator of pulmonary congestion on 8 or 28-zone LUS are a common finding within 12 hours of admission and that a higher number of B-lines on admission is associated with an increased risk of both in-hospital and long-term adverse outcomes. Although encouraging, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating given the heterogeneity of the reviewed studies, lack of off-line image analysis, and small sample size.

While LUS is becoming more established in the assessment of patients with heart failure, providing relevant diagnostic and prognostic information, systematic investigations regarding the utility of B-lines in the setting of ACS are still scarce. With increasing availability of portable and even pocket-size ultrasound machines in many hospital settings, the opportunity arises to perform ultrasound examinations immediately at the bedside and LUS seems ideal for a rapid assessment of pulmonary congestion in patients with ACS.

Prevalence of B-Lines in ACS

4.1

In this systematic review B-line prevalence varied among the included publications due to differences in patient cohorts, LUS imaging and B-line quantification methods. Besides a varying number of LUS zones investigated, B-line assessment comprised both scores and counts of B-lines, which precluded the performance of a meta-analysis of the B-line data. Furthermore, the following aspects may have affected B-line prevalence: First, there were considerable differences in the inclusion criteria regarding HF symptoms on admission. While one study only included patients without any signs of HF on admission,13 two investigations included a significant proportion of patients with cardiogenic shock.11,12 Only one study excluded patients with conditions that are known to affect B-line number for reasons other than pulmonary congestion.10 Second, the study cohorts included different proportions of patients with STEMI, NSTEMI or unstable angina. Not only the pathophysiologic features of these subgroups in general but also the affected cardiac region may have influenced the likelihood of (left-sided) HF development and resulting pulmonary congestion. In addition, the timing of the LUS examinations differed from immediately before primary PCI to 12 hours after admission. Studies in patients with acute HF receiving decongestive therapy demonstrated that B-line number can change significantly in as little as 3 hours suggesting that these time differences may have affected B-line prevalence, especially if patients received diuretics during this time.15 Nevertheless, all included studies indicated that the presence of B-lines in ACS is common with a wide spectrum of B-line number across patients.

Prognostic value of LUS in patients with ACS

4.2 |

In patients with STEMI and NSTEMI, a higher number of B-lines assessed on admission was associated with the development of symptomatic HF during the baseline hospitalization in both 8 and 28-zone LUS analyses. One study compared these findings to lung auscultation on admission, which demonstrated no difference between symptomatic HF patients and patients without any HF symptoms, highlighting the diagnostic superiority of LUS over lung auscultation.10 When serial LUS examinations were performed during the hospitalization, the number of B-lines significantly increased in patients who developed symptomatic HF compared to those who did not, suggesting the potential utility of serial LUS examinations.

Among patients with STEMI, as few as three B-lines in one LUS zone in an eight-zone protocol were an independent predictor of in-hospital MACE and in-hospital mortality, suggesting that even simplified LUS imaging and B-line quantification approaches can provide important prognostic information.12 LUS protocols with fewer LUS zones may save valuable examination time compared to more comprehensive assessments. Their potential utility in the setting of ACS should hence be investigated in future studies.

Two studies examined the additional prognostic value of B-lines beyond traditional markers of risk. Combining B-line data and Killip classification in a score improved the ability to predict in-hospital mortality in patients with STEMI compared to the Killip classification alone by reclassification of patients with ≥ 3 B-lines in one zone on eight-zone LUS despite having no findings on lung auscultation.11 Bedetti et al.14 demonstrated that a score comprising B-lines along with left ventricular ejection fraction and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion provided additional prognostic value to the GRACE and TIMI risk scores for the combined endpoint of all-cause death and nonfatal MI during a median follow up of 5 months. By contrast, in studies among patients with acute HF, B-lines early during the admission (baseline) are typically less prognostically important for post-discharge outcomes than B-lines assessed prior to hospital discharge.16,17 While all studies in this systematic review performed LUS examinations on admission, data on B-line number prior to hospital discharge and their prognostic relevance for subsequent outcomes such as HF-readmissions and mortality are lacking for patients with ACS.

Early risk stratification of patients with ACS is crucial for further treatment decisions such as initiation of HF therapy and determining the need for prolonged intensive monitoring. By detecting subclinical signs of congestion early during the hospitalization, LUS may aid in the diagnosis of patients with only subtle signs of HF that might not have been identified by lung auscultation alone. The implementation of LUS assessment in addition to clinical examination findings and traditional risk markers has the potential to improve risk identification in these vulnerable patients.

CONSIDERATIONS FOR FUTURE INVESTIGATIONS

5 |

Based on this systematic review, there are several gaps in the current literature. It is unclear whether different cut-off values for B-lines should be applied in patients with STEMI, NSTEMI or unstable angina using, for example, a simplified 8-zone imaging protocol. Standardized imaging protocols and reporting of LUS measures in patients with ACS will be important to facilitate the collection and analysis of larger datasets in the future.18 These data could facilitate a revised Killip classification incorporating LUS results. There is also a lack of longitudinal data in post-MI cohorts describing the evolution of pulmonary congestion assessed by ultrasound following hospital discharge and the association with subsequent HF events or death. These data could allow for the identification of patients who are most likely to benefit from optimized therapies and strategies that mitigate these risks.

Limitations

5.1 |

Our findings should be interpreted considering the small number of included studies and the limited number of reported events. The above discussed variable number of chest zones investigated and the different B-line quantification methods made it difficult to compare the prevalence of B-lines across the studies in ACS and did not allow for a meta-analysis. However, we believe that the findings of this systematic review are nevertheless hypothesis-generating for future investigations in patients with ACS.

CONCLUSIONS

6 |

Among patients with ACS, B-lines on admission LUS appear to be a common finding. Patients with a higher number of B-lines are at increased risk for in-hospital and long-term adverse outcomes using either 8- or 28-zone LUS protocols. Further investigations using standardized B-line quantification protocols are warranted to assess the incremental value of LUS findings beyond conventional risk markers to improve risk stratification in this vulnerable population.
