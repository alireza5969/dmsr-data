Mathematical modelling of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in low- and middle-income countries: A systematic review

Abstract

Background:

Due to high burden of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), international funding organizations have prioritized the development of RSV vaccines. Mathematical models of RSV will play an important role in assessing the relative value of these interventions. Our objectives were to provide an overview of the existing RSV modelling literature in LMIC and summarize available results on population-level effectiveness and cost-effectiveness.

Methods:

We searched MEDLINE from 2000 to 2020 for English language publications that employed a mathematical model of RSV calibrated to LMIC. Qualitative data were extracted on study and model characteristics. Quantitative data were collected on key model input assumptions and base case effectiveness and cost-effectiveness estimates for various immunization strategies.

Findings:

Of the 283 articles reviewed, 15 met inclusion criteria. Ten studies used modelling techniques to explore RSV transmission and/or natural history, while eight studies evaluated RSV vaccines and/or monoclonal antibodies, three of which included cost-effectiveness analyses. Six studies employed deterministic compartmental models, five studies employed individual transmission models, and four studies used different types of cohort models. Nearly every model was calibrated to at least one middle-income country, while four were calibrated to low-income countries.

Interpretation:

The mathematical modelling literature in LMIC has demonstrated the potential effectiveness of RSV vaccines and monoclonal antibodies. This review has demonstrated the importance of accounting for seasonality, social contact rates, immunity from prior infection and maternal antibody transfer. Future models should consider incorporating individual-level risk factors, subtype-specific effects, long-term sequelae of RSV infections, and out-of-hospital mortality.

Introduction

1.

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the most common viral cause of lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) in infants globally. By the age of two, nearly all children will have been infected at least once (Glezen et al., 1986). In healthy adults, infection can reoccur throughout life, but generally causes mild upper respiratory tract infections (URTI) (DeVincenzo et al., 2010). In infants, the elderly, and individuals with cardiopulmonary disease or immune suppression, RSV infection can lead to severe LRTI such as bronchiolitis or pneumonia. In 2015, RSV-associated LRTI led to an estimated 3.2 million hospital admissions and 118,200 deaths among children under five, with over 99 % of the deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) (Shi et al., 2017). Due to the high morbidity and mortality from RSV, Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, has identified RSV interventions as one of the six priority areas for the 2021–2025 funding period (GAVI, 2018).

At present, the only option for prevention of RSV is the monoclonal antibody (mAb), palivizumab. Although effective, the cost and need for multiple doses in the first few months of life limit its use to high-risk populations in high-income countries (HIC). However, there are currently 21 vaccine candidates and monoclonal antibodies in clinical trials, with many more in preclinical phases of development (Papadopoulos et al., 2018; Path, 2020). One of the challenges in developing RSV vaccines is that the majority of RSV hospitalizations and deaths occur in young infants, when it can be difficult or take several doses to elicit a robust vaccine-derived immune response. Therefore, three main active and passive immunization strategies have been proposed: vaccines targeting pregnant women in order to confer passive immunity to their fetus via transplacental antibody transfer; a mAb given in early infancy to provide infants with passive protection against the virus or severe outcomes in the first few months of life; and active immunization of infants and/or young children that would reduce community transmission and force of infection.

Due to the public health burden of RSV infection and limited public health resources in LMIC, it is critical that governments and large funding organizations have the means to assess population-level effectiveness of new interventions prior to introduction so that spending can be prioritized on interventions that have the highest impact and best value for money. Due to the diversity in RSV immunization products and the epidemiological settings in which they would be used, clinical trials alone will provide insufficient information. Mathematical modelling techniques can be employed to explore vaccine strategies in resource-constrained settings where there is a paucity of robust data on RSV. In addition, mathematical models are able to account for setting-specific health systems factors and disease transmission dynamics when esti-mating population-level effectiveness (Kinyanjui et al., 2015). The most recent World Health Organization (WHO) consultation on RSV vaccine development emphasized the importance of vaccine impact modelling in determining optimal vaccination strategies (Giersing et al., 2019).

In this paper, we present a systematic review of existing mathematical models of the natural history of RSV that have been calibrated to LMIC, which will be critical to help guide policymakers and researchers in immunization development, evaluation, and use. Our primary objective is to identify the landscape of the current modelling literature to inform the development of future models that can assess new technologies as they progress through clinical trials. Our secondary objective is to provide an overview of estimates of population-level effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of the vaccine and mAb strategies that have been tested in the existing modelling literature.

Methods

2.

Search strategy

2.1.

We conducted a systematic review of the literature through MEDLINE. The search strategy involved the use of medical subject headings and keywords from two categories: ‘RSV’ and ‘mathematical modelling’ (Table 1). To be identified in the search, each article was required to have one medical subject heading or keyword (title and/or abstract) from each category. We also reviewed the reference lists of selected studies and articles suggested by experts. We included studies published between January 1, 2000 and April 10, 2020.

To be included in this study, articles were required to be published in English and have used a mathematical model calibrated to RSV data from an LMIC. We defined LMIC according to World Bank income levels of low-, lower middle-, or upper middle-income countries (The World Bank, 2020). We included studies that modelled multiple respiratory viruses only if they employed a model that assessed RSV independently of other respiratory viruses. Studies that modelled both HIC and LMIC were included but results from HIC were excluded from our review.

Two reviewers participated in the search (AM and MR). All abstracts were screened by both reviewers. When an abstract was included by either reviewer, it received a full text review from both reviewers to determine if the study met inclusion criteria. Uncertainty about study inclusion following full text review was resolved by a third independent reviewer (AP).

Data analysis

2.2.

The objective of this literature review was to identify both strengths and areas for improvement in existing models. Due to the diversity in modelling techniques and primary outcomes in the studies included, we relied primarily on qualitative data extraction techniques. To enable comparison, we collated data on model type, regions assessed, vaccine strategies evaluated, study objectives, key results, funding source, individual level-characteristics included, and the method used to model disease transmission dynamics.

In the studies that evaluated either a vaccine or mAb, we collected quantitative data on the key assumptions underpinning the base case analyses as well as effectiveness and cost-effectiveness results, including incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) and quality-adjusted or disability-adjusted life years (QALYs and DALYs, respectively). When results were expressed in multiple units (e.g., reduction in infections vs. hospitalizations vs. mortality), we preferentially collected impact on mortality. For studies that included models on other respiratory viruses, data were only collected on the RSV models. Likewise, for studies that modelled LMIC and HIC, we only included results from the LMIC.

Results

3.

The initial MEDLINE data base search identified 279 articles, while supplementary search methods identified an additional three articles. Abstract review by two reviewers led to the identification of 36 potentially relevant articles (Cohen’s K = 0.705). These 36 articles received a full text review by both reviewers. At the peer review stage, an article was suggested for inclusion by a reviewer that was not turned up in the original search. The original search did not find this article because it did not include any reference to modelling as a medical subject heading or keyword in the title or abstract. This article received a full text review by two reviewers, and both determined it met inclusion criteria (Baker et al., 2019). A total of 15 articles were included in this review (Fig. 1).

A summary of the 15 included studies is presented in Table 2. Ten of the studies included exploring RSV transmission dynamics or natural history as a primary objective, while eight of the studies included evaluation of immunization strategies as a primary objective. Among the ten studies evaluating transmission dynamics or natural history, three of these studies focused on comparing model structures that differed in their assumptions around the duration and robustness of host immune response to RSV in preventing future infections (Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Weber et al., 2001; White et al., 2007). In general, these studies found that assuming either lifelong partial immunity or waning immunity that is boosted with re-infection demonstrated good data fit; however, these structures resulted in different effective reproductive numbers. Four studies employed mathematical models to help identify what age-groups and household structures led to the majority of disease transmission (Kinyanjui et al., 2015; Kombe et al., 2018; Mahikul et al., 2019; Poletti et al., 2015). These studies found that older children were often the index case in households and that a significant proportion of RSV transmission (approximately one third to one half) occurred within households. Three studies evaluated the drivers of RSV seasonality (Baker et al., 2019; Weber et al., 2001; Paynter et al., 2014). They identified climatic factors such as precipitation and humidity, school schedules, and seasonal variation in childhood nutritional status as possible drivers. One study explored the role of individual-level risk factors for RSV acquisition on disease transmission by demonstrating how accounting for malnutrition can increase transmission (Paynter, 2016).

Two studies were calibrated to data from low-income countries (Weber et al., 2001; White et al., 2007), seven studies were calibrated to data from lower-middle income countries (Kinyanjui et al., 2015; Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Kombe et al., 2018; Poletti et al., 2015; Paynter et al., 2014; Paynter, 2016; Brand et al., 2020), and five studies were calibrated to data from upper-middle income countries (Baker et al., 2019; White et al., 2007; Mahikul et al., 2019; Pouwels et al., 2016; Salinas-Escudero et al., 2012). Li, et al., included 72 Gavi-eligible countries, which predominantly encompasses low- and lower middle-income countries (Li et al., 2020), whereas Scheltema, et al., evaluated a global cohort that included cases from all World Bank country and lending groups (Scheltema et al., 2018).

A summary of the models developed for each study is presented in Table 3. Thirteen of the studies developed new models, and the remaining two either modified or re-applied previously developed models to answer a new question (Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Paynter et al., 2014). Six studies used deterministic compartmental models (Kinyanjui et al., 2015; Baker et al., 2019; Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Weber et al., 2001; White et al., 2007; Paynter et al., 2014), five studies used individual transmission models (Kombe et al., 2018; Mahikul et al., 2019; Poletti et al., 2015; Paynter, 2016; Brand et al., 2020), while the other four studies employed a static cohort model (Li et al., 2020), Markov model (Pouwels et al., 2016), decision tree model (Salinas-Escudero et al., 2012), and an equation-based model of maternal antibody transfer (Scheltema et al., 2018). The majority of models adjusted transition probabilities based on age and history of prior infection. Implicit to using age as a predictor of infection risk, most authors accounted for maternal antibody transfer conferring partial immunity to infection. One study stratified risk of infection based upon RSV subtype (Kombe et al., 2018), while two studies accounted for viral load (Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Kombe et al., 2018). Three studies accounted for individual-level co-morbidities such as malnutrition or pre-maturity when evaluating individual risk of developing severe infection or population-level risk of transmission (Paynter, 2016; Salinas-Escudero et al., 2012; Scheltema et al., 2018).

Most models included some estimate of RSV transmission dynamics. In particular, eleven of fifteen studies accounted for seasonal variation in RSV transmission. Six studies developed social contact matrices to determine exposure and transmission risk (Kinyanjui et al., 2015; Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Kombe et al., 2018; Mahikul et al., 2019; Poletti et al., 2015; Brand et al., 2020). Only two studies considered the interplay between multiple RSV subtypes in determining transmission of the virus (Li et al., 2020; White et al., 2004).

Among the eight studies included in this review that evaluated an immunization strategy, there was considerable heterogeneity in the strategies evaluated (Table 4). Five studies evaluated vaccines targeted at pregnant women (maternal immunization) (Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Poletti et al., 2015; Pouwels et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020; Scheltema et al., 2018), four studies evaluated vaccines targeted at infants six months old or less (infant vaccination) (Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Poletti et al., 2015; Pouwels et al., 2016), two studies evaluated mAb targeted at infants less than six months old (infant mAb) (Salinas-Escudero et al., 2012; Li et al., 2020), one study evaluated a vaccine targeted at children age six months or older (pediatric vaccination) (Poletti et al., 2015), and two studies evaluated combining multiple immunization strategies (combined immunization) (Brand et al., 2020; Pouwels et al., 2016).

A summary of effectiveness and cost-effectiveness results is presented in Table 4. Due to variability in how studies expressed their effectiveness and cost-effectiveness outcomes, we did not attempt a quantitative synthesis of the results. Every study provided some estimate of population-level effectiveness. Three of these eight studies included cost-effectiveness estimates (Pouwels et al., 2016; Salinas-Escudero et al., 2012; Li et al., 2020). Underpinning the results are a number of assumptions regarding model inputs such as vaccine coverage, vaccine/mAb efficacy, and costs. In base case analyses, most studies assumed a vaccine coverage of between 50 and 100 %. Regarding model inputs for vaccine or antibody efficacy, a variety of approaches were taken. The majority of studies assumed that vaccines or antibodies, regardless of type, would lead to a 60–100 % reduction in risk of infection over a duration of 75 days to one year. Other approaches included assuming no change in risk of infection but instead a reduction in force of infection and/or morbidity associated with infections (Kinyanjui et al., 2015; Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Brand et al., 2020; Salinas-Escudero et al., 2012), or assuming that maternal vaccination would extend or enhance natural immunity by two and a half to four months (Poletti et al., 2015; Brand et al., 2020; Scheltema et al., 2018). For the three studies that included cost-effectiveness estimates, cost estimates ranged from as low as three dollars per dose for a maternal vaccine to $2,400 per course for palivizumab, which requires multiple doses (Pouwels et al., 2016; Salinas-Escudero et al., 2012; Li et al., 2020). Seven out of eight studies explored a large range of inputs via univariate, multivariate and/or probabilistic sensitivity analyses, with one study reporting results along with certainty estimates based on the quality of the model input data (Li et al., 2020).

Effectiveness results were presented using a wide range of outcomes, making it challenging to compare specific interventions. Every analyzed immunization strategy demonstrated at least a one-third reduction in either infections, hospitalizations, or mortality, according to one or more studies. Four studies compared a maternal vaccination strategy to an infant immunization strategy. Among these, three showed greater effectiveness with an infant immunization strategy (Poletti et al., 2015; Pouwels et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020), while one showed greater effectiveness with a maternal vaccine strategy (Pan-Ngum et al., 2017).

Only one study evaluated a vaccine given to school-aged children. In this study, vaccination of school-aged children was compared to infant and maternal vaccination and found it was slightly more effective (Poletti et al., 2015). Combined immunization strategies included maternal vaccination with infant immunization, as well as maternal vaccination with vaccination of school-aged children. These strategies led to a 51 % and 54 % reduction in hospitalization and mortality, respectively, which was more effective than other strategies (Brand et al., 2020; Pouwels et al., 2016).

Given the small number and methodological heterogeneity of included studies, we did not compare the cost-effectiveness of different vaccine strategies. When individual studies used the cost-effectiveness threshold of three times gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, the two studies to evaluate hypothetical vaccine or mAb strategies demonstrated multiple strategies that are cost-effective (Pouwels et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020). For the only palivizumab study, it met this cost-effectiveness threshold when ICERs were expressed in cost per QALY, but not when they were expressed in cost per life years gained (Salinas-Escudero et al., 2012).

Discussion

4.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically review mathematical models of RSV in LMIC, where the burden of RSV is greatest. A number of model types were employed to explore uncertainty regarding RSV transmission dynamics, model structure, and/or estimate the population-level impact of various immunization strategies. The conclusions that can be drawn from this will be relevant to vaccine/mAb development, evaluation, and implementation.

Studies included in this review demonstrated the importance of accounting for the seasonal nature of RSV outbreaks. The majority of studies included in this review fit their models to seasonally-adjusted RSV data, indicating the significance of incorporating seasonality into mathematical models of RSV transmission. There is high-quality epidemiological literature demonstrating seasonal variability in RSV infections in most settings, with evidence suggesting more season to season variation in tropical climates (Obando-Pacheco et al., 2018; McCracken et al., 2013; Weber et al., 1998). Going a step further, the studies in our review have demonstrated the importance of weather, nutritional status, and school schedules as variables that can affect seasonal outbreak patterns of RSV in LMIC (Baker et al., 2019; Weber et al., 2001; Paynter et al., 2014). Modelling studies out of HIC have also suggested that birth rates and rates of waning immunity from prior infection are important factors (Hogan et al., 2016).

Social contact rates were shown to be an important determinant of RSV transmission. Studies included in this review demonstrated how changing household demographics can impact RSV incidence over time, and also emphasized the role of school-aged children as index cases for infection within a household. This result is consistent with genomic and prospective cohort studies conducted in LMIC (Agoti et al., 2017; Munywoki et al., 2014). The most common approach employed by authors in this review to model social contact patterns was to develop a contact matrix. The broader infectious disease modelling literature supports this approach, and in particular suggests using self-reported social contacts instead of making homogenous or proportionate mixing assumptions in modelling social contact rates (Wallinga et al., 2006). Of the papers in this review, Kinyanjui, et al., explored uncertainty in how to model social contact rates in the most detail by comparing contact data arising from diary collections against an estimated social contact matrix, finding that they produced nearly identical outcomes in terms of model fit and the effectiveness of an immunization strategy (Kinyanjui et al., 2015).

Modelling the effect of partial immunity acquired from prior infection is another variable that impacts disease transmission and model structure that received considerable attention by the studies included in this review. This was done on the basis of natural history studies showing a negative relationship between recent RSV infection and risk of re-infection with waning immunity over time (Glezen et al., 1986; Ohuma et al., 2012; Domachowske and Rosenberg, 1999). The majority of studies we reviewed accounted for prior infection history. To explore uncertainty in how best to model the effects of partial immunity from prior infection, three studies compared different model structures (Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Weber et al., 2001; White et al., 2007). Models of lifelong partial immunity and waning immunity that is boosted by re-infection were both shown to have good data fit across all three studies, although when compared head to head by White, et al., a model of lifelong partial immunity was found to have the best fit (White et al., 2007). Only one study included in this review accounted for subtype-specific effects (Kombe et al., 2018), despite evidence from a modelling study in a high-income study suggesting differences in sus-ceptibility between heterologous and homologous infections (White et al., 2004). For future models, it may be important to consider subtype-specific effects of prior infection on the basis of context-specific RSV subtype prevalence.

There are a number of aspects of RSV natural history that were not fully explored by studies included in this review. We identified only two studies that included how co-morbidities such as pre-term birth (i.e. less than 37 weeks gestation), bronchopulmonary dysplasia, congenital heart disease, malnutrition, and immunodeficiency would impact morbidity and mortality at a population level, despite epidemiologic evidence that these conditions are major risk factors for hospitalization and death from RSV (Welliver, 2003). No studies accounted for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), which has been shown to be a significant risk factor for the development of LRTI and may play an outsized role in HIV-endemic settings (Cohen et al., 2016). In addition, no studies included in this review accounted for how transplacental antibody transfer can be impaired by a variety of conditions, including HIV, pre-maturity, and hypergammaglobulinemia (Atwell et al., 2015; Atwell and Karron, 2016). This would have important implications on neonatal immunity and the efficacy of maternal vaccines (Jallow et al., 2018; Okoko et al., 2002; Palmeira et al., 2012).

Only one study accounted for the increased mortality of RSV subtype A (Hall et al., 1990), which could be significant in the case of setting-specific differences in genotype prevalence. Finally, epidemiologic literature has demonstrated associations between RSV infection in early life and development of recurring wheeze and asthma later childhood in a severity-dependent manner (Carroll et al., 2009; Lu et al., 2016). Only one study included in this review accounted for this relationship (Salinas-Escudero et al., 2012). While it is not yet clear if the relationship between RSV and asthma is causal, the high morbidity associated with childhood asthma could merit further consideration in future models. This will be difficult to fully understand until RSV prevention products are licensed and in widespread use for several years, but the potential reduction of asthma-related morbidity would sub-stantially increase the health and economic benefits of RSV vaccines or mAbs.

The secondary objective of this review was to provide an overview of the estimates of population-level effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of vaccine and antibody strategies that have been tested in the LMIC modelling literature. Seven of the studies included in this review evaluated hypothetical vaccine or mAb strategies and one evaluated palivizumab, a first step towards determining which immunization strategies will be the most impactful at a population level. While our analysis was limited by the methodological heterogeneity, these studies provide initial evidence that maternal, infant, and pediatric strategies have the potential to significantly reduce morbidity and mortality from RSV. At this point, there is not enough evidence to draw any definitive conclusions about what strategy will be the most effective, especially taking into consideration that the performance characteristics of the vaccines and mAb that are currently under development are still unknown, and the likelihood that different strategies will be preferred in different settings.

Only three studies included in this review evaluated cost-effectiveness. Of these, two suggested that maternal or infant immunization strategies have the potential to be cost-effective when measured against a cost-effectiveness threshold of three times GDP per capita. Salinas-Escudero, et al. author the only study to evaluate palivizumab, and their results out of Mexico suggest that ICERs for palivizumab administered to pre-term infants meet common cost-effectiveness thresholds only when expressed in QALYs, but not life years gained. Given the role that cost-effectiveness analysis often plays in determining introduction of public health interventions, this is an area that requires further analysis.

There is a paucity of data in the current RSV modelling literature in LMIC on disease transmission, hospitalization, and mortality, especially from geographically diverse settings. Five of the eight studies included in this review that evaluated effectiveness of a hypothetical immunization strategy relied on data from a cohort in Kilifi, Kenya (Kinyanjui et al., 2015; Pan-Ngum et al., 2017; Kombe et al., 2018; Poletti et al., 2015; Brand et al., 2020). The single country context indicates considerable uncertainty in model inputs. Most authors addressed this uncertainty by providing a range of results or running extensive sensitivity analyses. Some studies supplemented their model inputs with data from high-income countries (Baker et al., 2019; Scheltema et al., 2018), while others attempted to quantify uncertainty and demonstrate the potential impact on results (Kinyanjui et al., 2015; Li et al., 2020). In addition, due to the lack of data, few studies reported mortality outcomes and, only one included out-of-hospital mortality, despite out-of-hospital mortality accounting for an estimated 53 % of RSV-related mortality LMIC (Shi et al., 2017; Li et al., 2020). To robustly evaluate immunization products, it is critical that more data is collected around transmission, hospitalization, and mortality in LMIC.

Several limitations of this systematic review should be noted. The scope of this study was intentionally narrow to identify gaps in the existing modelling literature to evaluate the potential impact of RSV prevention products in LMIC. There are multiple studies that assess the impact of immunization in HIC that were not included, which could provide insights into model structure for assessing vaccines or mAbs in developing countries (Hogan et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2017; Cromer et al., 2017; Bos et al., 2007; Acedo et al., 2010). Our ability to compare the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of specific interventions was limited by differences in underlying assumptions for primary effectiveness outcomes. Given the considerable differences in settings and perspectives, as well as the methodological heterogeneity of included studies, an observed difference between ICERs may well reflect an artifact of the different study designs rather than a true feature of the interventions under study. We did not specifically comment on the methodological quality of individual studies, which might have introduced bias in the generalizations of the systematic review.

In this paper, we presented a summary of mathematical models of RSV that have relevance to vaccine evaluation in developing countries. With over 100,000 deaths among children under five each year, it is critical that policymakers have the tools to determine how to allocate resources and optimize value for money as RSV vaccines progress through clinical trials. The importance of seasonality, social contact rates, and immunity from prior infection has been emphasized by the existing LMIC modelling literature, suggesting that these variables warrant inclusion in future model-based evaluations of immunization strategies. The impact of HIV prevalence, individual-level co-morbidities, RSV subtype, and out-of-hospital mortality have not been fully evaluated in the LMIC modelling literature, despite epidemiologic literature indicating the importance of these variables. Incorporating these variables into future models represents an important area for future research.
