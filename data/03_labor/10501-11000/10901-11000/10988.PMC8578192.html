Interventions to Improve Primary Care Provider Management of Atopic Dermatitis: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Background:

Most atopic dermatitis (AD) patients are managed by primary care providers (PCPs). PCP discomfort diagnosing and managing AD leads to suboptimal patient outcomes.

Objective:

To determine the efficacy of interventions aimed at improving PCP management of AD.

Methods:

A systematic literature review was performed for interventions to improve primary care management of AD. PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, LILACS, Cochrane, GREAT, and CINAHL were searched from database origin to February 24, 2020. Two reviewers independently performed the title/abstract and full-text review, and data extraction.

Results:

Overall, 3,009 non-duplicate articles were screened; 145 full-text articles were assessed. Thirteen studies met inclusion criteria, including 8 randomized controlled trials, 2 cohorts, 2 qualitative studies, and 1 unspecified design. Seven interventions (53.8%) significantly improved PCP knowledge/ability and/or a patient outcome, including patients consulting with a dermatology-trained nurse, pairing clinical education with expert consultation, pairing trainees with clinical mentors, giving clinicians a treatment guide, pairing clinical education with a treatment guide, and providing an eczema action plan.

Limitations:

Studies had moderate-high risk-of-bias, moderate quality, and heterogeneous designs.

Conclusions:

There are few studies published and little evidence supporting the efficacy of interventions aimed at improving primary care management of AD. Further research is required to develop and implement effective interventions to improve primary care management of AD.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a leading cause of disability in children and adults worldwide.1 The annual economic burden of AD is high for patients, their families, and national health care systems.2,3 AD negatively impacts many aspects of quality of life (QoL), such as sleep, behavior, marital relationships, and mental health.4–6 Poor AD control is associated with worse QoL and lifelong health implications.4,5

Most children with AD receive initial care for AD from their primary care provider (PCP).7,8 Two-thirds of children with mild AD can likely be managed in the primary care setting, and PCPs continue to play an important role in AD management for children who are referred to a dermatologist.7 However, many PCPs, including more than half of United States (US) pediatricians, refer even mild AD to dermatologists.8 This is likely owing to discomfort diagnosing and managing AD or adhering to AD guidelines across pediatric PCPs.9 Guidelines for appropriate management of AD exist.8,10 However, some lack practical algorithms/guides for treatment decision-making which may be confusing to PCPs. Dependence on subspecialty management of AD is problematic in many countries due to a shortage of dermatologists, particularly those that see children, accept government-funded insurance, and/or practice in rural areas. In fact, there is only one pediatric dermatologist per 120,000 children in the US.11

AD, a disease with a high global prevalence and a profound economic burden, is most often managed in the primary care setting. Yet there exists a demonstrated need for increased PCP ability to manage AD. Previous studies of interventions aimed at improving PCP knowledge and treatment behaviors found conflicting results. Thus, an aggregated summary of attempted interventions and their efficacy is needed to improve AD management by PCPs. This systematic literature review determined the efficacy of interventions aimed at improving primary care management of AD and this information will help guide future interventions.

Materials and methods

This study was exempt from institutional review board approval because the search only included published literature. The systematic review was registered in the PROSPERO database (ID: CRD42020168605) and was carried out in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.12

Literature Search

Comprehensive searches of PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, LILACS, Cochrane, GREAT, and CINAHL were conducted on February 24, 2020, with no restriction imposed on publication date. Search terms were adapted from two previously published Cochrane reviews13,14 (Supplemental Table 1). The inclusion and exclusion criteria were organized according to PICOS criteria (Supplemental Table 2). Inclusion criteria: any age or gender patient with a diagnosis of AD; any intervention aimed at improving PCP management of AD; with or without a comparator; outcomes include participant AD outcomes, clinician knowledge, or clinician practice; all study designs; published in English, Spanish, or Portuguese language in peer-reviewed journals. The PROSPERO protocol specified English but Spanish and Portuguese were added due to authors’ ability to screen in these languages. Exclusion criteria: animal studies; intervention not targeted to primary care setting; no report of any primary outcome; secondary research (review or meta-analysis); gray literature (dissertation, conference abstract, letter, review, commentary, editorial, note); language other than English, Spanish, or Portuguese.

Data Extraction

Search results were uploaded to Covidence systematic review software (www.covidence.org) and duplicates were removed. Two authors (E.C. and F.C.P.S.L.) independently performed title/abstract screening, full-text screening, data extraction in duplicate, quality or risk-of-bias assessment, and forward/reverse citation search of included articles. Conflicts were assessed in full-text review and resolved by consensus. Data elements were extracted and organized in table format for qualitative synthesis.

Included RCTs were assessed using the revised Cochrane Collaboration tool for assessing risk of bias (RoB 2). A study was considered “low risk” if it was classified as “low risk” in all domains, “some concerns” if there was at least one domain with a rating of “some concerns,” and “high risk” if there was at least one domain with a rating of “high risk” or several domains with a rating of “some concern”. Cohort studies were assessed using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS).15,16 The use of the NOS for non-randomized studies deviated from the PROSPERO protocol because it was a better fit. The NOS uses a star system to assess selection of study groups, comparability of study groups, and ascertainment of the outcome of interest. The overall quality score can range from 0-9 stars, 7 and above meaning high quality studies. Discrepancies in quality and risk-of-bias assessments were discussed and resolved by discussion. Qualitative studies were not evaluated using a formal tool due to a lack of expert consensus on the best assessment approaches. All authors, however, screened them for general quality and agreed on their inclusion.

Results

Search Results

The initial search identified 3,009 non-duplicate articles; 2,864 were excluded during title/abstract review. Full-text review was performed on 145 articles, of which 13 met inclusion criteria and were included in the review. No additional articles were discovered through forward or reverse citation search (Figure 1). Study characteristics are presented in Table 1, Supplemental Table 2, and Supplemental Results.

ROB and quality assessment

Of the eight included RCTs, six (75%) were rated as “some concerns” using the RoB 2 tool, and two (25%) were rated as “high” risk of bias (Table 2). Both of the two included cohort studies received six out of nine possible stars on the NOS (Table 3).

Study outcomes

Outcomes measured varied across studies, with changes in patient outcomes, clinician knowledge, and/or clinician practice assessed. Nine interventions (69.2%) targeted pediatric providers (attending physician, general practice physician, resident physician, and/or nurse practitioner), 2 (15.4%) targeted adult providers, and 2 (15.4%) targeted providers who treated both children and adults. Seven interventions (53.8%) significantly improved PCP knowledge/ability and/or a patient outcome, including: patients consulting with a dermatology-trained nurse, pairing clinical education with expert consultation, pairing trainees with clinical mentors, giving clinicians a treatment guide, pairing clinical education with a treatment guide, and providing an eczema action plan.

Interventions that improved patient outcomes

Interventions that led to significant patient outcome improvement (n = 3) included dermatology-trained nurse consultation for patients, instructing PCPs to follow treatment guidelines based on patient AD severity, and an educational module followed by employment of an eczema action plan (EAP) in clinic.

Kernick et al.17 conducted a RCT in which patients in the intervention group attended consultations with a dermatology-trained nurse over a four-month period while the control group received routine care. Patient outcomes were assessed with the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), Euroqol Instrument, a subjective scale, and a qualitative questionnaire. While the DLQI improved in the intervention group, this improvement was not significantly different than observed in the control group. The intervention group had a significantly greater improvement in the subjective scale than the control group. However, the subjective scale was not previously validated, and the small effect-size may not be clinically meaningful.

Sánchez et al.18 created observational, cohort registers based on the European Task Force on AD (ET-FAD) and instructed PCPs to follow treatment recommendations based on patients’ SCORing AD (SCORAD) scores. Patient outcomes were measured with SCORAD, DLQI, and an unvalidated subjective scale. At 6, 12, and 24 months, patients experienced significant reductions of SCORAD, DLQI, and the subjective scale. The SCORAD and DLQI reductions at 12 and 24 months were greater than the minimal clinically effective difference (MCID) for these instruments.19,20

Brown et al.21 conducted a RCT in which residents and attending physicians a) completed the Atopic Dermatitis 101 (AD101) online module, b) were instructed in the use of an eczema action plan (EAP), and c) were told to use the EAP at AD patient visits. The control group provided usual care. Patient outcomes were measured via survey, including the Children’s Dermatology Life Quality Index (CDLQI) or Infant Dermatitis Quality of Life Index (IDQoL). Patient understanding significantly improved in the EAP group compared to usual care; QoL scores increased in both groups. The effect-size was not reported and interpretation in terms of clinical significance, such as MCID, for these results is unclear because the results are presented generally as “QOL” instead of by instrument.

Interventions that improved clinician knowledge

Interventions that led to significant improvement in clinician knowledge (n = 3) included an educational module by itself, an educational module paired with a treatment algorithm (guide), and an educational module paired with EAP use.

Craddock et al.22 performed a prospective, cohort study in which first-year pediatric resident physicians completed AD101 training and the control group, second-year pediatric resident physicians, did not. Outcomes were measured via pretests and posttests. There was a significant increase in intervention group posttest scores at six months that was sustained at 12 months. The intervention group scores were significantly higher than the control group scores at 12 months. However, clinical documentation was reviewed from a subset of resident physicians, and the intervention did not appear to change clinical practice.

Miyar et al.23 conducted a RCT in which pediatric residents and attending physicians in the intervention group attended a 20-minute lecture regarding how to use an AD treatment algorithm designed by pediatric dermatologists and received an electronic copy of the algorithm, a skin atlas, and a steroid treatment table. Treatment algorithms typically direct clinicians’ management of AD, whereas an EAP is a set of instructions provided directly to the family. The control group did not receive the training. Knowledge change was assessed via pretests and posttests. The intervention group scored significantly higher on posttests, controlling for baseline scores, and the intervention test score changes were significantly higher than the control group changes.

Brown et al.,21 whose RCT involving the AD101 module, EAP instruction, and EAP use in practice led to improved patient outcomes, also reported clinician knowledge gains. There was significant improvement in intervention group clinician posttest scores for medication comfort and understanding, providing anticipatory guidance, and understanding AD stages. However, the intervention group had higher baseline scores and gained less than one point on a 5-point Likert-type scale on each of these three domains so the significance of these results is again difficult to interpret.

Interventions that improved clinician practice

Interventions that led to significant improvement in clinician practice (n = 3) included an educational module combined with expert consultation in clinic, trainees practicing in pairs combined with expert consultation in clinic, and use of an EAP in clinic.

Schopf and Flytkjær24 conducted a RCT in which PCPs in the intervention group participated in a web-based AD management course and were able to seek dermatologist guidance via email or text. Outcomes included physician-reported duration of topical corticosteroid treatment, number of treatment modalities, and number of specialist referrals. The intervention group had significantly fewer specialist referrals.

Macaulay et al.25 analyzed results of joint clinics in which pediatric and GP trainees were paired and also received supervision and support from GP trainers and consultant pediatricians. The study type was not reported, and no outcomes specific to AD were reported because AD, constipation, asthma, and fever were aggregated for analysis. Patient outcomes included not returning unnecessarily for a visit within seven days, though results were not reported. The intervention led to significant improvement in trainees’ guidance adherence during joint clinic and after the intervention. Again, AD was grouped with three other diagnoses in the analysis.

Rea et al.26 performed a RCT in which attending physicians, pediatric residents, and nurse practitioners were randomized to use an EAP during patient visits or provide usual care. They observed significant improvements in the intervention group’s documentation of discussing diagnosis, eczema, existing emollient, and topical corticosteroid regimens. The intervention group was significantly more likely to recommend wet wraps and bleach baths, and to document a management or “step-up” plan. Intervention group providers were also more likely to document provision of written information. Patient outcomes were measured via survey, including Patient Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM), CDLQI, or IDQoL. Both the intervention and control groups had significant improvements in POEM, IDQoL, and CDLQI, but there was no significant difference between the groups. Eczema knowledge and understanding improved significantly in the EAP group, but only when controlling for demographics and clustering by provider.

Providing a treatment algorithm or guidelines with or without expert consultation appeared to improve clinician practice in two additional studies, but significance was not reported. Sánchez et al.18 reported most physicians met ET-FAD guideline recommendations while treating patients based on SCORAD scores. The authors did not, however, specify how adherence was tracked. Liau et al.27 conducted a qualitative study in which internal medicine residents received a pocket guide to eczema prescribing in the intervention group or no guide in the control group. The residents determined a prescription for a patient, consulted with a dermatologist, and adjusted the prescription if necessary. The intervention group required intervention on 7.6% of prescriptions, whereas the control group required intervention on 86.7% of prescriptions. However, the sample size was small and statistical significance was not reported. Most of the resident physicians said they would recommend the guide.

Interventions without significant results

Santer et al.28 performed a RCT in which they randomized parents/guardians of children with AD to either a website group, usual care by PCP, or website plus usual care by PCP. The authors did not find significant improvement in any of the patient outcomes, which included POEM, Dermatitis Family Impact (DFI), CDLQI, or IDQoL. In qualitative interviews, providers and parents/guardians felt the website was helpful.

Mathes et al.29 conducted a RCT in which pediatrics resident physicians in the intervention group took online spaced education (SE) on AD, warts, and molluscum while those in the control group took SE on acne and melanocytic nevi. Pretest and posttest knowledge were measured, and no significant improvement occurred in the intervention group’s posttest scores.

Chinn et al.30 performed a RCT in which the intervention group patients received a single, 30-minute consultation with a dermatology-trained nurse in the primary care setting. The control group received usual care. Patient outcomes were assessed using the CDLQI or IDQoL, and the Family Dermatitis Index (FDI). There were no significant improvements in patient outcomes between the two groups.

Le Roux et al.31 conducted a qualitative analysis of GPs’ perceptions regarding diagnosing and managing childhood AD. Semi-structured interviews were performed with GPs and analyzed thematically. Major themes included GP-reported uncertainty managing AD, lack of routine information and review, and perceived dissonance with parents of children with AD. While they established clinician needs and preference for education in AD, they did not address a specific intervention: PCP or patient outcomes. Significance is not applicable based on the study design.

Discussion

Expert consensus guidelines for AD have limitations for PCPs including: variation by source, information written for a target audience other than PCPs, and the complexity of treatment of AD that may require flexibility in interpreting the recommendations. Le Roux et al.31 validated the importance of more interventions to address this issue, with their findings that pediatric general practitioners largely feel uncertainty managing AD and that there is a lack of information and review on the subject. Fortunately, AD experts recognize this gap, and some attempted to tailor interventions or recommendations to the primary care setting.7 This systematic review identified 13 studies, including 8 RCTs, addressing or attempting to improve PCP management of AD.

Didactic education that included an interactive portion to use in clinical practice, such as employing an EAP in clinic and/or expert support available during the intervention, led to some improvements, such as fewer referrals to specialists,24 prescriptions requiring less expert intervention,27 improved understanding regarding medications, providing anticipatory guidance, understanding AD stages, and patient understanding,21 improvement in providers’ documentation and treatment recommendations, and improvement in families’ eczema knowledge and understanding after controlling for demographics and clustering by provider.26

Though some aspects of the PCPs’ training were not clear, having physicians follow ET-FAD treatment recommendations based on SCORAD score arguably led to the most significant, measured, clinical gains.18 The evidence from this study, and others that included employment of an evidence-based tool such as an EAP, support the need for clear, objective guidelines that PCPs can follow in clinical practice. Asthma action plans, which are similar to EAPs, were once thought to improve asthma outcomes but have more recently been shown to be no more effective than parent education, and in some cases lead to worse outcomes.32 Further study to elucidate the true efficacy of EAP use alone in comparison to periodic educational repetition for the provider and/or family is warranted.

The strictly educational interventions included in our review support the Davis et al.33 finding that didactic medical education alone does not typically cause physician performance change. One-time or brief interventions in particular do not tend to lead to sustained practice improvement and in addition, most studies included in this review did not track longitudinal changes. Though several of the educational interventions showed an improvement in posttest knowledge scores, they either did not assess or did not achieve practice changes or improved patient outcomes.22,23,29 While the educational component is likely a useful portion for future interventions, clinical impact should be measured to support the design and periodic refreshers such as Maintenance of Certification (MOC) are likely needed to sustain any positive impacts in participants who benefit positively from this type of education. The studies in which families utilized HCP with/without website support or were invited to attend one or more consultations with a dermatology-trained nurse in the primary care clinic also did not lead to significant changes as a result of the intervention.17,30 Finally, interventions involving results from pooled diagnoses were difficult to interpret in terms of efficacy.25

Based on the findings, several considerations might be useful for future interventions to ensure a clinically meaningful study design. We recommend assessment of the clinical impact of the intervention performed by including patient/family variables such as QoL, disease control, and disease severity. Ideally, provider and patient changes might be assessed longitudinally to determine whether improvements are retained over time. Our findings support the use of an educational intervention to increase provider knowledge, which should focus on evidence-based, expert consensus guidelines for primary care management of AD and likely be revisited periodically. Following the educational intervention, PCPs would likely benefit from following a therapeutic ladder or treatment algorithm such as those presented in expert consensus reports in order to implement their newfound knowledge based on objective patient criteria. The real-world practicality of a specialist or attending physician that would regularly offer treatment guidance in general practice is low.

There were several limitations. Studies had moderate-high risk-of-bias or moderate quality. We were unable to perform a meta-analysis or fully rank the studies in terms of their benefit due to the diversity of study design and outcomes that were measured. In conclusion, this systematic literature review highlights the need for many more studies that aim to improve primary care management of AD and an understanding of what is effective considering the breadth of the problem.
