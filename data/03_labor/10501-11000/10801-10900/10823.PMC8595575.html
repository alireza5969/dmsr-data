Surgical factors influencing wound complication after cochlear implantation: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Objective:

To identify perioperative surgical factors associated with wound complications following cochlear implantation (CI).

Data Sources:

PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases

Study Selection:

Eligible studies included peer-reviewed research in English evaluating wound complications (wound infection, skin flap breakdown/dehiscence, seroma/hematoma) following CI. Studies with paired samples were included in the meta-analysis.

Data Extraction:

Surgical factors (techniques and perioperative management) in CI and reported wound complications were examined. Level of evidence was assessed using the Oxford Centre for Evidence-based Medicine guidelines and bias was assessed using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool.

Data Synthesis:

Twenty-six studies representing 10,214 cochlear implantations were included. The overall wound complications rate was 3.1% (range 0.03–13.9%). Eleven studies contained paired data and were used for meta-analysis regarding three different surgical factors: incision length, implant placement method, and antibiotic usage. Longer incision lengths (≥7cm) demonstrated a higher risk of wound complications (risk ratio 2.27, p=0.02, CI 1.16–4.43). Different implant placement techniques (suture fixation versus periosteal pocket) (p=0.08, CI 0.92–3.69) and postoperative antibiotic regimens (postoperative use versus none) (p=0.68, CI=0.28–7.18) were not associated with differences in wound complication rates following CI.

Conclusions:

Overall rate of wound complications following CI is low. Shorter incision length is associated with lower risk of wound complications. Differences in perioperative techniques and practices regarding implant placement and antibiotic use were not associated with differences in wound complication rates. Considering the low number and quality of studies, there is a need for research in CI outcomes using paired sample prospective designs and standardized reporting.

INTRODUCTION

Cochlear implantation (CI) is a standard of care option for treating significant hearing impairment and consistent use of a cochlear implant can significantly improve the quality of lives of patients [1]. Like any surgical procedure, postoperative wound complications are a risk of cochlear implantation. Wound complications, such as infections, skip flap breakdown, wound dehiscence, hematoma, and seroma, can compromise the health of the patient and function of the implant. Postoperative wound complication rates range from 0.03% to 13.9%, with incidence of major wound complications (those requiring additional hospitalization and/or surgery) reaching as high as 2% [2–6]. In a small number of cases, major wound complications may necessitate explantation of the device [6,7,8].

While cochlear implantation is routinely performed around the world, there are many different surgical techniques and perioperative management strategies and there is a lack of widespread consensus on standardized CI techniques. From the location, size, and shape of the incision to the technique of placing and securing the receiver stimulator beneath the skin, each step of the procedure has variations from which a surgeon must choose [9]. The lack of a standardized technique for cochlear implantation may contribute to the varying rates of wound complications seen at institutions across the globe. A recent systematic review evaluated the efficacy of postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis with regards to wound infection following cochlear implantation [10], but there is a lack of quantitative meta-analysis data to assess for risk and association of surgical techniques and perioperative practices with wound complications. The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to identify the surgical factors and quantify the association of different techniques with wound complication rates following cochlear implantation.

METHODS

Search Strategy

This study was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval, as it is a systematic review of the literature. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis checklist was used to guide our review [11] (Figure 1). Our primary question was composed with the following PICOTS (population, intervention, comparisons, outcomes, time, study design) elements: 1) adults and/or children with hearing loss, who 2) underwent unilateral or bilateral cochlear implantation, with 3) description of surgical technique or perioperative management approach, with 4) reporting of a postoperative wound complication, 5) at any time following surgery, and 6) were reported in either retrospective or prospective research. Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) Studies reporting wound complications following cochlear implantation, 2) studies reporting wound complications with a description of the surgical technique and/or perioperative management (incision type, fixation method, antibiotic use), 3) original research in the form of prospective or retrospective observational studies or as a case series. We defined wound complication as any process that disrupts the normal healing of the surgical site (wound infection, wound dehiscence, necrosis, abscess, cellulitis, seroma, hematoma, and skin flap breakdown). Exclusion criteria included: 1) case reports or non-original research, 2) language other than English, 3) studies not reporting postoperative wound complications. Although we included studies without control/comparison groups in the systematic review for qualitative data analysis and synthesis, only studies with paired samples were included in the meta-analysis.

The search was developed using the National Library of Medicine’s medical subject headings (MeSH), allowing an inclusive search that encapsulated studies reporting wound complications following cochlear implantation. The search was conducted on 02/10/2020 in PubMED. No publication date restrictions were placed on the search. The search string is found in Appendix 1. After the initial search, Web of Science and Cochrane Database were searched using the same string. Duplicates between the three databases were removed. Finally, the bibliographies of included studies were searched to identify additional articles.

Study Selection

The studies retrieved from the search were reviewed by four authors. Titles and abstracts were initially reviewed with respect to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Titles/abstracts in question were reviewed and discussed by all four authors. Three authors conducted a full text review of the studies that passed abstract review, with all articles being reviewed by at least two authors. There were no disagreements between the authors regarding inclusion/exclusion. After full-text review, data extraction was conducted from the remaining studies.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Data collected from each study included: country of origin, study type, study dates, participant number, age group of patients (adult, pediatric, or both), etiology of hearing loss (if available), brand/model of cochlear implant (if available), surgical incision description, implant placement and fixation technique (suture fixation with drilling versus subperiosteal pocket with no drilling), perioperative management considerations (i.e. - antibiotic regimen, head wrapping), and reported wound complications (including the type of complication). Studies with paired samples were included for meta-analysis.

The level of evidence of each article was assessed based on the standard Oxford Centre for Evidence-based Medicine guidelines [12]. The quality of studies was assessed using the National Institute of Health Study Quality Assessment Tool [13]. This assessed bias based a number of measures that can be found on the NIH website https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health-topics/study-quality-assessment-tools. Heterogeneity of studies included in the meta-analysis was assessed using the I2 statistic test. An I2 value of 0–25%, 26–75%, and above 75% was considered low, moderate, or high, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

During the qualitative analysis of the included studies, we identified studies that reported specific surgical factors with paired samples. These specific factors included surgical incision length (with greater than or equal to 7cm being a standard incision and less than 7 cm being a minimally invasive incision), postoperative antibiotic usage (postoperative antibiotic use versus none), and implant placement/fixation method (drilled well with or without sutures versus subperiosteal pocket). Meta-analysis was conducted on these studies with paired samples for each surgical factor using a dichotomous outcome variable (wound complication versus no complication). We calculated a summary risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence interval with p-value using the inverse variance method with a random model. The analysis was performed with Cochrane Review Manager (RevMan 5.3.5, Copenhagen: Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014).

RESULTS

The initial search yielded 2923 studies (1,641 from PubMed, 1,233 from Web of Science, and 49 from Cochrane). After 799 duplicates were removed, 2124 papers remained. 1945 studies were excluded via title and abstract review. The remaining 179 studies underwent a full-text review. An additional 2 studies were identified during the review of the bibliographies of included studies. A total of 26 studies were selected for inclusion in the review and 12 had paired samples appropriate for quantitative meta-analysis (Table 1). The quality/level of bias assessment of these studies was assessed (Appendix 2). These studies came from 16 countries and the level of evidence ranged from 2b-4. Of the 26 studies, 10 were observational cohort studies, 15 were case series, and 1 was a case-control study. 16 studies involved both adult and pediatric patients, 6 studies involved only pediatric patients, 3 studies involved only adults, and 1 study did not report the age of the patients. A total of 10,214 cochlear implantations were performed in these studies with an overall wound complication rate of 3.1%. The studies did not include enough information on the specific type of wound complication to perform subgroup analysis.

Three surgical factors were identified that were consistently reported in the context of wound complication: incision length, implant receiver-stimulator (IRS) placement method, and antibiotic regimen. There were 12 studies that included paired samples as a part of their study design and were suitable for meta-analysis to calculate a pooled risk ratio for cochlear implant wound complications.

Incision length

We found 7 different types of incisions used by various authors and many did not describe their incision in the methodology. Based on the review of methodology of studies included, and other studies cited, less than 7cm was considered a cut-off for minimally invasive incisions and incision longer than or equal to ≥7cm was categorized as standard. Eleven studies were identified that reported both the length of the incision and the associated wound complication rate. Of these 11 studies, 3 studies compared patients receiving a minimally invasive incision to those receiving a standard incision [14–16]. For these 3 studies, a meta-analysis was performed (Figure 2). Patients with a longer incision had a significantly higher incidence of wound complication (2.9%) compared with patients with a minimally invasive incision (1.2%) (RR 2.27, p=0.02, CI 1.16–4.43). There was low heterogeneity between these studies (I2=0%). There were an additional 7 studies that did not have paired samples that reported use of either a minimally invasive or standard incision (Table 2). Of the unpaired studies, 2 studies reported a wound complication incidence of 6.2% with the use of a standard incision [17,18]. 5 studies reported an overall wound complication incidence of 3.7% with the use of a minimally invasive incision [4, 19–22].

Implant placement/fixation

Eleven studies reported wound complication rates in the context of IRS fixation. Five of these studies compared cochlear implantation with suture fixation of the IRS to cochlear implantation with a subperiosteal pocket fixation of the IRS using a paired-sample design and, thus, a meta-analysis was conducted with these studies (Figure 3) [5,16,23–25]. While well-drilling was common among the suture fixation studies, not all studies commented on whether a well was drilled or not. No significant difference was found between wound complication rates in patients whose IRS was secured with suture fixation (2.3%) and patients whose IRS was placed using a subperiosteal pocket (1.1%) (RR 1.85, p=0.08, CI 0.92–3.69). There was low heterogeneity between these studies (I2=0%). An additional 6 studies reported wound complication incidences with the use of either suture fixation or the subperiosteal pocket technique (Table 2) [15,22,26–29]. In 4 studies using suture fixation there was a cumulative incidence of wound complication of 2.3%. Two studies reported a cumulative wound complication incidence of 1.3% using the subperiosteal pocket technique.

Antibiotic regimen

Fourteen studies were identified that reported on antibiotic usage and wound complications in cochlear implantation. There was a wide range of different types of antibiotics used and significant variation in duration and dosage of antibiotics; however, 4 of these studies compared used similar designs to compare wound complications between patients receiving a single intraoperative antibiotic dose and patients receiving intraoperative along with postoperative antibiotics [20,30–32]. These studies were appropriate for meta-analysis (Figure 4). With regards to wound complication incidence, no significant difference was found between the use of a single intraoperative dose (2.3%) and those receiving intraoperative dose with a postoperative course (1.7%) (RR 1.41, p=0.68, CI=0.28–7.18). There was moderate heterogeneity of these studies (I2=72%). Nine additional studies were identified that did not have paired groups (Table 2) [4,6,15,21,29,33–36]. In 3 studies using a single intraoperative antibiotic dose, there was a wound complication incidence of 4.4%. The remaining 6 studies reported a cumulative incidence rate of 4.1% using a postoperative antibiotic course.

DISCUSSION

Despite advances in cochlear implantation, wound complication remains a problem associated with severe consequences. Although rare, with a cumulative rate of 3.1% across 10,214 cochlear implants from our review, wound complications can be catastrophic in cochlear implantation. In some patients, explantation is necessary, with the possibility of reimplantation not guaranteed. To the best of our knowledge, our systematic review is the first of its kind to identify and quantitatively analyze surgical factors that may influence wound complication rates after cochlear implantation. Our meta-analysis identified that incisions longer than 7cm have a 2.27 times greater risk of postoperative wound complication than incisions shorter than 7cm. It also found that there is no significant difference in risk between two common IRS placement/fixation methods (suture fixation to the bone versus subperiosteal pocket) or antibiotic regimen (intraoperative dose versus postoperative course).

Patients receiving longer incisions are at a higher risk of postoperative wound complications. Minimizing scalp and subcutaneous tissue trauma is vital to maintaining a healthy skin flap and minimizing wound complications [37]. A longer incision has the potential to interrupt local vascular supply which could subsequently lead to necrosis [38–40]. Blood to the postauricular area is predominantly supplied by the postauricular artery and the occipital artery. Longer incisions have the potential to interfere with both parts of this dual blood supply [40,41]. Venous return in this area runs alongside arterial supply, so longer incisions may also interrupt venous flow, increasing the risk of edema as well [37, 40]. Neither analysis nor the individual included papers take into consideration the specific conformation of the incision or underlying patient factors (such as diabetes, vascular disease, heart disease, smoking, nutrition), which could also play a major role in wound healing and the health of a post-operative skin flap. However, based on this data, incision length should be taken into consideration during cochlear implantation.

A wide range of surgical techniques are employed by experienced surgeons in placement of the IRS of a cochlear implant. The traditional placement involves drilling a bony well with fixation of the IRS with sutures; however, the subperiosteal tight pocket method has been touted to shorten operative times and minimize soft tissue dissection. [27,41] The creation of small bony channels for sutures placement has been reported to cause rare complications such as CSF leak and intracranial bleeding [42]. In contrast, the subperiosteal pocket method does not necessitate suturing or drilling into the temporal bone. Another potential differentiator between the two methods is time. When compared to the suture fixation method, the subperiosteal tight pocket technique has reported reduced time in the OR by as much as 30% [41]. One study from this review reported advantages of the traditional technique in a sample of over 1500 CI cases. [5] This study describes similar wound complication rates between the two techniques but found higher revision rates in the subperiosteal pocket technique due to IRS migration [5]. Although our meta-analysis did not report a significant difference in the risk of wound complication between the two IRS fixation methods, the overall data, including unpaired samples, showed a trend toward higher rates of wound complications in the standard fixation method.

Standardization of perioperative antibiotic regimen in cochlear implantation has not been established. This is underscored by the heterogeneity of the data in our review. Previously, Anne et al conducted a systematic review on intraoperative versus postoperative antibiotic approaches in cochlear implantation. They described insufficient evidence to make a definitive decision [10]. A 2004 Cochrane review found no benefit to antibiotic prophylaxis of any kind when compared to the absence of antibiotic administration in clean and clean-contaminated otologic surgery. However, this study does not specifically analyze risk of infection in cochlear implantation [43]. A study on general recommendations for antibiotic use in otolaryngologic surgery supports the use of antibiotic prophylaxis in surgeries whose complications are severe; however, they do not report any additional benefit to a postoperative antibiotic course over an intraoperative dose [44]. Our meta-analysis found no significant difference in wound complications between patients receiving only a single intraoperative antibiotic dose and those receiving an intraoperative and a postoperative course. Other factors to consider in postoperative antibiotics use includes cost (especially costs related to postoperative complications), adverse reactions, and resistance.

There are limitations to our meta-analysis and review, as well as the studies included in the review. The most glaring issue is the lack of rigorous research in this field that would be necessary to determine factors that influence wound complications. That would require studies with multivariate analyses and/or paired cohort designs but there is a deficiency of these types of studies in CI research. By its very nature, a meta-analysis excludes single arm unpaired studies thus it is difficult to account for that body of research in quantitative analysis and this also results in a lower number of included in quantitative analyses. Nevertheless, in this study we included single arm studies and presented their outcomes in a more qualitative fashion. Reporting bias of complications are historically a limiting factor in surgical research fields. There is no universal reporting system or categorization system for complications following cochlear implantation [45]. Our review includes 26 studies spanning nearly 3 decades. Within these studies there is an inherent great variability in the techniques of cochlear implantation, as well as dramatic changes in the conformation and design of cochlear implants. We found 7 different types of incisions used by various authors and many did not describe their incision in the methodology. However, we did identify some degree of consistency in recording of incision length and thus this was used as a variable to quantitatively assess complication rates. Based on the review of methodology of studies included, and other studies cited, 7cm was considered a cut-off for minimally invasive incisions and incision ≥7cm was categorized as standard. Even with this simplification, many studies did not describe the length of incision used and could not be included in our study, thus limiting the sample size of our review. The analysis of antibiotics faced similar constraints. Our categorization was based on duration of antibiotic course. However, for the postoperative antibiotic course, there was no standardized regimen described in the literature. Our category of postoperative antibiotic course consisted of any study that administered more than a singular intraoperative dose of antibiotics. Thus, the duration of antibiotic course within this category of our analysis ranged from 5 days to 6 weeks. The classes of antibiotics used in our review also varied from study to study, which may place another limitation on our analysis. None of the studies used multivariate analysis to assess the risk of post-cochlear implant wound complication, making it difficult to identify the exact risk of wound complication with any particular risk factor. Considering all the variability in surgical techniques and perioperative management, there is a pressing need to develop minimum reporting standards for studies involving cochlear implantation.

CONCLUSION

Patients receiving cochlear implantation are at a low risk for postoperative wound complications. However, shorter incision lengths are associated with a lower overall risk of wound complication than those with a longer incision. There is no clear difference in wound complication rates when comparing subperiosteal pocket technique and suture fixation technique. Furthermore, there is no clear evidence of differences in wound complication rates between a postoperative antibiotic course and a single intraoperative dose. Further research is needed to identify the key factors influencing adverse events and wound complications in cochlear implantation.
