Abstract

BACKGROUND:

Certain racial/ethnic minority groups have a higher risk of developing dementia, yet studies have demonstrated that they often have limited knowledge and understanding of this disease. An increasing number of educational and advocacy programs have been developed to promote dementia knowledge. We aimed to assess current evidence and quality regarding educational interventions for promoting dementia knowledge.

METHODS:

We searched for intervention studies published in English that focused on educational interventions for promoting dementia knowledge among racial/ethnic minority groups. We identified twenty-five relevant studies through PubMed, PsycInfo, CINAHL, and Scopus, using tailored search terms. We screened titles and abstracts, reviewed full texts, synthesized relevant evidence, and evaluated the studies’ quality based on the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. Relevant intervention studies took place in communities, hospitals or clinics, schools, and online.

RESULTS:

Most studies were conducted in the United States (n = 21), followed by the UK (n = 3). Over half of the studies included Asian/Pacific Islander groups (n=14), followed by Black groups (n=12), and Hispanic groups (n=11). The intervention delivery mode varied across studies—from workshops hosted in a faith community to talk shows on YouTube. Target populations included middle-aged and older adults, caregivers and family members, health students and professionals, and elementary school students. Common content included symptoms and signs of dementia, protective and risk factors, and local resources. The assessment of study outcomes varied across studies. Improvement in dementia knowledge and attitudes towards dementia was reported in many studies. Among the included studies, intervention satisfaction was high. The overall quality of the interventions was low.

CONCLUSION:

Formally evaluated educational interventions promoting dementia knowledge are at an early stage. Existing published interventions showed adequate acceptability and promise in promoting better understanding and awareness of dementia in minority groups. More well-designed randomized controlled trials are needed.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) are projected to affect 75 million adults worldwide by 2030.1 The World Health Organization declared dementia a public health priority in 2015.1 In the United States, approximately one-in-ten White older adults have dementia, whereas the prevalence is higher among Black/African American (18.6%) and Hispanic adults (14%).2 This may be due to socioeconomic factors, health behaviors, and/or health risk factors such as the increased prevalence of heart disease among the Black and Hispanic population in the US.3,4,5 Despite having a greater risk of developing dementia, racial and ethnic minority groups in the US are more likely to receive delayed dementia diagnosis and face increased barriers to accessing dementia care than White individuals.3,6 Asian Americans are less likely than White Americans to receive dementia care after the diagnosis.6 Two thirds of Black/African Americans, one third of Asian Americans, and 40% of American Indians and Hispanic Americans believe their race/ethnicity makes it more difficult to receive dementia care.2 Similarly, ethnic minority older adults in the UK underutilize dementia resources when compared to White British groups, due to barriers to access.7,8

Mistrust of healthcare systems among racial/ethnic minority groups and cultural stigma surrounding dementia result in reduced help-seeking.1,8 These attitudes are fostered by health system factors like communication barriers, lack of minority representation and cultural awareness among providers, and poor understanding of dementia among racial/ethnic groups.8 Promoting dementia knowledge among minority racial and ethnic groups might be the first step toward improving early identification of dementia.1 Prior research has suggested that over one half of non-White Americans attribute dementia to a normal part of aging.2,9,10

To address disparities in ADRD, increasing effort has been devoted to improving the early identification of dementia among racial and ethnic minority groups. National, and international health policies have included specific goals to enhance public awareness of ADRD.1,11,12 Since then, studies have been designed to evaluate measures of dementia knowledge and promote dementia knowledge in local communities, hospitals, and schools.10,13–17 Previous systematic reviews have focused on dementia care interventions among caregivers and healthcare providers that increase competency in caring for persons with ADRD.18–22 However, there are no systematic reviews that evaluated interventions aimed to promote dementia knowledge among racial and ethnic minority groups. Therefore, this review aims to assess whether and to what extent existing interventions increase dementia knowledge among racial/ethnic minority participants.

METHODS

We first searched the Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and PROSPERO Registry and identified no similar reviews. PubMed, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and Scopus, were our primary data sources. We applied an exhaustive search strategy by using variations of search terms related to ADRD, awareness, knowledge, and public educational programs/campaigns/interventions in racial/ethnic minority populations (Supplementary Table 1). We included studies published in English between 2015 and 2020 as national organizations have started to devote more efforts to increase public awareness of ADRD since 2015.23,24 We performed further searches via reference lists of included articles.

Overall, we identified 3813 studies from the four databases and four through hand-searching references (Figure 1). After we removed 1732 duplicates, 2081 studies remained. After title/abstract and full-text screening, a total of 25 studies were included. We assessed the eligibility of each article independently and discussed and resolved any disagreement.

We extracted the following information independently from each included article: author/year, setting, study population, study design and intervention content (Supplementary Table 2) and main results (Tables 1–2). We extracted information into a matrix, then reviewed and resolved any discrepancies within the study team. We evaluated the study quality using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).25 This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42020206304).

RESULTS

Design

Most studies were conducted in the US (n=21), three were conducted in the UK, and one in Australia. The majority of the studies (n=17) were conducted in local communities. Other settings included hospitals or clinics (n=2), institutions of higher learning (n=3), online (n=2), and an elementary school (n=1). Most studies used quasi-experimental design (n=21). Of these quasi-experimental studies, 13 used one group pre-posttest design and five used one-group with posttest alone. There were three randomized controlled trials (RCTs)16,26,27 and one intervention development study.28 Based on the MMAT, the overall study quality was low to moderate (Supplementary Table 3).25

Studies incorporated culturally tailored methods in study design and/or dissemination of dementia knowledge. A portion of interventions were offered in multiple languages (n=14). Non-English languages included Spanish13,29–33, Mandarin/Cantonese6,34–37, Vietnamese28, Punjabi, Hindi, and Urdu38, Indian, African, Caribbean, and East and Central European “community languages” 10. Beyond language, cultural tailoring included using community based participatory research (CBPR) models to create “culturally sensitive” materials13 and establishing a Community Advisory Board.32 One study consulted with the Australian Vietnamese community via focus groups to inform cultural details and language of their talking book intervention28 and another study incorporated Hispanic presenters who received training on cultural sensitivity and community health.31 One study included dementia knowledge of cultural differences in coping styles, stress models, and caregiving for African American caregivers.39 Studies were also conducted in culturally sensitive settings. For example, one study held educational sessions at Black/African American churches as these are pillars of trust for African Americans in the Atlanta area of the US.14 Additionally, a study observed religious customs in refreshments, music, venue, and intervention timing.10

Characteristics of participants in the included studies

All studies included racial and/or ethnic minority participants. As relevant terminology for racial and ethnic groups varies slightly between countries, a breakdown of racial and ethnic groups by country of study is included as Table 3. In the US, there were 11 studies targetting Hispanic/Latino groups and 10 studies targeting Black/African American groups and Asian groups which were primarily Chinese or unspecified. In the UK, Black groups (Black British, Black African, Black Caribbean, African and Caribbean) were the most targeted group (n=4) followed by studies targeting Asian groups (n=2). The one study conducted in Australia included Vietnamese participants. Among the included studies, 56% targeted individuals of a specific race/ethnicity (n=14). Five studies focused on Hispanic/Latino populations, three focused on Black populations, and three focused on Asian populations. Another three studies were conducted through YouTube in Cantonese but did not report the race/ethnicity of their audiences.34,35,37 Among the 11 studies that included participants from more than one racial/ethnic group, seven included non-Hispanic White individuals. Within the 11 studies, three included racial/ethnic subgroups such as Pakistani, Bangladeshi, and Black Caribbean.10,26,38

About two-thirds of the included studies targeted the general public. Four studies targeted adults aged 40+.26–28,37 Three studies targeted older adults (aged 60+).13,15,23 Other groups included nursing assistants, hospital workers and health profession students (n = 5), and caregivers and family members of dementia patients (n=4). One study targeted 4th and 5th grade elementary school students.15

Intervention

Content

Interventions covered five main content areas: ADRD-related behaviors and symptoms (n=22); Basic background knowledge of causes, development, and diagnosis of ADRD (n=20); ADRD prevention, risk factors, and brain health promotion (n=7); Support resources and seeking help for ADRD (n=10); Communication between patients, healthcare providers, caregivers, and family (n=6).

In addition to these themes, seven studies covered coping practices for families and caregivers of persons living with ADRD.29,33,35,38,40–42 Six studies designed interventions to combat dementia stigma and myths.10,26,39–41,43 Three studies contained content on patient-centered dementia care.16,29,43 Four studies provided education on the content of cognitive assessments.13,32,37,43

Eight studies followed up with study participants after intervention completion. The follow-up period varied, from 30 days27 to one year.13 Fourteen studies stopped data collection immediately after the intervention. There were three studies that used data collected from YouTube analytics and collected data 17 months,37 1 and 2 years,34 and 3 and 6 years35 after posting their educational videos. However, no information was reported regarding whether they removed the videos from YouTube.34,35,37

Delivery mode

Technology was often incorporated into intervention delivery. YouTube was the main intervention delivery tool for three studies and videos could be accessed by the public.34,35,37 Other studies incorporated videos or online modules in their interventions (n=9). One RCT made use of text-messaging to disseminate dementia information.27

Outcomes

Dementia Knowledge

The most common study outcome was dementia knowledge (n=17). Of the 17 studies measuring dementia knowledge, four used the Alzheimer’s Disease Knowledge Scale (ADKS). Other instruments included the Knowledge of Alzheimer’s Disease/Dementia Scale ([KAD], n=2), Dementia Symptom Knowledge Assessment ([DSKA], n=1), Epidemiology/Etiology Disease Scale (n=1), and the Dementia Knowledge Assessment Tool Version 2 (n=1). The KAD was also used in one study to measure ADRD attitudes. Eight studies used self-developed measurements including Likert scales, multiple-choice, true/false, and open-ended questions. One study did not specify the instrument used to measure dementia knowledge.42

Ten of the 17 studies that assessed dementia knowledge reported a statistically significant increase in dementia knowledge over the course of the intervention. Two other studies used the KAD as the outcome assessment and reported improvements in dementia knowledge that were not statistically significant. Similarly, one study reported a marginal increase in dementia knowledge based on an unspecified instrument. Two studies did not report the significance of results.13,38 The one study of the 17 assessing dementia knowledge that did not report an increase in knowledge was of a one-group posttest design and thus lacked baseline measures.10 After this intervention, groups unterstood the biological basis of memory loss, but misconceptions on the causes of dementia remained. One study used qualitative interviews to measure dementia knowledge and participants reported increased dementia knowledge post-intervention.28

Attitudes Towards Dementia

Nine studies assessed the effects of the interventions on participants’ attitudes or stigma pertaining to ADRD. Three studies used self-developed questionnaires to measure ADRD attitudes, three studies used the Dementia Attitudes Scale,16,40,43 one study used the Attitudes of People from Ethnic Minorities to Help-Seeking for Dementia questionnaire26, one study used the DSKA,44 and another study used the KAD.28

Among the nine studies, three reported statistically significant improvement in attitudes using the Dementia Attitudes Scale.16,40,43 These three studies targeted health professional students, were conducted at institutions of higher learning, and utilized both learning material and hands-on experiences. One study that focused on a Hispanic/Latino group found statistically significant improvements only in certain attitudes.33 There was no significant difference before and after the intervention in attitudes towards the value of early detection, learning whether they were at high risk for dementia or the belief that people with dementia deserve more sympathy than those with other chronic diseases. Five studies did not report statistically significant changes in attitudes towards dementia.10,26,36,41,44

Intervention Satisfaction

Intervention satisfaction was assessed in nine studies.14,26,28,31,32,36,38,39,41 Intervention satisfaction was measured based on Likert-type scales (n=2), interviews or focus groups (n=6), and a single yes/no question.36 All studies reported positive intervention satisfaction. Four of the six studies assessing intervention satisfaction via focus groups had the focus groups at an early stage to inform intervention development.28,32,39,41 Two other studies reported that the intervention satisfaction was higher in some subgroups. Specifically, for one US-based video intervention, the group who found the intervention very helpful had higher baseline stigma toward dementia and lived in the US for a shorter period than the group who found it less helpful.36

Other Outcomes

Other study outcomes include dementia care competence/confidence, which was measured by the Confidence in Dementia Scale,40 and the DSKA.44 Both studies found improvements in care confidence/competence.

Two studies assessing help seeking behaviors26,32 found increased interests in visiting doctors for memory problems26 or obtaining a brief memory screening after the intervention.32 One study found that the intervention generated more interest among participants in learning more about dementia.13 Only one out of all 25 tracked interest in research participation over time.30 After the intervention, 41.1% of participants enrolled into the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center Cohort. An increase in research participation attitudes was also reported.

Four studies assessed improvement in awareness/use of services and social support as an outcome.28,38,39,42 Two studies conducted qualitative interviews and found that participants established new social support through the intervention.38,39 Another study reported increased recognition of the importance of sharing information with others.42 Another study conducted in Australia reported an increased knowledge in accessing information and services.28

Three YouTube-based studies assessed watch time and viewership as the outcomes. Two of the three studies reported increased watch time and number of viewers over the study period.34,35 One study reported a significant increase in the number of viewers who were older adults (aged 55+) over a two-year period.34 However, this finding was only partially supported by another study. Specifically, Zheng, 2017 reported that while YouTube videos reached a greater audience, talk-based workshops reached a more older adults.

Four studies assessed challenges to obtaining dementia knowledge among study participants. The following barriers were identified through interviews and focus groups: lack of culturally tailored resources,6 access to care difficulties due to language/cultural differences,6,10 stigma around dementia,39 and mistrust in healthcare services.42 Figure 2 summarizes the findings for each main outcome.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review identified and evaluated existing interventions on promoting knowledge of ADRD in racial and ethnic minority groups. A total of 25 studies were included and most used a quasi-experimental design. Black and Hispanic/Latino groups were largely represented among the US-based studies and Black groups were the most common focus of UK studies. Many US studies focused on Asian groups, however there was a lack of diversity of Asian groups as most of these studies focused on Chinese Americans. The single study from Australia targeted Vietnamese participants. Overall, some studies reported improvements in dementia knowledge, attitudes towards dementia, and awareness of dementia related services. Still, the outcome measures varied across studies, and the overall study quality was low to moderate.

Among all US-studies, American Indians were not represented. A recent population-based study suggested that American Indians have the second highest incidence of dementia in the US.5,12 Interventions that aim to increase dementia knowledge among American Indians are much needed.

Studies noted that their methods were culturally tailored in terms of setting, education content, dissemination, and 14 interventions were offered in non-English languages. Culturally tailored interventions offered in preferred languages are more likely to engage study populations. One RCT found that the group presented with culturally tailored information (i.e culturally tailored text messages that incorporate Black/African American colloquialisms, idioms, and content etc.) had a greater increase in dementia knowledge than the group receiving general messages untailored to Black/African Americans.27 The success of culturally tailored interventions is consistent with prior studies that focused on providing education on cancer, HIV, and obesity to Black/African American populations by involving the target community in intervention design and using valued cultural settings like barbershops to disseminate health knowledge.45–47 Similarly, faith-based interventions in Black communities have successfully disseminated dementia knowledge.14,48,49 Therefore, when designing interventions that target racial/ethnic minority groups, it is critical to incorporate culturally tailored strategies into the study design, such as: delivering the intervention in trusted community settings, limiting barriers to access and literacy requirements, offering quality content in preferred languages, and using validated instruments tailored to the language and culture of the target population.

Technology was explored in multiple interventions as a dissemination tool. This method may be particularly useful during a pandemic. Still, when designing the intervention delivery mode, the sociodemographic characteristics of the target audiences must be considered. Prior studies have suggested that older Black/African American adults are disproportionately affected by poor-literacy and lack of stable internet access.50 Black older adults were also found to have a high level of religious participation.51 Therefore, when promoting dementia knowledge in this population, barriers were addressed via use of low-literacy text messages rather than via app that requires internet access27 and recruitment via faith-based organizations14. Among all studies, three used YouTube as an intervention delivery mode, and all reported an increase in video engagement over time. However, one of the three studies reported that talk-based workshops reached a more targeted age group audience (i.e., older adults). Therefore, YouTube-based interventions may be more beneficial for increasing dementia knowledge and awareness in younger generations. As these three studies only focused on Chinese-speaking viewers, more research is needed to assess the efficacy of YouTube-based interventions across different groups.

Most interventions covered the basic background of ADRD, behaviors, and symptoms. Only a few studies included information on dementia-specific local resources. Although professional organizations have developed materials related to dementia resources, participants from four included studies identified several barriers related to accessing dementia information and services.6,10,39,42 Prior research has suggested that persons with dementia and their caregivers often found it difficult to navigate the healthcare system and obtain relevant information about dementia.52,53 Therefore, future interventions are needed to incorporate information about local resources that can more easily be accessed by older adults and their families. This may be especially important for racial and ethnic minority groups who have been found to access local resources for dementia less than White populations.7 Additionally, only two of the included studies assessed participants’ attitudes towards discussing memory concerns with healthcare providers or getting cognitive testing. As improved knowledge and awareness of dementia may lead to help-seeking behaviors and ultimately early identification of dementia, future interventions should move beyond solely assessing participants’ attitudes towards help-seeking. Interventions should incorporate healthcare utilization measures to evaluate whether these education interventions increase utilizations of brain health services such the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Annual Wellness Visits.54 This focus may reduce disparities in diagnosis and early intervention of ADRD.

We found that intervention content and outcome measures varied greatly across studies and few studies used standard, validated instruments. The ADKS is a well-accepted measure, yet only 4 studies used it to evaluate improvements in dementia knowledge. One barrier to using validated instruments might be the lack of standardized and validated measures in non-English languages. Of the interventions offered in non-English languages, only two used standard measures of dementia knowledge.29,30 Therefore, as older adult populations become more diverse, there is a need for translating and validating existing measures into non-English languages as well as developing linguistically and culturally appropriate measures.

Only one study was designed to target elementary school children.15 This population was not widely included in the current interventions but could be a beneficial target for increasing awareness of dementia across the lifespan. In the US, Black/African American, Hispanic/Latino, and other racial/ethnic minority groups are more likely to live in multigenerational households than non-Hispanic Whites.55 Therefore, targeting children could be a beneficial way to disseminate dementia information to both children and their family members.55,56

This review has identified several strengths in the current interventions. First, most studies included targeted recruitment strategies and found overall high interests in intervention participation, despite the strong stigma surrounding dementia among certain racial/ethnic minority communities. Studies also incorporated culturally sensitive design, adapted content, community settings, and many interventions were offered in other languages.

Findings from this systematic review have several clinical implications. First, the identified intervention content is a valuable resource for clinicians as they plan to provide education on dementia to patients and their families from diverse backgrounds. Clinicians should also direct patients to more culturally tailored interventions and resources through trusted pillars of their communities as patients may be more likely to access those resources. Additionally, clinicians may incorporate these culturally tailoring strategies identified from this review as they develop their own education materials and/or community outreach programs to promote awareness of dementia. Still, there were several limitations to the included studies. First, the overall study quality is low to moderate. Only three RCTs were included and many quasi-experimental studies did not include a control group. Thus, more RCTs are necessary to comprehensively assess intervention efficacies. Additionally, most interventions were short in duration and failed to follow-up with participants over time. Most outcome data collection ended immediately after the education dissemination. Sustained time is needed between the educational component and collection of additional outcome measures to assess the intervention efficacy remains over time. Finally, very few studies assessed whether increased knowledge leads to help-seeking behavior. Therefore, high-quality educational interventions for racial and ethnic minority populations in these English-speaking countries are much needed.

Limitations

One major limitation of this systematic review arises from heterogeneous study designs and outcome measures, which made it difficult to compare the results between studies. We also limited our review to studies published between 2015–2020. Still, this range should capture the most up-to-date envidence because, the CDC and Alzheimer’s Association have started to devote more efforts to increase public awareness of ADRD since 2015.23 Additionally, the review was limited to studies published in English. Thus, findings from other non-English articles are unknown. Lastly, we may have missed some community, healthcare, and school-based programs that have not been formally evaluated and published as peer-reviewed journal articles in this review.

Conclusion

There remains a need for high quality educational interventions to promote dementia knowledge among racial/ethnic minority groups. Existing interventions have reported moderate success in improving dementia knowledge in the short term. Dissemination of culturally adapted dementia education in these communities may lead to greater understanding of dementia, reduced stigma, increased trust in the healthcare system, and help-seeking behaviors. More well-designed educational interventions are needed to help to diminish racial/ethnic disparities in dementia.

Key Points

  • More high-quality educational interventions for minority populations are much needed: larger sample sizes, use of control groups, collection of baseline statistics, and a long term follow up period

  • Validated, culturally tailored measures need to be available in more non-English languages

  • Interventions targeting American Indians and more diverse Asian groups are highly needed

Why Does This Paper Matter?

Interventions that improve dementia knowledge are needed to reduce disparities in dementia risk for racial and ethnic minority groups
