Pathways of personality and learning risk for addictive behaviors: A systematic review of mediational research on the acquired preparedness model

Abstract

Objective:

The Acquired Preparedness (AP) model proposes that impulsive personality traits predispose some individuals to learn certain behavior-outcome associations (expectancies), and that these expectancies in turn influence the escalation of risky behaviors. This theory has been applied to the development of behaviors such as drinking, drug use, gambling, and disordered eating. In the current study, we aimed to summarize empirical tests of this model over the 20 years since it was proposed.

Method:

We used a descriptive approach to summarize tests of mediation across 50 studies involving n = 21,715 total participants.

Results:

We observed a consistent effect of personality on expectancies (median effect size = .22), of expectancies on behavior (.24), and a small mediated effect (.05) of personality on behavior via expectancies. Impulsive traits that involve positive or negative affect showed the most consistent support for AP, as did positive expectancies. Most studies testing AP focused on alcohol, but research on other behaviors also showed support for AP.

Conclusions:

The literature appears to support a small mediated effect consistent with the AP model. Future research should continue to clarify which AP pathways are most influential in explaining risky behaviors, and supplement correlational research with experimental and quasi-experimental designs.

INTRODUCTION

1 |

Impulsive personality traits are consistent and powerful predictors of current and future addictive behaviors; this has been shown consistently across a range of samples and methodologies (for meta-analyses see Berg et al., 2015; Coskunpinar et al., 2013; Griffith et al., 2010; Stautz & Cooper, 2013). Accounting for this relation has been the topic of much study – several pathways from personality to substance use have received empirical support, highlighting the importance of factors as varied as genes, development, social learning, and cognitive learning (Chassin et al., 2016; Littlefield & Sher, 2010). Social and cognitive approaches hold strong appeal for interventionists in part because clinical interventions seek to alter behavior by changing social contexts, attitudes, and thoughts. Understanding personality-related biases in social learning might help to explain the relations between personality and risky behaviors. The Acquired Preparedness (AP) model (Davis et al., 2018; Smith & Anderson, 2001) is a theoretical model focused on integrating personality research and learning theory into conceptions of risk for addictive behaviors, and as such has received much empirical attention over the past 20 years since its formulation. Given the accumulation of studies testing the AP model and the disparate methods, samples, and findings of these studies, we sought to integrate and summarize the current literature by describing and quantifying the relations hypothesized by the AP model. In doing so, we hoped to highlight the strengths and limitations of the current literature and provide suggestions for future directions for research on the AP model.

Integrating personality and learning history: The acquired preparedness model

1.1 |

The AP model describes a developmental process through which individuals with certain personality traits possess a latent tendency, or preparedness, to develop certain associations, often expressed as explicit beliefs, about alcohol (Smith & Anderson, 2001). These individuals then may acquire these beliefs via learning experiences, including direct experience, exposure to information, or vicarious learning. For example, an adolescent who tends to act impulsively when experiencing strong emotions might develop the belief that drinking alcohol on a stressful day helps to reduce those negative emotions, and based on this belief begin to drink more often over his early twenties. Notably, in Smith and Anderson’s (2001) original work, they focused on trait disinhibition, which represents a broad, higher-order dimension of personality related to agreeableness and conscientiousness in the Big Five framework (Costa & McCrae, 2008) and planning and persistence in the UPPS framework (Whiteside & Lynam, 2001). In the ensuing years, the AP framework has been tested with respect to a broader set of interrelated traits (adding in traits concerning positive and negative affect) broadly defined as impulsive traits (Sharma et al., 2014), thus we frame the following investigation as one of impulsive traits. Moreover, the AP model was originally proposed with a focus on alcohol use, but has been applied to other risky behaviors, including cannabis use, smoking, disordered eating, gambling, and even depression.

The AP model is an application of person-environment transaction theory (Buss, 1987; Caspi & Roberts, 2001) which emphasizes that individuals accumulate different learning experiences and learn differently from them as a function of their personalities. As a result, individuals develop different beliefs and associations between behaviors and outcomes (i.e., drinking makes me less nervous at parties; Goldman et al., 1999). In the case of impulsive traits and alcohol use, for example, AP posits that impulsive individuals experience both increased exposure to alcohol and biases in learning from the same alcohol-related experiences. These differences lead to stronger beliefs about alcohol, which in turn influence drinking behavior. Figure 1 summarizes the conceptual model hypothesized by AP.

Separate literatures support each of the hypothesized links in the AP model in cross-sectional, experimental, and correlational designs. Research has robustly linked personality traits to risky behaviors, personality traits to expectancies for risky behaviors, and expectancies to risky behaviors (for review, see Davis et al., 2018; Smith & Anderson, 2001). For alcohol use and problems, impulsive personality traits have been linked to alcohol use and problems in several reviews and meta-analyses, with Berg and colleagues reporting moderate meta-analytic effect sizes around .30 (Berg et al., 2015; Stautz & Cooper, 2013). These impulsive traits have been found to predict both positive and negative alcohol expectancies in research predating the AP model (Brown & Munson, 1987; Leonard & Blane, 1988; Williams & Clark, 1998), and this link has been corroborated in the studies presented in this review. Thirdly, the link between alcohol expectancies and drinking/alcohol problems is supported by literature using a variety of methods, including correlational studies (Christiansen et al., 1989; Colder et al., 2014; Goldman et al., 1999, 2006) and studies involving experimental manipulation of expectancies (i.e., expectancy challenge interventions; Jones et al., 2001; Scott-Sheldon et al., 2012).

Given recent treatment development efforts based on tailoring interventions toward certain types of personality risk (Conrod et al., 2007, 2013; Litten et al., 2015), as well as interventions based on changing substance-related associative learning (Mogoaşe et al., 2014; Schoenmakers et al., 2010; Scott-Sheldon et al., 2012), a review of the empirical support for the AP model is both timely and warranted.

Specific pathways of acquired preparedness

1.2 |

It is impossible to characterize research on AP without considering specificity: does the AP model capture a risk process specific to a particular impulsive style, or is it likely to describe sociocognitive learning across a range of impulsive traits and target behaviors? Does the AP model, initially formulated with regards to alcohol use, describe the developmental trajectory of other risky behaviors? In the following section, we briefly discuss issues related to measurement and conceptualization of each construct in the AP model and their implications for evaluating AP evidence.

Impulsive traits

1.2.1 |

In the current review, we adopted an empirically-derived, hierarchical model of impulsive traits to summarize findings (Cyders & Smith, 2008;King et al., 2014; Sharma et al., 2014). Sharma et al. (2014) subjected a broad range of measures, including the UPPS (Whiteside & Lynam, 2001), NEO-PI-R (Costa & McCrae, 1985), and MPQ (Tellegen, 2000), to exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis. The authors found support for three impulsive traits, one of which consists of two lower-order traits. The three higher-order traits are: Neuroticism, which encompasses measures that capture impulsive responding to emotion as well as general neuroticism; Extraversion, which encompasses extraversion, sensation seeking, and reward drive; and Disinhibition/Conscientiousness (D/C), which describes impulsive processes related to planning and persistence, which largely do not include emotion and affect. The D/C trait can be further broken down, with disinhibition and conscientiousness differentiating from one another: disinhibition reflects primarily planfulness, whereas conscientiousness reflects persistence (King et al., 2014; Sharma et al., 2014). See Table 1 for a summary of representative measures of each trait.

Expectancies

1.2.2 |

Expectancies for substance use or other behaviors are cognitive representations of the expected consequences of a behavior (Bolles, 1972; Goldman et al., 1999, 2006). These expectancies may be represented by explicit beliefs or implicit links, though research on the AP model has focused on explicit expectancies. Both positive expectancies (e.g., “drinking relieves boredom”) and negative expectancies (e.g., “people do stupid things when they drink alcohol”) have been linked to drinking behavior in experimental and cross-sectional studies (Hull & Bond, 1986; Jones et al., 2001; Reich et al., 2010). Positive expectancies refer to those beliefs that a behavior is associated with a desired outcome, whereas negative expectancies refer to beliefs that a behavior is associated with an undesired outcome. Within positive expectancies, researchers often distinguish between enhancement expectancies—the belief that a substance or behavior will help to upregulate positive affect—and coping expectancies—the belief that a substance or behavior will help to downregulate negative affect.

Notably, motives for using substances (Cooper, 1994) are a closely-related concept to expectancies which relate to explicit intentions for using a substance (i.e., I drinking in order to increase positive affect) rather than beliefs about the substance (i.e., when I drink, my positive affect increases). Expectancies and motives share a great deal of construct overlap, where motives are thought to be more proximal to the decision to engage in a behavior, while expectancies provide a broader context of beliefs about the behavior. However, the AP literature has overwhelmingly focused on expectancies rather than motives, and expectancies and motives are thought to function in the same manner with respect to AP; thus we considered motives together with expectancies in our review.

Another group of studies in the AP literature examines beliefs about refusal self-efficacy—defined as an individual’s hypotheses about whether or not he or she will be able to resist or refuse using a substance at will—as they relate to personality and risky behaviors (Bandura & Walters, 1977; Oei & Baldwin, 1994). In contrast to expectancies about outcomes of a behavior (such as drinking alcohol), these refusal self-efficacy expectancies can be thought of as a type of expectancy about the likelihood of enacting a behavior in a given situation. Research in this vein has found that college students’ self-perceived ability to refuse alcohol correlates with less drinking and fewer problems (Baldwin et al., 1993; Oei & Morawska, 2004). As such, we also evaluated studies which measured refusal self-efficacy and summarize results for these expectancies separately.

Risky behaviors

1.2.3 |

When considering substance use disorders, it is important to distinguish between measures of frequency/intensity of risky behaviors and measures of problems related to those behaviors. For example, individuals who drink alcohol at high levels may not experience many problems related to their use; conversely, individuals who have many problems may not drink a high volume of alcohol (Somers & Marlatt, 1992). Thus, it is important to distinguish between levels of a risky behavior and problems due to the behavior.

Developmental considerations

1.3 |

The AP model is a developmental theory meant to explain how personality risk factors contribute to substance use problems over the course of development. This model lends itself well to mediational analysis, which seeks to understand whether an intermediate variable (expectancies) helps to explain the relation between a preceding variable (personality) and a resultant variable (risky behavior). Though analyses of cross-sectional data might yield results consistent with a mediational process, longitudinal data represent much stronger evidence for a developmental process. In particular, assessing individuals repeatedly at three different time points allows for temporal separation between each construct (MacKinnon et al., 2002) and studies with fewer than three measurement occasions provide much weaker evidence than those that do. Consequently, we distinguish cross-sectional studies from longitudinal studies longitudinal, and lend particular weight to studies that assessed individuals at three time points.

A second developmental consideration germane to the AP model is the question of at what point in development the AP process operates. Is the effect of personality on expectancies most pronounced during childhood? Or might expectancies influence outcomes most strongly during the adolescent years when individuals experiment with drugs and alcohol (Kann et al., 2018)? Although these answers might critically inform policy and intervention around risky use, theory on the critical developmental phase at which to understand expectancies is still underdeveloped. Thus, we aimed to summarize results by developmental period to begin to develop more precise theory on when in development the AP model might operate.

Methodological considerations

1.4 |

Though statistical mediation analyses are well-suited to the AP model, these methods present methodological challenges to creating quantitative summaries of results. Whereas established methods exist for summarizing a single effect size across many studies, mediational methods inherently involve at least three constructs (personality, expectancies, and risky behavior) and two estimated paths (personality predicting expectancies, expectancies predicting risky behavior). As a complicating factor, each mediational model must control for an idiosyncratic set of covariates, which can make comparisons of individual regression coefficients challenging. Though some novel methods have been developed to summarize across structural equation models (Cheung, 2019; Cheung & Chan, 2009), these methods require pooling covariance matrices, which is quite challenging with models tested across such different samples, constructs, and developmental periods. At the same time, it is also known that systematic reviews which rely on a “vote counting” approach, where results are counted for or against a hypothesis depending on their significance, conflates sample size, effect, size, and publication bias. Thus, we conducted a systematic review rather than a meta-analysis, while also describing quantitative relations using median-based descriptive statistics rather than mean-based statistics. We believe this provides a somewhat more robust starting point for an estimate of effect size that is nonetheless less precisely estimated than a traditional meta-analysis.

The current study

1.5 |

In the nearly 20 years since the AP model of risk for alcohol use disorders was first formulated in 2001, direct tests of this proposed mediational model have propagated through the literature, and the psychological constructs involved have become the target of prevention, treatment, and relapse prevention efforts (Conrod et al., 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2010; Scott-Sheldon et al., 2012; Somers & Marlatt, 1992). Regarding specificity, little is known about which personality traits and expectancies might show evidence of support for the AP model and which might be less relevant to this developmental process. Moreover, though the AP model was first formulated with respect to drinking, it has come to be applied to a range of other risky behaviors including smoking, cannabis use, gambling, and disordered eating. As such, a systematic review of the current literature is timely, warranted, and necessary to (a) evaluate the viability of the AP model, (b) characterize specific personality and learning pathways to risky behaviors and related problems, and (c) gauge the generalizability of the AP model to outcomes other than alcohol use.

METHOD

2 |

Literature review

2.1 |

Literature searches were first conducted in August 2019 and are reported in accordance with PRISMA guidelines (Moher et al., 2009).1 Searches were updated in June 2020. References were obtained via online Boolean search in four databases: PsycINFO, Google Scholar, EBSCO, and Web of Science. Figure 2 summarizes the study selection process, and the paragraphs below describe the process in greater detail.

First, we screened the abstracts of all papers which matched a global search for “Acquired Preparedness.” Second, we downloaded PDF files for those papers which at first glance seemed to test the AP model using mediation analysis. Third, we conducted a full-text screening of each downloaded article and included those that reported a novel test of mediation involving both an impulsive trait measure and an expectancy measure. Inclusion criteria were: (1) explicit mention of the AP model, (2) empirical data from a mediation analysis were presented, and (3) personality, expectancies, and an outcome were each examined.

Searches proceeded sequentially, starting with a PsycINFO search. A PsycINFO search for “Acquired Preparedness” in any search field yielded 47 papers, including 10 published dissertations. Supplementing the exhaustive PsycINFO search, we also conducted a Google Scholar search for “Acquired Preparedness” and found an additional 16 papers by screening the first 200 search results. The Google Scholar search was deemed to be saturated at 200 papers, as the final three pages (20 search results per page) yielded no search results that met inclusion criteria. We then supplemented these 63 papers with a search of the EBSCO database for the term “Acquired Preparedness,” finding an additional 9 papers from a pool of 70. Finally, we searched Web of Science for “Acquired Preparedness” and found no additional papers that fit our inclusion criteria.

Exclusion criteria were then applied to the sample of sixty-nine papers. Four papers were counted twice due to multiple reported studies within a single manuscript (for a total of 73 studies). The final sample of papers included 50 papers. As six studies reported analyses from one preadolescent sample, two studies reported analyses from one high school sample, and three studies reported analyses from one undergraduate sample, the final number of study samples was k = 42. Exclusion criteria for the sample of 71 papers included: (1) lack of an impulsive trait measure; (2) no report of mediation results or reporting results in a way that could not be summarized, (e.g., moderated mediation, serial mediation); (3) no novel empirical data presented; and (4) analyses were reported which were published elsewhere in greater detail.

Coding and measures

2.2 |

Impulsive traits

2.2.1 |

Following the framework of impulsive traits proposed by Sharma et al. (2014), we coded each impulsive trait measure as one of the four factors in Table 1: neuroticism, extraversion, disinhibition, or conscientiousness. Example impulsive trait measures included the UPPS (Whiteside & Lynam, 2001), the Zuckerman-Kuhlman Personality Questionnaire (Zuckerman et al., 1993), and the I7 (Eysenck & Eysenck, 1978). For measures that were included in the Sharma et al. (2014) factor analysis, we coded each subscale according to its highest factor loading. For measures that were not included in the Sharma et al. (2014) study, we analyzed item content and assigned the impulsive trait that matched the most similar subscale from the published factor analysis. Several measures could not be coded as one of the four traits because they combined subscales that loaded most highly onto different traits. These measures were coded as mixed measures and analyzed as a separate category. Measures of positive urgency were not present in the factor analysis described above, and these measures were coded as neuroticism measures based on empirical evidence that positive and negative urgency correlate as highly as .69 to .80 (Pedersen et al., 2016; Roley et al., 2017; Settles et al., 2014).

Expectancies

2.2.2 |

Following the evidence described above, we coded each expectancy measure as a positive expectancy, a negative expectancy, or a refusal self-efficacy expectancy. Within positive expectancies, we further coded measures of enhancement expectancies and coping expectancies to test theories about specificity of affect-based impulsive traits. Although coping expectancies relate to negative emotions, we considered them to be positive expectancies because negative affect reduction tends to be seen as a positive outcome from the perspective of the individual. Example expectancy measures were the Alcohol Expectancies Questionnaire (AEQ; Brown et al., 1987), and the Comprehensive Effects of Alcohol (CEOA; Fromme et al., 1993).

Outcomes

2.2.3 |

Each measure of risky behavior was coded as capturing either use, problems, or both. Example measures of levels of risky behaviors were the revised daily drinking questionnaire (DDQ-R; Collins et al., 1985) and cannabis items from the Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System (YRBSS; Brener et al., 1995), while example measures of problems due to risky behaviors were the Marijuana Problems Scale (MPS; Stephens et al., 2000) and the Rutgers Alcohol Problems Index (White & Labouvie, 1989).

Analysis plan

2.3 |

To synthesize results across studies, we examined both results of null hypothesis significance tests and, when possible, effect sizes. If effect sizes (standardized regression coefficients) were reported in an article, we reported them directly; if standardized model coefficients were not reported, we used standard deviation-based formulae to calculate them when possible. As most studies reported multiple significance tests that could be considered tests of AP, we report results of significance tests at both the study level and the level of statistical tests. We considered a study to support AP for a given personality trait if at least one test involving that personality trait supported AP. However, it was common for studies to report multiple tests of the same AP pathway. For instance, a study might find support for one measure of extraversion predicting alcohol use through positive expectancies, but also report a null result for a second measure of extraversion. In this case, we considered the study to support AP for that pathway, but counted the tests as one supporting AP and another failing to support AP. We included both study-level summaries and test-level summaries, as each provided independent information. There were a broad range of statistical tests in each study which could be considered to test the AP model. We chose the broadest definition possible, considering each relation between personality traits, expectancies, and risky behaviors to be a test of the AP model. This choice may have led us to include null findings that are arguably consistent with theory; for example, extraversion predicting negative expectancies for a substance. However, in the interest of summarizing broadly, we included these tests in our pool.

One challenge in summarizing results across mediation studies lies in the variety of mediation tests in the literature. MacKinnon et al. (2002) identified as many as 14 different common methods for testing mediation. Given the heterogeneity in methods, focal measures, and covariates across studies, we reported results of published tests as reported and noted differences based on method. When possible, we extracted standardized regression estimates from multivariate tests. We chose this multivariate approach, rather than analyzing raw correlations, because these estimates control for potential confounds and likely more closely represent true relations. Due to the lack of established meta-analytic methods for analyzing mediation, the variety of tests of mediation, and the variety of covariates included in analyses across different studies, we opted not to subject these effect sizes to meta-analysis. To summarize tests, we present descriptive summaries and plot distributions of effects. When indirect effects were not reported, we calculated the point estimates of these effects as the product of the constituent a (personality to expectancy) and b (expectancy to outcome) paths. When considering direction, all effects were recoded such that higher values represented a relation between more impulsivity and more positive (less negative) expectancies.

RESULTS

3 |

Summary of tests

3.1 |

Table 2 lists sample demographic information for each study. Table 3 examines the number of studies that examined each risk-behavior outcome, whether the studies were longitudinal, and the number of statistical tests for each outcome. We reviewed the results of 50 studies from 43 distinct study samples, which comprised 321 statistical tests of the AP model. These 50 studies included 21,715 unique participants. The median study included 41% male participants (59% female) and 30% non-White participants.

Support for AP

3.1.1 |

Overall, 48 of 50 studies (96%) reported at least one result supporting the full mediational path proposed by the AP model. Of the 321 statistical tests reported, 143 (45%) supported AP. Effect sizes were reported across 36 of the 50 studies for a total of 131 statistical tests. Of these 131 reported effect sizes, 91 were associated with statistical tests which showed support for AP (81%). The median reported indirect effect of personality on outcomes via expectancies was .05 (SD = .07, min = .00, max = .37). The median reported standardized effect size for personality predicting expectancies was .22 (SD = .12, min = .02, max = .67), and the median reported effect size for expectancies predicting outcomes was .24 (SD = .17, min = .03, max = .86). Only 6 of the 131 mediated effect sizes were in the direction opposite expected. Figure 3 displays standardized effect sizes across all studies.

Multiple tests of AP per study

3.1.2 |

Across 50 papers, 321 tests were reported (M = 6.4, SD = 7.5, median = 3.0). Eight papers (16%) reported only a single statistical test, while 21 papers (42%) only reported statistically significant AP findings (i.e., did not report any null results). Seventeen papers (34%) reported AP tests for more than one broad impulsive trait; 25 papers (50%) reported AP tests for more than one broad type of expectancy; and 13 papers (26%) reported AP tests for risky behavior frequency and problems separately.

Study methodology

3.1.3 |

Thirteen studies, comprising 73 effects, tested the AP using data that were longitudinal in nature. Only nine studies, comprising 49 effects, tested AP using data with temporal separation between each measure (e.g., expectancies measured later than impulsive traits, outcomes measured later than expectancies). An additional 28 studies, comprising 248 effects, tested mediational models in cross-sectional data. Whereas longitudinal tests tended to report a small but significant effect, cross-sectional studies displayed a wider range of estimates.

Eleven of the 13 longitudinal studies found some support for the AP model. The two papers which did not report any results supporting AP in the overall sample of 50 were longitudinal studies. However, each of the 11 studies which reported at least one statistically significant finding found support for positive expectancies mediating the relation between personality and risky behavior. The 13 longitudinal studies reported an average of 5.6 statistical tests per study, and 24.7% of these tests found support for AP. Figure 4 displays effect sizes by longitudinal study design.

Research on AP by impulsive trait

3.2 |

The pattern of support for AP varied by the impulsive trait tested, such that impulsive traits that included affect (extraversion and neuroticism) were most consistently linked to outcomes. Measures of disinhibition showed a similar rate of support for AP across fewer tests. However, measures of conscientiousness were less likely to show support for AP (see Table 4). The majority of tests involving measures of extraversion, neuroticism, and mixed impulsivity measures reported some support for the AP model along the corresponding pathway (41%, 55%, and 50%, respectively). In contrast, only 30% of studies testing D/C as an AP predictor found support for the corresponding pathway.

Research on AP by type of expectancy

3.3 |

Overall, the body of literature supporting AP for positive expectancies was larger and more consistent than that for negative expectancies. All but one AP paper contained a test involving a positive expectancy, and all but 3 (94%) found some evidence for a role of positive expectancies. In contrast, only 24 papers tested a role of negative expectancies, and only 54% found evidence of their role in AP. A small but consistent literature (5 papers) supported a role of perceived control (or lack thereof) over a behavior/substance mediating the relation between impulsive traits and outcomes. The individual patterns of findings for enhancement and coping expectancies was largely in line with the pattern of findings for positive expectancies as whole.

Including null results in the pool of AP tests resulted in a tempered but consistent picture of the role of expectancies in the development of risky behaviors. Impulsive traits were linked to risky behaviors through positive expectancies in 47% of cases, whereas impulsive traits related through negative expectancies in only 27% of tests. A much smaller literature on perceived control over risky behaviors showed consistent support for perceived control as a mediator (72%). Table 4 summarizes findings from measures by expectancy.

Research on AP by outcome

3.4 |

Risky behaviors were either measured as levels of the behavior (e.g., quantity or frequency of drinking) of behavior or as problems due to the behavior (e.g., missing work or school due to drinking). No notable pattern was observed across type of outcome: 39% of statistical tests for quantity/frequency of behavior supported AP and 46% of statistical tests for problems due to behavior supported AP. Though null results were more common in alcohol studies by percentage of tests (see Table 5), studies of alcohol also tended to report more statistical tests in each study (average of 7.7 per alcohol study compared to 4.1). Table 4 summarizes tests of AP by outcome.

Alcohol

3.5 |

Alcohol was the most-studied risky behavior in tests of the AP model; thirty-two empirical papers have been written on the AP model, with 247 statistical tests reported. Across all measures of alcohol, 97 of these tests (39%) found evidence of a mediated effect of personality through expectancies on alcohol use and/or problems. Statistically significant AP effects were found for alcohol use (quantity and/or frequency) more frequently and more consistently than for alcohol-related problems. The AP model was evaluated with respect to the quantity/frequency of alcohol use in 165 statistical tests. Overall, 67 of these tests (41%) found support for the AP model. The AP model was applied to consequences of drinking in 80 statistical tests. Thirty-three percent of these tests found support for the AP model. Table 5 summarizes tests of AP for alcohol.

Five studies (across three different participant samples) have investigated the AP model in the onset of drinking during the preadolescent period (Anderson et al., 2005; Gunn & Smith, 2010; Lopez-Vergara et al., 2012; Peterson et al., 2018; Settles et al., 2014), with four finding support for a mediated effect of impulsive traits on likelihood of early drinking initiation via alcohol-related expectancies. Taken together, the existing research on alcohol use in preadolescents suggests that preadolescent children who are high in emotion-based impulsive traits are more likely to learn the reinforcing effects of alcohol (especially in social domains), and in turn experience increased risk for early drinking initiation.

Three studies tested and found support for AP for drinking in high school/adolescent samples, again finding support for an extraversion pathway via positive expectancies. Two studies found support for extraversion predicting drinking frequency via positive (but not negative) expectancies. Research on youth and adolescents has tended to focus on levels of drinking (initiation, frequency) rather than problems; only one study included a measure of problems that was combined with a measure of drinking frequency.

The bulk of research on AP in alcohol use (and AP at large) has been conducted with college undergraduates. Twenty-one studies in total have examined these relations, with each finding some evidence of a mediated link between an impulsive trait and alcohol use or problems via alcohol expectancies. This literature consists of 15 cross-sectional studies (Anderson et al., 2003; Banks & Zapolski, 2017; Coskunpinar & Cyders, 2012; Heath et al., 2019; Kazemi et al., 2014; Marks, 2007; McCarthy, Kroll, & Smith, 2001; McCarthy, Miller, et al., 2001; Mezquita et al., 2015; Morris, 2016; Peterson et al., 2018; Staszkow, 2013; Treloar et al., 2012; Vangsness et al., 2005; Wardell et al., 2015) and six longitudinal studies (Corbin et al., 2011; Fu et al., 2007; Han, 2010; Park et al., 2014; Settles et al., 2010; Wardell et al., 2012). All six of the longitudinal studies found a mediated effect of extraversion or neuroticism on drinking levels through positive expectancies. Three of these studies had temporal separation between personality, expectancies, and alcohol use/problems, whereas three additional longitudinal studies tested mediation, but did not have full temporal separation between personality, expectancy, and outcome. Across all studies of AP for alcohol in college-age youth (including both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies), neuroticism was most consistently linked to alcohol outcomes via alcohol expectancies (29 of 69 tests across 13 papers, 42% of tests supported AP). Positive expectancies were most often tested in this age group and were linked to an AP process for alcohol in 38% of tests (50 of 132 tests across 19 studies).

Three published articles have examined AP in adult samples, with six tests finding support for AP. The pattern of results was varied, and the studies found evidence for neuroticism, extraversion, and disinhibition measures predicting both alcohol use and problems via either positive expectancies or perceived impaired control (Corbin et al., 2015; Kabbani & Kambouropoulos, 2013; Spillane & Smith, 2010). Notably, one of these investigated AP in a group of 211 tribal members from a First Nation community (Spillane & Smith, 2010), finding that positive alcohol expectancies mediated the effect of negative urgency on drinking problems. An identical model fit well in a parallel sample of 236 White individuals. More studies are needed to understand the impact of neuroticism in an AP process in adulthood, especially given high rates of comorbidity between internalizing problems and alcohol use disorders in adulthood. However, positive expectancies appeared to be the primary pathway through which impulsive traits impacted adult drinking, though other alcohol-related beliefs such as the perceived ability to control one’s drinking may play a role as well.

Cannabis

3.6 |

The AP model of cannabis use was tested in 22 separate tests across six studies, with 14 tests finding support for AP (64%). In tests of cannabis use (rather than problems) 7 of 11 tests (64%) showed support for an AP process. Three of the five studies finding support for the AP model were conducted in adults, and the other two sampled undergraduates (see Table 5).

A single study of AP found a lack of support for AP in a longitudinal sample of 277 teens across 3 years of high school (Ninnemann, 2016). The study used an aggregate measure of disinhibition which included both extraversion and conscientiousness items, and did not find support for a mediated effect of disinhibition on the frequency of cannabis use through either positive or negative expectancies.

Two cross-sectional studies investigated AP for college cannabis use. One study of 337 college undergraduates (Vangsness et al., 2005) reported a relation between disinhibition and cannabis use which was mediated by negative expectancies. Individuals who were higher in disinhibition had fewer negative expectancies and used cannabis more. The second study tested the AP model for cannabis use in 252 undergraduates (Papinczak et al., 2018), finding an indirect effect of extraversion and cannabis use via both positive outcome expectancies and cannabis refusal self-efficacy.

Three studies tested the AP model of adult cannabis use, each finding a slightly different pattern of support for AP (Curry et al., 2018; Hayaki et al., 2011; Luba et al., 2018). The findings included a variety of evidence for extraversion and neuroticism predicting cannabis use and dependence through positive, negative, and refusal self-efficacy expectancies.

Smoking

3.7 |

The development of smoking via the AP model was examined in 4 studies reporting 9 mediated effects. In each reported test of the AP model, a mediated effect of personality on smoking was observed (see Table 5).

A single study of 309 5th-grade students from Smith and colleagues’ teen sample found support for the AP model of smoking initiation (Combs et al., 2012). In particular, the authors found that neuroticism predicted smoking initiation at age 11 through positive smoking expectancies.

Two studies have examined the AP model of smoking in college undergraduates, each finding evidence of both extraversion and neuroticism on smoking. The first study (Doran et al., 2013) followed 400 college freshmen across their first year, finding several pathways through which impulsive traits influenced smoking initiation. The authors reported four significant findings: (1) positive expectancies mediated the effect of extraversion on smoking initiation, (2) positive expectancies mediated the effect of neuroticism on smoking initiation, (3) negative expectancies mediated the effect of extraversion on smoking initiation, and (4) negative expectancies mediated the effect of neuroticism on smoking initiation. A second study examined affect-specific expectancies for smoking in a sample of 131 undergraduates (Spillane et al., 2013), focusing on nicotine dependence rather than smoking initiation. The authors found evidence for two affect-specific pathways: negative affect reduction expectancies mediated the relation between negative urgency and nicotine dependence, whereas positive affect expectancies mediated the relation between positive urgency and nicotine dependence.

One cross-sectional study (n = 207; Pang et al., 2014) tested the role of AP in adult nicotine dependence, finding evidence for two AP pathways. Neuroticism predicted nicotine dependence through both positive and negative expectancies.

Gambling

3.8 |

Two studies reported on mediated effects of personality on gambling via gambling expectancies, reporting 30 effects in total. Most tests of the AP model involving gambling frequency found support for AP (60%), whereas a minority of tests involving gambling problems/consequences found support (20%; see Table 5).

One study (Canale et al., 2015) examined the AP model of the development of problem gambling in a mixed sample of high school students (n = 385) and college undergraduates (n = 209; total n = 594). In this cross-sectional sample of individuals who had gambled at least once in the past year, several pathways showed support for AP. Enhancement motives mediated the relation between sensation seeking and problem gambling, as well as the relation between positive urgency and problem gambling. Coping motives also mediated the relation between positive urgency and problem gambling.

In addition to the study described above, one additional study examined the AP model with respect to gambling in a college student population. In a sample of 355 students at two universities and one college (Ginley et al., 2015), the authors found broad support for AP in predicting gambling frequency, and more narrow support for AP in predicting gambling symptomatology. Sensation seeking, reward responsiveness, and behavioral inhibition each predicted gambling frequency via affect expectancies, self-evaluation expectancies, and social consequence expectancies. In predicting gambling symptomatology, however, only self-evaluation expectancies mediated the relations between the three impulsive trait measures and gambling symptomatology.

Disordered eating

3.9 |

Across 5 studies, 10 tests of the AP model of disordered eating were reported. Eighty-two percent of reported AP tests were statistically significant, and all but one significant finding reflected an association between neuroticism and levels of disordered eating (2 tests) or problems due to disordered eating (3 tests) via positive expectancies for eating. Table 5 summarizes tests of AP for disordered eating.

Two studies examined the AP process for the development of eating disorders in preadolescent youth. One study, for which analyses are reported in two manuscripts (Pearson et al., 2012, 2015), examined the role of neuroticism in predicting eating expectancies and eating disorder symptoms in a longitudinal sample of 1906 5th and 6th graders. These students were assessed at the end of their 5th grade year, at the beginning of their 6th grade year, and at the end of their 6th grade year. Evidence was found for an effect of neuroticism on binge eating via negative affect eating expectancies, even when controlling for raw negative affect and depressive symptoms. The second study (Davis & Smith, 2018) followed the same sample across a fourth wave of data collection when the students were in the 10th grade, finding additional support for the same negative urgency pathway through negative affect eating expectancies.

Three studies examined the AP model of negative urgency and disordered eating in undergraduates. Two of the studies found evidence of a negative urgency path through eating expectancies on disordered eating, and one of the studies found evidence of an extraversion path.

Depression

3.10 |

A single dissertation (Anderson, 2002) investigated the AP model in the context of depressive symptoms in a sample of 217 high school students. Using a measure of general positive and negative outcome expectancies, the author found support for the AP model: general positive outcome expectancies and negative outcome expectancies each mediated the effect of a broad impulsive index on depression.

DISCUSSION

4 |

Across nearly twenty years of research on the AP model, studies tended to show a small mediated effect of impulsive traits on risky behaviors and their consequences via expectancies for risky behaviors. Though this summary should be understood with caution due to the methodological challenges related to summarizing mediation results, we observed a median effect size of approximately .05 for the mediated effect of personality on risky behaviors via expectancies. Across all risky behaviors studied, the most consistent evidence was reported for extraversion, neuroticism, and disinhibition conveying risk for both levels of risky behavior and consequences of risky behavior through positive expectancies. In contrast, the evidence for conscientiousness and negative expectancies was more mixed.

For alcohol in particular, extraversion and positive expectancies appear to be important during youth and adolescence, whereas a neuroticism pathway begins to predict use and problems through expectancies during the college years. The alcohol literature, however, raises important methodological questions about publication bias and reporting. Alcohol studies reported the greatest number of tests per study (n = 7.7), and tested the broadest range of candidate constructs. Likely due to this high number of tests per study, only 39% of statistical tests supported the AP hypothesis for alcohol use despite nearly every study finding some support for AP. We believe this transparency in reporting null results is important in advancing the field; yet we also acknowledge that any given study is more likely to find support for the AP model by testing many candidate constructs. Notably, the types of constructs tested were somewhat conflated with developmental period; research on youth and adolescents tended to study positive expectancies, whereas research on college-age and adult populations tested a broader range of constructs. Each of the impulsive traits included in this review showed some support for AP. Overall, we observed support for the AP model across several developmental periods and across different operationalizations of impulsive traits and expectancies.

Though the bulk of research on AP has been conducted on alcohol and alcohol problems, studies of other risky behaviors have generally shown support for the AP process as well. Alcohol studies were by far the best-represented in our literature review (32 of 50 studies), whereas cannabis, tobacco, gambling, disordered eating, and depression were studied in 6 or fewer studies each. Though the disordered eating literature had only 5 studies, a consistent picture of neuroticism impacting disordered eating behavior via expectancies for eating emerged. For other outcomes, the patterns of findings were more difficult to summarize given heterogeneity in measures and findings, and the low number of studies. Though the general AP process (that personality influences risky behaviors via learned expectancies) appears to receive support across a range of outcomes, more research is still needed to discern whether the same personality traits and expectancies are operant for different risky behaviors.

Impulsive traits that involved affect (neuroticism and extraversion) showed greater support for AP than impulsive traits reflecting cognitive control or “pure” inhibition. Indeed, affect appears to be central to both impulsive traits and expectancies. Regarding impulsive traits, this empirical review lends support to the focus on urgency traits recently endorsed by Davis et al. (2018). This finding mirrors the emergence of urgency as the strongest and broadest predictor of psychopathology among the impulsive traits (Berg et al., 2015; King et al., 2018; Stautz & Cooper, 2013). Furthermore, alcohol expectancies have been described explicitly as expectancies of affective response (Goldman et al., 1999), and research on motives for drinking (a construct related closely to expectancies) places desired affective states in a central position (Kuntsche et al., 2005).

Across a broad range of impulsive traits and outcomes, positive expectancies mediated the effects of impulsive traits on outcomes relatively robustly and consistently. In spite of substantial heterogeneity in measures, nearly half of reported tests of AP involving a measure of positive expectancies showed support for AP. Some support was observed for both coping expectancies and enhancement expectancies, again indicating that affective processes may be central to risky behaviors. In spite of a number of studies supporting the importance of negative expectancies for risky behavior (i.e., consequences; for review see Monk & Heim, 2013), it does not appear that the AP process applies to the learning of these negative expectancies as consistently as it does for the learning of positive or reinforcing expectancies. Though clinical levels of substance use disorders are often characterized by negative affect regulation motives, many instances of substance use are more focused on positive affect enhancement than on negative affect reduction.

Longitudinal studies provide the most direct evidence for AP; however, only 13 of the 50 studies reviewed were longitudinal in nature. These 13 studies examined nine unique datasets, comprising 73 statistical tests. Only 24.7% of these statistical tests provided support for AP. This lower number is likely influenced by reduced power in predicting forward in time; but might also suggest that the AP process might unfold across briefer or longer developmental periods than the ones studied. Seven of the 13 longitudinal studies predicted across time frames of one year or less. Given the developmental predictions of the AP model, a good deal of additional research across different developmental periods and with longitudinal designs is still needed to discern how the AP process plays out across development from preadolescence, through adolescence and young adulthood, and into adulthood. Only 9 studies were fully longitudinal (i.e., had temporal separation between each of the three primary constructs). Of the 13 longitudinal studies, 11 found some support for the AP model; notably, the only two studies in the sample of 50 to report only null findings were longitudinal in nature. This may suggest that studies with strong methodology may be more likely to be published, even in the face of null results. Also of note, 9 of the 13 longitudinal studies were alcohol studies. Across the 11 studies which did find support for AP, each of them found support for positive expectancies mediating the relation between personality and risky behavior. Only one found support for negative expectancies mediating this relation. Finally, longitudinal studies tended to report smaller effects than cross-sectional studies, but less variability was noted in these effects across studies. Taken together, these findings suggest that fully longitudinal studies appear to lend some support to the AP model, particularly in terms of the role of positive expectancies predicting later risky behaviors; but this support remains mixed.

Limitations

4.1 |

Limitations of the AP literature qualify the conclusions that can be drawn from this review. An important limitation of the AP literature is that the sheer number and heterogeneity of measures used in studies of AP makes meta-analytic summaries of any relation involving “impulsivity” difficult. Given substantial progress in understanding the structure of self-reported impulsive traits, adopting either the widespread UPPS framework (Whiteside & Lynam, 2001) or the broader frameworks proposed by Sharma and colleagues (King et al., 2014; Sharma et al., 2013, 2014) would aid the effective synthesis of results and development of theory. We chose to utilize the Sharma et al. (2014) framework in order to capture positive and negative affect-related traits which likely relate to risky behaviors, but do not map closely onto the UPPS urgency constructs. Whereas the Sharma et al. framework captures traits that concern broader positive and negative affect (extraversion, neuroticism), the UPPS framework does not accommodate these traits quite as directly. We acknowledge that both frameworks have major strengths (careful derivation of scales, empirical validation, data on hierarchical structure), as well as limitations, and researchers should be thoughtful in interpreting findings regardless of which framework they use. In addition, our choice of framework may influence patterns of results, as each framework collapses across certain traits and differentiates between others. A second limitation of the AP literature is related to the reporting of null results. In published path diagrams, non-significant paths are often omitted by convention. If these estimates were available, meta-analytic SEM techniques (Cheung, 2013) might begin to supplant other summative methods.

In addition to limitations of the literature, our approach came with both strengths and limitations. The current review was also limited by an explicit focus on self-report data, and on tests of mediation. Our review did not include studies which involved experimental manipulations, those that used behavioral measures, and those that used alternate interpretations of the AP hypothesis (i.e., moderation vs. mediation). Though these choices limited the scope of our review, they also made it possible to summarize across a relatively homogeneous literature in terms of methodology. Given our focus on explicit tests of the complex model at hand, it is also possible that our literature search did not capture mediational tests that did not mention the AP model explicitly. Though it is possible that additional literature might have been relevant to our review, we judged the complex and specific nature of the AP model was such that it would be unlikely to test the particular hypothesis without mentioning the AP model by name. Another limitation was our choice, due to staffing and time constraints, not to ask authors for datasets or correlation matrices; future work might do so to obtain even more precise estimates of mediated effects. Such work will surely require methodological advances and substantial expertise, which holds promise to advance work in summarizing mediation analyses.

The AP literature is surely not immune from the publication bias that impacts psychology at large (Ferguson & Heene, 2012; Kühberger et al., 2014). As a result, the general finding that all studies found some support for AP should not necessarily be taken as evidence that the AP is empirically supported. We applaud researchers for presenting null results along with statistically significant findings as they enable more comprehensive integration of evidence even in the face of publication bias. Indeed, this may be one advantage of the AP model. Because it hypothesizes associations with multiple impulsive traits and multiple dimensions of expectancies, it may be easier to publish null findings for some associations when others are confirmed. On the other hand, that same flexibility may lead to opportunities for selective reporting, especially as different substance use outcomes (such as alcohol quantity, frequency, binge drinking, or alcohol use disorder symptoms) are frequently measured in the same sample. Future research should take advantage of open science practices such as pre-registration and registered reports in order to increase transparency and improve the fields’ ability to publish null results.

Future directions

4.2 |

The AP model implicitly and explicitly describes a transactional learning process that unfolds over many years, yet many tests of AP (and mediation more broadly) use cross-sectional data. Prioritizing longitudinal research, experimental study designs, natural experiments, and intensive longitudinal designs holds great promise for advancing AP research. In the alcohol literature, which has accumulated a good deal of evidence consistent with the AP model, a shift away from cross-sectional designs toward longitudinal designs may help to advance the field. A recent narrative review by Peterson and Smith (2018) highlights the importance of experimental designs, quasi-experimental designs, and intervention work in understanding the implications of the AP process. Experimental designs (when feasible) and the use of natural experiments will be necessary to provide stronger causal support for the AP model. Moreover, intensive repeated measurement over a shorter time frame might allow for closer examination of transactional processes as they occur. In a recent ecological momentary assessment study, for example, Stamates (2019) assessed heavy drinking college students 3 times a day over 14 days, finding that the relation between daily urgency and drinking that evening was mediated by both positive and negative expectancies. Though these designs are more resource-intensive they are essential for validating the predictions of the AP model.

Studies which test several candidate pathways for AP simultaneously are especially informative, though they may be more methodologically complex. Thus far, most of the AP studies which included multiple impulsive traits and multiple expectancies were from the alcohol literature. Presenting both null and statistically significant findings will help to clarify the mechanisms at play. For example, urgency appears to play a role in the development of eating expectancies and later eating disorders, but the majority of these studies did not report the analysis of any other impulsive traits. Reporting a fuller set of analyses will help to separate out the predictive power of shared variance among impulsive traits from unique variance.

CONCLUSIONS

5 |

The AP model represents an important advance in applying person-environment transaction theory to the study of risky behaviors, and the empirical literature thus far on AP supports a mediated effect of affect-laden impulsive traits on risky behaviors, primarily via positive expectancies. This mediated process highlights the importance of both personality and learning in the development of substance use disorders and other risky behaviors. To supplement the important work thus far, future research should clarify the mechanisms at work during critical developmental periods when AP processes are taking hold. To further understanding of processes of AP as they unfold, new study designs including (a) experimental designs and natural experiments, (b) ecological momentary assessment, and (c) multiple-timepoint research that allows investigation of transactional effects will help to paint a more vivid picture of the development of risky behaviors, and provide targets of intervention.
