Medical diagnosis at the point-of-care by portable high-field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Non-invasive medical diagnosis by analysing volatile organic compounds (VOCs) at the point-of-care is becoming feasible owing to recent advances in portable instrumentation. A number of studies have assessed the performance of a state-of-the-art VOC analyser (micro-chip high-field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry, FAIMS) for medical diagnosis. However, a comprehensive meta-analysis is needed to investigate the overall diagnostic performance of these novel methods across different medical conditions. An electronic search was performed using the CAplus and MEDLINE database through the SciFinder platform. The review identified a total of 23 studies and 2312 individuals. Eighteen studies were used for meta-analysis. A pooled analysis found an overall sensitivity of 80% (95% CI, 74%–85%, I2 = 62%), and specificity of 78% (95% CI, 70%–84%, I2 = 80%), which corresponds to the overall diagnostic performance of micro-chip FAIMS across many different medical conditions. The diagnostic accuracy was particularly high for coeliac and inflammatory bowel disease (sensitivity and specificity from 74% to 97%). The overall diagnostic performance was similar across breath, urine, and faecal matrices with sparse logistic regression and random forests algorithms resulting in higher diagnostic accuracy. Sources of variability likely arise from differences in sample storage, sampling protocol, method of data analysis, type of disease, sample matrix, and potentially to clinical and disease factors. The results of this meta-analysis indicate that micro-chip FAIMS is a promising candidate for disease screening at the point-of-care, particularly for gastroenterology diseases. This review provides recommendations that should improve the techniques relevant to diagnostic accuracy of future VOC and point-of-care studies.

Introduction

1.

The need for more rapid and accurate analytical solutions in medical diagnosis is a growing topic of interest in a variety of fields including respiratory medicine and oncology [1, 2]. Typically, a reliable diagnosis involves clinical factors and the use of biomarkers. In recent decades, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are of considerable interest as potential biomarkers are generally the products of metabolic activity and can thus provide useful physiological information [3–6]. VOCs can be measured in a number of bodily fluids and excretions including breath, urine, and faeces [7]. As a result, the detection of VOCs from such matrices can potentially be a non-invasive and cost-effective approach towards a clinical diagnosis, and could also prove useful for disease screening and management [1, 8].

Gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (GC-MS) has been one of the most common methods used to detect VOCs for medical diagnosis [5, 9, 10]. Although GC-MS has the advantage of sensitivity and provides chemical identity information [5], a key limiting factor in its clinical applicability is the lack of corroboration when assigning VOC biomarkers. That is, using GC-MS, not a single VOC for a given disease has been demonstrated to be reproducible across different studies [8]. Owing to the methodologic challenges surrounding VOC analysis, the analysis of VOCs as a ‘fingerprint’ or pattern of a given sample could potentially be more informative.

High-field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry (FAIMS) is an emerging method for VOC analysis [11–13]. In contrast to GC-MS in which VOCs are primarily separated based on chemical volatility in a process that typically takes up to an hour, FAIMS separates ionized VOCs in near real-time based on their behaviour in an electric field and buffer gas [13, 14]. Briefly, under the application of an asymmetric waveform, ion mobility becomes non-linearly dependent on the electric field. That is, ions experience differential ion mobility under high and low electric fields [14]. As such, FAIMS can generate ion heatmap ‘fingerprints’ that show different VOCs that have been separated using different electric fields. These heatmaps can then be analysed using statistical and machine learning algorithms [12]. Unlike other pattern-based technologies such as electronic nose (e-nose) which uses sensor-based arrays, FAIMS is less susceptible to technological limitations including intra-device repeatability [15], chemical suppression and interference [16].

Recently, a commercial and portable micro-chip FAIMS device has become a promising alternative to assist with on-site clinical diagnosis. Using FAIMS principles, and by applying classical machine learning algorithms to analyse VOC ‘fingerprints’, microchip FAIMS has been used to assist in the diagnosis of different diseases and medical conditions. However, to date, the clinical applicability of microchip FAIMS and its potential as a next-generation point-of-care diagnostic tool has not been fully studied.

Here, we report a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of the published literature with the following aims: (a) investigate the reported overall diagnostic accuracy of VOC-based analysis using micro-chip FAIMS across different medical conditions; (b) assess the challenges in experimental and analytical methods; and (c) provide a framework for future VOC-based studies.

Methods

2.

This meta-analysis review was conducted in accordance with the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17, 18] (table S1 (available online at stacks.iop.org/JBR/15/046002/mmedia)).

Eligibility criteria and study selection

2.1.

Two reviewers (JDZ and MJB) screened the titles and abstracts of journal articles listed in the database based on the following eligibility criteria:

  1. studies must describe some aspect of medical diagnosis (e.g. diagnostic accuracy, disease type); and

  2. studies must detail an additional keyword based on FAIMS (e.g. IMS, ion mobility).

Subsequent studies for inclusion were based on the following inclusion criteria that studies must:

  1. use micro-chip FAIMS (also known as Lonestar) as a standalone portable instrument; and

  2. include one measure of diagnostic accuracy. Here, diagnostic accuracy refers to a quantitative measurement of diagnostic performance (e.g. sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, area under curve (AUC)).

Additional articles were identified through handsearching reference lists of included studies.

Information sources

2.2.

An electronic search was performed using the CAplus and MEDLINE database through the SciFinder platform. The search was limited to journal articles published in English from 2010 to 2019. The final search was conducted on 1 September 2020.

Search

2.3.

The following keywords were used to search all relevant journal articles: lonestar, faims. A summary of the search strategy can be found in table S2.

Data collection process and data items

2.4.

For each study that met the inclusion criteria, the following information concerning experimental and diagnostic performance were extracted:

  1. characteristics of study (i.e. demographic details of participant groups, type and severity of disease or infection, and type of analyte matrix);

  2. diagnostic performance (i.e. accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC);

  3. method of data analysis (i.e. type of machine learning algorithm); and

  4. comparative data (if applicable) using alternative instrumentation (e-Nose or GC-MS).

One reviewer (JDZ) extracted the data for assessment by a 2nd reviewer (MJB).

Risk of bias

2.5.

Evaluation of the risk of bias within and between included studies were made using the standards for reporting of diagnostic accuracy studies (STARD) checklist [19], which is a 30-item checklist to assess bias, variability, transparency, and applicability of results reported in diagnostic studies (table S3). The STARD scores were each independently assessed by two reviewers (JDZ and MJB) and a 3rd reviewer (KMMK) settled disagreements.

Outcomes

2.6.

Sensitivity and specificity were the primary measures of diagnostic performance. The meta-analysis was conducted in R version 4.0 using the meta and metafor package [20, 21]. Pooled estimates and associated confidence intervals were calculated based on the random effects meta-analysis model [22]. Logit transformation of proportion was used. Heterogeneity (i.e. how much variation in study outcomes is present between studies) was quantified using the I2 statistic [23]. The funnel plot was employed to examine the presence of asymmetry and identify potential bias. Because conventional funnel plots are inaccurate for meta-analyses of proportion studies [24, 25], the funnel plots of study size against log odds and the weighted linear regression test of asymmetry by Peters et al [25] were used. Subgroup analysis was further performed to compare the diagnostic accuracy between different machine learning algorithms and sample matrices.

Results

3.

Systematic review

3.1.

Our systematic search yielded 572 potentially eligible studies. After removal of duplicates, review of titles and abstracts, and assessment of eligibility, 18 studies were identified. An additional five studies that met the eligibility criteria were identified by handsearching reference lists from these 18 papers. This gave a total of 23 studies which are included in this review [26–48]. Studies that did not provide a measurement of diagnostic accuracy in terms of sensitivity and specificity were excluded (figure S1), resulting in 18 studies for meta-analysis.

The included studies involved 2312 individuals with the number of disease patients ranging from 6 to 127 in each study (table 1). Diagnostic performance was assessed for a range of medical conditions: inflammatory bowel disease (n = 5) [27, 33, 36–38]; colorectal cancer (n = 3) [29, 42, 44]; various infections (i.e. C. difficile, Escherichia coli, and sepsis) (n = 3) [32, 43, 48]; liver disease (n = 2) [31, 34]; diabetes (n = 2) [39, 40]; anastomotic leakage (n = 2) [46, 47]; bile acid diarrhoea (n = 1) [28]; coeliac disease (n = 1) [30]; gastrointestinal toxicity (n = 1) [26]; tuberculosis (n = 1) [35]; ovarian cancer (n = 1) [41]; and pancreatic cancer (n = 1) [45]. Most of the studies involved the analysis of urine (n = 13) [27–31, 39–42, 44–47], followed by faeces (n = 6) [26, 32, 36–38, 43] and breath (n = 3) [33–35]. One study involved the analysis of muscle or tissue excisions and secretions during surgery or postoperative care [48]. The majority of studies reported the use of the random forest algorithm (n = 7) [31, 32, 36, 38, 43, 46, 47] to give the best diagnostic performance, followed by sparse logistic (n = 6) [30, 33, 34, 37, 40, 44], fisher discriminant (n = 3) [26, 27, 29], Gaussian process (n = 2) [35, 39], linear discriminant (n = 2) [28, 45], quadratic discriminant (n = 1) [41], logistic regression (n = 1) [42], and other (n = 1) [48].

The STARD scores ranged from 9 to 20 with a mean score of 15 (SD = 3.1; table S4). Based on STARD guidelines, the major sources of potential bias include a lack of: (a) a reference standard; (b) time interval between reference standard and index test; (c) reporting of blinded studies or otherwise; (d) pre-specified analyses of variability to distinguish from exploratory studies; (e) determination of sample size; and (f) cross-tabulation of results.

The sensitivity of using micro-chip FAIMS ranged from 58% to 100% and specificity ranged from 44% to 97%. Of all included studies, the pooled sensitivity was 80% (95% CI, 74%–85%), and pooled specificity was 78% (95% CI, 70%–84%) (figure 1). Although the overall heterogeneity was relatively high for both sensitivity (I2 = 62%) and specificity (I2 = 80%), the purpose of pooled estimates is to obtain the overall diagnostic performance of micro-chip FAIMS across different medical conditions, and random effects meta-analysis model was adapted to incorporate the heterogeneity between studies.

There was no obvious asymmetry for the sensitivity funnel plots weighted by study size (figure S2), and the weighted linear regression test by Peters et al [25] is insignificant (p = 0.46); the specificity funnel plot (figure S3) may suggest asymmetry, and the asymmetry test was significant (p = 0.02).

Further subgroup analysis on machine learning methods (random forest and sparse logistic) revealed no significant difference between the performance of these methods in relation to sensitivity (p = 0.65) and specificity (p = 0.80). However, sparse logistic appears to perform slightly better compared to random forest (table 2 and figures S4–S7).

Similarly, the diagnostic performance is not significantly different between the two main sample matrices (faeces and urine) in terms of both sensitivity (p = 0.78) and specificity (p = 0.69). However, faecal analysis appears to perform slightly better compared to urine (table 2 and figures S8–S11).

Comparative performance of micro-chip FAIMS

3.2.

The overall performance of VOC analysis using micro-chip FAIMS was found to be comparable to that of VOC analysis of cancer using GC-MS based on the meta-analysis review by Hanna et al [8]. Out of 18 micro-chip FAIMS papers and 28 GC-MS papers (on breath samples only) [8], the overall sensitivity of micro-chip FAIMS is 80% compared to 79% for GC-MS; the overall specificity of microchip FAIMS is 78% compared to 89% for GC-MS. In addition, for the three micro-chip FAIMS studies that compared directly with GC-MS or e-nose methods, i.e. in the diagnosis of bile acid diarrhea [28] and diabetes [39] (e-nose), and colorectal cancer [44] (GC-MS) from urine samples, micro-chip FAIMS achieved comparable or higher performance (table S5).

Discussion

4.

The analysis of VOCs in the diagnosis of various medical conditions has demonstrated that microchip FAIMS is a promising point-of-care device and a potential alternative to conventional instruments such as e-nose and GC-MS for a broad range of sample matrices whilst obtaining comparable diagnostic performance.

However, multiple sources of variability were identified in the micro-chip FAIMS methods (table S6), which could impact diagnostic performance.

Influence of micro-chip FAIMS performance based on protocol

4.1.

Sample storage

4.1.1.

Although the sample size for exhaled breath was too small for meta-analysis, the diagnostic performance for exhaled breath was relatively high compared to urine and faeces (table 1). This could be partly attributed to differences in sample storage. Breath samples were analysed within 24 h of collection, unlike the urine and faeces samples which were stored for up to 60 months (table S7). Sample degradation can depend strongly on storage conditions. For example, the presence of microbial intervention in urine or faecal samples when not stored optimally is documented (e.g. storing urine samples above −25 °C, storing faecal samples at room temperature or undergoing freeze-thaw cycles) [49, 50].

Amongst urine studies, samples analysed within 12 months resulted in similar or higher diagnostic performance than those stored longer [39, 46]. In particular, the analysis of urine samples that were frozen at ∼−80 °C then thawed in ∼4 °C for 24 h prior to sample introduction resulted in better performance than that for samples which were defrosted at room temperature [39].

Amongst faecal studies, the highest diagnostic accuracy was obtained using an optimised sampling procedure in which ∼500 mg of faeces were stored at −20 °C, and kept at room temperature for only 10 min prior to analysis [37, 38]. Diagnostic accuracy also decreased when faecal samples were stored at room temperature for 3 h prior to freezing rather than directly freezing [37].

Sampling protocol

4.1.2.

Broad diagnostic performance can be influenced by various external factors such as dietary food, medication, and sampling technique. For example, the higher diagnostic accuracy observed in breath analysis could be related to lower levels of interference as individuals needed to fast for 2 h prior to sample collection. In contrast, for both urine and faecal analysis, little information is presented for pre-sample collection. However, for one urine study that detailed a morning sample collection, the sensitivity was relatively high, indicating that the time of sample collection could be an important factor in minimising interference [41]. Similarly, for three faecal studies that reported exclusion details (e.g. use of antibiotics/probiotics 3–6 months prior to sampling), diagnostic performance was relatively high, indicating a possible influence of medication on faecal VOCs [36–38].

Data analysis

4.1.3.

Although there was heterogeneity in the algorithm that was used to obtain the highest accuracy, the most frequently reported algorithms were sparse logistic regression and random forests. These algorithms are distinct frameworks that can be useful for data analysis. In the case of sparse logistic regression, a small subset of relevant predictors are used to model and report a binary response (e.g. patient with disease vs healthy control) [51]. Similarly, in random forests, a final outcome is made by aggregating predictions from a number of decision ‘trees’ that are built using different subsets of training examples and features [52, 53]. Here, for studies that reported direct comparisons between the two algorithms, random forests generally performed better [36–38]. This supports recent research that indicates random forests generally perform better on real world datasets [54].

Additional challenges and proposed framework

4.2.

Some heterogeneity could also be attributed to several intrinsic and extrinsic experimental and analytical-based factors. Here, sources of variability are discussed and recommendations are provided to assist reducing heterogeneity in future studies (table 3).

Types of samples

4.2.1.

Breath

4.2.1.1.

Although the breath matrix can be considered less complex than other bodily fluids [55], exhaled breath contains thousands of substances and molecules, the understanding of which is limited [56]. In addition, the collection of exhaled breath has logistical and methodological challenges [46, 47]. The primary challenge is that some VOCs from breath can be exogenous [57, 58]. The detection of endogenous VOCs can be masked by several factors including back-ground air quality, food intake, medication, infections, and environmental contaminants [59]. Many trace VOCs are present in ambient air which can be inhaled. This can be further complicated when exposed to areas with high air pollution levels. In addition, poor oral hygiene and the consumption of some foods such as sugars, banana, and coffee can influence VOC profiles, in addition to both local and systemic disease processes [59–61]. Moreover, owing to diffusion effects and decay of gas samples [59, 62], breath samples have relatively short storage lives. Exhaled breath also reflects a component deriving from the pulmonary vasculature as well as the lungs, further complicating the interpretation of VOC results.

Urine

4.2.1.2.

Urine matrices are more chemically complex than breath. The VOC profile of urine also depends on many metabolic pathways involving food, environmental contaminants, and bacterial by-products [63]. One of the main challenges in urine analysis is to control for exogenous VOCs, particularly those derived from diet and food [7, 64]. For example, foods that produce more volatile VOCs such as asparagus, onions, garlic, broccoli, and cabbage should be avoided prior to sample collection [64, 65]. Although urine samples can be stored for longer periods of time, obtaining optimal defrosting conditions for improved VOC detection should be considered. That is, variables such as defrosting at room temperature and thawing in a fridge prior to analysis should be investigated.

Faeces

4.2.1.3.

Faecal matrices are also highly complex as they involve various digestive processes and metabolic pathways, and reflect the individual faecal microbiome in addition to sample heterogeneity [30, 66, 67]. Naturally, variability in food intake and diet can influence VOC profile of faeces, e.g. alcohol, grains, plants, and western diet [68]. Similar to urine samples, VOC analysis can vary greatly depending on the sampling conditions. Thus, variables such as the amount of faeces and storage conditions (e.g. freezing and thawing at room temperature) should also be considered.

Analytical

4.2.2.

Greater standardisation of diagnostic reporting (e.g. STARD guidelines) [19] is recommended to strengthen experimental design, completeness of reporting, and interpretation of results between studies. For example, micro-chip FAIMS performance should ideally be benchmarked with a reference standard (e.g. histopathology in inflammatory bowel disease or malignancy, or definitive culture results with infections), analysis of variability should be prespecified, and sample size should be pre-determined. To reduce potential bias in analysis, an independent and blinded validation cohort should be used to report the final diagnostic accuracy with a particular machine learning algorithm.

Although more standardised procedures should be considered in single-centre trials, evidence from these studies generally do not necessarily have broad implications [69]. Thus, to ensure more widespread clinical applicability, large-scale multicentre studies should be investigated.

Limitations

5.

The results of this review and meta-analysis are limited to the performance of micro-chip FAIMS and studies in which diagnostic accuracy was included (i.e. five studies did not report values for sensitivity and specificity, and were thus excluded from this meta-analysis). Due to the recent emergence of micro-chip FAIMS for medical diagnosis there is limited evidence from multi-centre trials and some studies had relatively small sample sizes. Results may also be confounded by clinical factors, including the heterogeneity and stage of disease, co-morbidities, and exogenous compounds. Thus, the findings reported here should be considered in this context. Given that the meta-analysis pools data across multiple medical conditions to obtain an overall diagnostic performance, the results of this analysis does not directly inform clinically on specific diseases and cannot be used to predict the performance of micro-chip FAIMS for diseases that were not included in this analysis. In addition, the overall diagnostic accuracy across various medical conditions may be lower than that reported here if there were any unsuccessful studies that were not reported in the literature. In addition, the asymmetry observed in the funnel plot of specificity might result from the difference between smaller and larger studies which arise from inherent between-study heterogeneity [25, 70], however potential publication bias might also exist, and the pooled estimate from the meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

6.

The integration of micro-chip FAIMS and classical machine learning algorithms is promising for portable medical diagnostics. In particular, the overall diagnostic performance of micro-chip FAIMS for various medical conditions and sample matrices is comparable or higher than conventional lab-based instruments. Of particular note, micro-chip FAIMS has shown promising results for pancreatic cancer and diabetes in which ∼80% or higher sensitivity and specificity were obtained from a reasonably-sized cohort. Additionally, similar diagnostic performance was observed in gastroenterology studies (i.e. inflammatory bowel disease and coeliac disease) in which the sensitivity and specificity ranged from 74% to 97%. The relatively high overall sensitivity and specificity indicate that VOC analysis using microchip FAIMS may be useful in population screening campaigns such as for gastroenterology diseases. For example, the overall sensitivity and specificity using low-dose computed tomography to screen over 53 000 participants for lung cancer was 93.8% and 73.4%, respectively [71]. Similarly, overall sensitivity and specificity using mammography to screen over 72 000 participants for breast cancer was 77.0% and 91.4%, respectively [72].

Investigations using carefully designed prospective multi-centre clinical trials are anticipated in the future which would be useful to further evaluate the clinical applicability of micro-chip FAIMS. Moreover, for micro-chip FAIMS to be used at the point-of-care, several additional considerations should be investigated such as testing for instrument robustness and reliability, establishing an optimised and universal sampling procedure, automating data analysis, and engaging medical professionals and patients to test for user-friendly protocols. In addition, with recent developments in FAIMS instrumentation, such as high-resolution ion separation [73, 74], further technological improvements to portable instrumentation can be expected to significantly improve diagnostic performance, particularly at earlier disease stages, and enhance clinical relevance. The recommendations reported here should be considered in the development of portable instrumentation for VOC analysis and considered in the design of clinical studies that would allow for biomarker analysis to contribute towards medical diagnoses at the point-of-care.

Table 3.

Summary of experimental and analytical recommendations.

Experimental
Breath Environmental:
• Monitoring air quality in the laboratory.
• Exposing participants to ‘clean’ air prior to analysis.
• Oral hygiene (e.g. use of mouth wash).
Dietary:
• Longer fasting period prior to analysis.
Sample analysis:
• Analysing samples as soon as possible.
Analyse samples at body temperature.
Urine Dietary:
• Including a fasting period prior to analysis.
• Monitoring diet and/or keep record of food intake and medication.
Sample storage and analysis:
• Investigate optimal defrosting conditions (e.g. thaw in fridge or freezer prior to analysis).
• Investigate optimal time of sample collection (e.g. first thing in the morning).
Faeces Dietary:
• Including a fasting period prior to analysis.
• Monitoring diet and/or keep record of food intake and medication.
Sample storage and analysis:
• Investigate optimal defrosting conditions (e.g. thaw in fridge or freezer prior to analysis).
• Investigate optimal faecal mass for sample analysis.
Analytical
(a) Standardisation of diagnostic reporting (e.g. STARD guidelines)
   • e.g. inclusion of reference standard, pre-specified analysis of variability, pre-determined sample size.
(b) Validation using an independent and blinded cohort
   • e.g. testing and training of machine learning algorithm with one cohort; validation of model with a different cohort.
(c) Broad clinical applicability
   • i.e. conducting large-scale, multi-centre studies.
