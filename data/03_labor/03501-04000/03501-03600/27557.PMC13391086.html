Exercise Therapy and Radiation Therapy for Cancer: A Systematic Review

Abstract

Purpose:

Exercise therapy (ET) is shown to improve toxicity and surrogates of survival for patients receiving chemotherapy. Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines lack recommendations for concurrent radiation therapy (RT) and ET. The main objective was to determine the impact of concurrent ET + RT with respect to (1) acceptability, feasibility, safety; and (2) to demonstrate how incorporating ET in cancer treatment can enhance patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and physical function–defined as strength or exercise capacity.

Methods and Materials:

A PICOS/PRISMA selection protocol was used to search PubMed, Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and Cochrane Review for prospective randomized controlled trials evaluating concurrent ET + RT, including >10 patients and with 1 or more study arms. Acceptability, feasibility, and safety rates were calculated. PROs were assessed with study-specific metrics. Physical function was defined as improvements in strength or range of motion. Statistically significant improvement was defined by P <.05.

Results:

Twenty-six of 693 screened studies including 1563 patients (831 receiving exercise, 732 controls) with localized breast cancer (67.1% of patients), prostate cancer (27.4%), head and neck cancers (2.8%), and spinal metastases (2.8%) were assessed. Objective 1: Among 3385 patients approached for ET, 1864 (55.1%) accepted the treatment; of those, 1563 patients (83.9%) completed the trials. Objective 2: Statistical improvements were noted in these PROs: quality of life (14 of 15 studies), fatigue (12 of 16 studies), mood/depression (9 of 13), and anxiety (6 of 7). Physical function improved statically in 16 of 16 studies.

Conclusions:

Combination ET + RT is safe and well-tolerated with improvements in PROs and physical function. Additional studies are needed in patients with metastatic cancers to assess survival and to compare effectiveness of different exercise regimens.

Introduction

In 2021, the American Cancer Society (ACS) estimates 1.9 million newly diagnosed cancer cases in the United States. Radiation therapy (RT) is used in 50% of cancer patients1 and may have significant toxicities that negatively affect quality of life (QOL). Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that exercise therapy (ET) improves treatment by reducing toxicities among cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy and into survivorship. ET improves patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and surrogates of longevity, including performance status.2–5 Moderate ET has been shown to improve fatigue, anxiety, and self-esteem, as well as improve vascular stability, muscle strength, and muscle mass.6–9

Although the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) endorses the importance of regular exercise for cancer prevention and recurrence, there are no recommendations regarding the formal integration of ET into the treatment regimen for cancer patients receiving RT. Similarly, the American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) and the European Society for Radiation Oncology (ESTRO) do not comment on integration of ET in radiation oncology–specific guidelines. According to the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), most clinical studies analyzing the effects of ET are limited to patients receiving chemotherapy. Furthermore, most studies focus on patients who have completed anticancer therapy5 and patients with localized (nonmetastatic) breast10 or prostate cancer.11

Our purpose is to determine the impact of concurrent ET + RT with respect to (1) clinical acceptability, feasibility, and safety; and (2) to demonstrate how incorporating ET in cancer treatment can enhance PROs and physical function–defined as strength or exercise capacity. We hypothesize that integration of ET in radiation oncology clinics is acceptable by patients, is feasible in the clinic, can be done without an increase in toxicity, and improves PROs of patients receiving RT.

Methods

Literature selection

We used the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study design (PICOS) approach to define the inclusion criteria (Supplementary Table E1).12–14 We performed a systematic search using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) literature selection process (Supplementary Fig. E1).

We searched for full-text, published medical literature with human beings from 1960 to 2017 in PubMed for the terms (“cancer” or “neoplasm” or “malignancy”) and (“radiation therapy” or “radiotherapy”) and (“exercise” or “yoga” or “resistance training” or “weight bearing exercise” or “weight-bearing exercise” or “weightbearing exercise” or “cardiovascular training” or “total body training” or “strength training” or “weight-lifting” or “weightlifting” or “weight training” or “Pilates”). These search terms were specifically modeled from a previous review to optimize search results and exclude unrelated articles.15 The terms were in titles or in medical subject headings (MeSH). The same search strategy was applied for the Cochrane and CINAHL databases. A depiction of the search strategy was detailed in a supplementary document, and a flow diagram is presented in the PRISMA figure (Supplementary Fig. E1). Initially, 733 articles were identified; removal of 15 duplicated studies within a single database and 25 duplicated articles across the databases yielded 693 unique results. Three authors (T.A., J.L., N.Z.) screened the studies and articles were excluded if they had the following features: (1) not written in English, (2) not having human beings, (3) no ET or RT, and (4) lack relevance to the study goal. Of the 693 articles, 631 did not meet our screening criteria, leaving 62 articles for eligibility review. Articles excluded based on eligibility had the following features: (1) lack of PROs, (2) systematic review or meta-analysis (though these were searched to find other eligible articles), (3) feasibility or pilot studies, as well as study protocols and/or designs, (4) abstract alone, (5) nonrandomized studies, (6) no use of concurrent RT, and (7) lack of relevance to study goal. Of the 62 studies, 39 were not eligible, which left 23 studies that met the inclusion criteria.3,4,16–36 The eligible articles as well as relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses were then examined for other potential studies by searching through their reference sections; through this backward-searching method an additional 3 studies were found.37–39 In addition, through Google Scholar we used the forward-searching method to look for studies that may have cited our eligible articles. No new studies were found through the forward-citation method. Ultimately, we included 26 studies in this study.4,16–40

Data abstraction and analysis

From the 26 studies, characteristics of patients (eg, age), cancer (eg, disease site, stage), treatment (eg, RT doses, chemotherapy), ET (eg, intensity, frequency, duration), PROs, physical function, toxicity, and other outcomes were coded.

PROs included QOL, fatigue, sleep, sexual function, anxiety, pain, and cognition. PROs were commonly measured using standardized questionnaires and scales. For QOL, these include the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–General (FACT-G),4,21,28,30,38 Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Breast Cancer (FACT-B),3,38 Medical Outcomes Study 36-Item Short Form Health Survey on Quality of Life (MOS-SFFS6336-QOL),16,17,26,27 European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire (EORTC-QOL),18,24,28,29 and World Health Organization Quality of Life Questionnaire (WHO-QOL).34 For fatigue, these include the Brief Fatigue Index (BFI),16,17,21,31,34,35,37 Piper Fatigue Scale (PFS and R-PFS),25,30,39 Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Fatigue (FACT-F),3,4,28,29,38 and Fatigue Assessment Questionnaire (FAQ).22,24 If intensity was not reported explicitly, we categorized intensity based on the type of ET described (eg, qigong and yoga were low-intensity ET) or as the authors described in the studies. Exercise characteristics were coded using the frequency, intensity, time, and type (FITT) criteria.

For objective 1, acceptability was defined as: (the number of patients accepting to perform ET + RT)/(the number of participants screened). The number of participants screened was not always available, so the number of participants approached or eligible was substituted for the acceptability calculation. Feasibility was defined as: (number of patients who completed ET + RT)/(number accepting to perform ET + RT). Dropout was defined as the number of patients who did not complete the study intervention, including those who died during the course of the study. Safety was defined as freedom from any Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade 3 or higher event attributable to the addition of ET, per the assessment of the authors of the primary study. The CTCAE grading of the studies was reported based on the finding of each particular study. For those studies that did not report a CTCAE, the authors (TA, JL, NZ) formulated a grade based on the CTCAE version used during that time. Overall, the CTCAE ranged from version 2 to 5, depending on the year of the study. In general, for all versions of the CTCAE, a grade 3 toxicity is considered requiring procedural intervention, and grade 4 toxicity is considered to require more intensive care. For any grade 3+ adverse events from ET + RT, we also clarified whether they were due to combination ET + RT or an unrelated event.

Assessment of the risk of bias

The risk of bias for each study included was assessed using the Cochrane’s “Risk of Bias” Assessment Tool (Table 1), which uses 6 domains to rank study bias as low-, medium-, or high-risk. These domains include selection bias (use of randomization), performance bias (blinding of participants and study personnel), detection bias (blinding of outcome assessors), attrition bias (incomplete outcome data collection), reporting bias (selective reporting of some outcomes but not others, depending on the nature and direction of the results), and other additional sources of bias (dissimilarities in groups at baseline).41

All 26 (100%) studies4,16–40 avoided bias in the selection process by randomizing the participants; they also avoided attrition bias by obtaining outcomes from more than 85% of their participants and reporting bias. Only 5/26 (19.2%) studies4,28,29,38,40 addressed detection bias and none addressed performance bias. Finally, all 26 (100%) studies4,16–40 reported on the similarity among groups at baseline. All included studies, abstracted data, and bias assessments were re-reviewed by 3 oncologists and exercise physiologist (N.Z., J.R., N.S., K.S.). Owing to variability in the measurement of PROs among the included studies, a quantitative heterogeneity assessment could not be performed, so a qualitative systematic review was performed instead.

Results

Studies and patients

The 26 studies included in the analysis were published from 2003 to 2017 across 9 countries, including the United States, China, India, Korea, Germany, Spain, Poland, Canada, and the United Kingdom. In total, 1563 patients were included, with 831 patients in ET + RT intervention groups and 732 patients in RT-alone groups. The median age of all participants was 57.5 years with an interquartile range (IQR) of 50.8 to 65.4. The 26 papers studied the effects of ET + RT on spinal metastases and breast, prostate, and head and neck cancers; none studied the effects of ET + RT on more than 1 disease site. With the exception of the 3 papers on spinal metastases,32,33,36 all of the papers included nonmetastatic cancers.

Exercise types

ET interventions were grouped into 4 categories, including resistance training only (6/26 studies, 23.0%),22–24,32,33,36 aerobic exercise only (7/26, 27.0%),25,30,31,34,38–40 yoga (6/26, 23.0%),16–21 and mixed/other exercise (7/26, 27.0%).4,26–29,35,37 Mixed/other exercise studies were most common (n = 519, 33.2%) with 2 studies using qigong (n = 158, 10.1%)35,37 and 5 studies using a combination of aerobic exercise and resistance training (n = 361, 23.1%).4,26–29 This was followed by aerobic training alone (n = 478, 30.6%), then yoga (n = 368, 23.5%), and finally resistance training alone (n = 198, 12.7%). Supervised interventions were applied in 23/26 (88.5%) studies4,16–24,26–30,32–38,40, and 14/26 (56.0%) studies16–20,25,31–33,35–39 were home-based. In addition, 11/26 (42.3%) studies combined supervised and home-based exercises.16–20,32,33,35–38

All studies reported exercise intervention duration, which included the average length of each session (hours), average number of sessions per week (frequency), and the number of weeks of ET. Reporting using the FITT criteria is provided in Table 2. The median session duration was 0.8 hours (IQR, 0.5–1), performed for a median of 3 times per week (IQR, 3–3) during a median of 7.8 weeks (IQR, 6–12). For intensity levels, 8/26 (34.6%) were “low-,” 16/26 (53.8%) were “moderate-,” and 2/26 (11.5%) were “high-” intensity ET interventions (Table 2). The intensity levels were determined based on exercise type or as described by the author in the studies.

Objective 1: acceptability, feasibility, and safety of ET + RT

Among 3385 patients initially approached for ET intervention, 1864 (55.1%) accepted; of these, 1563 (83.9%) completed the study. The studies using resistance training22–24,32,33,36 had the highest rates of acceptability with an average of 95.7%. Some of the commonly reported reasons for patient refusal to partake in studies were low interest, work or time constraints, physical or medical contraindications, and access to transportation. Studies looking at spinal metastases32,33,36 and head and neck cancer27 reported the highest percent acceptability. The overall adherence and completion for the studies was 84.4%. These results are summarized in Table 3.

The majority of the studies we analyzed did not mention adverse events related to ET + RT occurring during their trials. Only 2 studies reported on any adverse events in general, and only 1 study reported on adverse events specifically due to ET + RT. Segal et al4 reported a total of 3 individuals in the exercise arm who experienced cardiac-related complications during ET + RT. One participant reported chest pain in the resistance training group, and another participant had serious ventricular tachycardia while performing aerobic exercise. The third individual in the aerobic training group experienced syncope before aerobic exercise, which was not due to the exercise intervention. Courneya et al40 reported having 7 individuals (5 in the exercise group and 2 in the control group) experience adverse events, but they were considered to be unrelated to ET + RT. Overall, only 1/26 (3.8%) studies4 reported on adverse events possibly related to ET + RT, indicating that combination ET + RT is safe (Table 2).

Objective 2: PROs and objectively assessed outcomes

Outcomes of interest

Given the heterogeneity in the reporting of outcomes and outcome assessment metrics, a systematic review was performed instead of a meta-analysis. Statistically significant findings of PROs and objectively measured physical functions are displayed in Table 3 and Figs. 1 and 2. The most commonly reported PROs were anxiety, mood/depression, fatigue, and QOL. Anxiety was improved in 6 of 7 (85.7%) studies18–20,29,34,39 and unchanged in 1 of 7 (14.3%) studies,17 and mood/depression was improved in 9 of 13 (69.2%) studies18–20,29,33,34,37–39 and unchanged in 4 of 13 (30.8%) studies.16,17,24,30 Fatigue showed improvement in 12 of 16 (75.0%) studies4,16,21,24,25,28–31,34,37,39 and was unchanged in 4 of 16 (25.0%) studies,17,22,35,38 and QOL showed improvement in 14 of 15 (93.3%) studies4,16,17,21,24,26–29,33,34,37,40 and was unchanged in 1 of 15 (6.7%) studies.38 Physical function demonstrated improvement in 16 of 16 (100%) studies,4,16,17,22–31,34,38,39 and measurements of exercise capacity (eg, maximum rate of oxygen consumption or VO2 max and metabolic equivalents or METs) showed improvement in 7 of 7 (100%) studies.4,26,28–30,39,40

Outcomes as a function of exercise type

Of the 6 studies that used yoga as the primary exercise intervention, the outcome measures most commonly reported included anxiety (4 of 6 studies, 66.7%),17–20 mood/depression (5 of 6, 83.3%),16–20 and fatigue (3 of 6, 50.0%).16,17,21 For anxiety, 3 of 4 (75.0%) studies18–20 demonstrated significant improvement for the patients in the exercise arm compared with the control arm, and 1 study reported no significant effect on anxiety.17 For mood/depression, 3 of 5 studies (60.0%)18–20 found significant improvement in the exercise arms compared with the control arms. For fatigue, 2 of 3 studies (66.7%) demonstrated significant improvement in the exercise group.16,21 Two studies16,17 found that yoga yielded no effect on mood/depression, and 1 study16 found no effect on fatigue, likely because mood/depression and fatigue are at their worst at the completion of RT, which was noted in the control groups of those studies.

Of the 6 studies that used resistance training as the primary exercise intervention, 3 studies22–24 focused on the same breast cancer patient population, and the other 332,33,36 focused on the same spinal metastasis patient population. Thus, the effects of resistance training were limited to only 2 sets of patients. Outcome measures most commonly reported included fatigue (2 of 6, 33.3%),22,24 pain (2 of 6, 33.3%),24,33 QOL (2 of 6, 33.3%),24,33 mood/depression (2 of 6, 33.3%),24,33 and physical function (2 of 6, 33.3%).22,24 Significant improvements regarding these 5 outcomes were found in both studies for QOL (2 of 2, 100%),24,33 physical function (2 of 2, 100%),22,24 and pain (2 of 2, 100%),24,33 and only in 1 study for fatigue (1 of 2, 50.0%)21 and mood/depression (1 of 2, 50%).36

Of the 7 studies that used aerobic exercise as the primary intervention, the outcome measures most commonly reported included mood/depression (4 of 7 studies, 57.1%)30,34,38,39 VO2/METs (3 of 7, 42.9%),30,39,40 fatigue (6 of 7, 85.7%),25,30,31,34,38,39 QOL (4 of 7, 57.1%),30,34,38,40 and physical function (7 of 7, 100%).25,30,31,34,38–40 For physical function and VO2/METs, all studies that reported on these outcomes found significant improvements for the patients in the exercise arm compared with the control arm.25,30,31,34,38–40 In terms of fatigue, 5 of 6 (83.3%) studies25,30,31,34,39 reported significant improvement, and 1 (16.7%) study38 showed no improvement. Three studies demonstrated significant improvement for QOL30,34,40 and mood/depression,34,38,39 and 1 study showed no improvement for QOL38 or mood/depression.30

For the 7 studies that used a mixed intervention (ie, aerobic exercise and resistance training) or other forms of intervention (ie, qigong), the outcome measures most commonly reported included fatigue (5 of 7, 71.4%),4,28,29,35,37 measurements of exercise capacity (ie, VO2 and METs) (4 of 7, 57.1 %),4,26,28,29 QOL (6 of 7, 85.7%),4,26–29,37 and physical function (5 of 7, 71.4%).4,26–29 For the 5 studies reporting on fatigue, significant improvements were shown in 4 studies (4 of 5, 80.0%),4,28,29,37 and 1 study (1 of 5, 20.0%) showed no significant improvement.35 In the 4 studies assessing VO2 and METs, significant improvements were reported in all studies (4 of 4, 100%)4,26,28,29 when comparing the exercise arm with the control arm. Similarly, for QOL4,26–29,37 and physical function,4,26–29 all studies (4 of 4, 100%) demonstrated significant improvements for patients in the exercise arm (Fig. 1).

Outcomes as a function of disease site

Of the 26 studies, the most common cancers included were those of the breast (n = 1049, 67.1%),16–20,22–26,34,37–40 prostate (n = 428, 27.4%),4,21,28–31,35 spinal metastases (n = 43, 2.8%),32,33,36 and head and neck (n = 43, 2.8%).27 With the exception of the 3 separate manuscripts by Rief et al that described the effects of ET + RT on patients with spinal metastases, none of the papers discussing breast, prostate, or head/neck cancers included patients with metastases, instead only including participants with stages I to III cancer.

Of the 15 studies analyzing the effects of ET + RT on participants with breast cancer,16–20,22–26,34,37–40 improvements were most commonly seen in physical function and strength (10 of 10, 100%),16,17,22,24–26,34,38–40 QOL (7 of 8, 87.5%),16,17,24,26,34,37,40 fatigue (6 of 9, 66.7%),16,24,25,34,37,39 depression (7 of 10, 70%),18–20,34,37–39 anxiety (5 of 6, 83.3%),18–20,34,39 and cognition (3 of 3, 100%).16,18,34 Of the 7 studies4,21,28–31,35 analyzing the effects of ET + RT on participants with prostate cancer, improvements were most commonly seen in physical function and strength (5 of 5, 100%),4,28–31 fatigue (6 of 7, 85.7%),4,21,28–31 QOL (5 of 5, 100%),4,21,28–30 and VO2 (4 of 4, 100%).4,28–30 Of the 4 studies analyzing the effects of ET + RT on participants with head/neck cancer or spinal metastases,27,32,33,36 improvements were most commonly seen in medication use (2 of 2, 100%), bone density (1 of 1, 100%), QOL (2 of 2, 100%), mood/depression (2 of 2, 100%), and pain (1 of 1, 100%).27,32,33,36 All findings are shown in Fig. 2.

Additional findings that were reported include health perception, sleep, and sexual function. Health perception improved in 2 of 2 (100%) studies, of which 1 study analyzed the effects of aerobic exercise34 and another study analyzed the effects of yoga17 on breast cancer patients. Sleep improved in 3 of 6 (50.0%) studies, with 2 involving breast cancer patients: 1 study used aerobic exercise34 and 1 study used resistance training.23 The other study with sleep improvement looked at prostate cancer patients who underwent mixed/other intervention, although the benefits occurred midway through treatment and was not durable.35 On the other hand, no change was seen in 3 of 6 (50.0%) studies for sleep that analyzed breast cancer patients, in which 2 studies16,17 used yoga as an intervention and 1 used a mixed/other exercise intervention.37 Furthermore, sexual function was shown to have significant improvement in 2 prostate cancer studies: 1 using yoga training as an exercise intervention21 and another using a mixed/other intervention.28

Discussion

Combination ET + RT is well accepted by patients with a low risk of ET-related injury (<1%). Combination ET + RT significantly improves physical function and PROs, including QOL, fatigue, mood/depression, anxiety, and more. Integration of ET into treatment guidelines for select patients receiving RT is warranted.

The mechanism in which ET works concurrently with RT in improving physical function and PROs may be owing to exercise’s effect on inflammation.42,43 Although RT improves pain and can improve global QOL by reducing cancer-related symptoms, it is a double-edged sword in that RT may increase systemic inflammation, thereby negatively affecting fatigue and other aspects of QOL.44 Several methods have attempted to improve QOL, including altering the modality or dose of a type of cancer therapy,45,46 using granulocyte colony-stimulating factor support,47 introducing religion and spirituality,48 and using cognitive-behavioral therapy.49 These interventions have generally been limited because they either focus on altering the prescription of the therapy to treat the cancer (eg, radiation or chemotherapy),6–9 or because they likely have no impact on the physiology of the disease. On the other hand, randomized clinical trials demonstrate that ET (ie, individual courses of ET for 4–8 weeks’ time) decreases systemic inflammation, which is associated with decreased pain, anxiety, depression, and fatigue, and improves physical function, lean mass, and QOL.50 The ACS and the ACSM also recommend ET to improve outcomes and toxicities among nonmetastatic cancer patients receiving chemotherapy. Although ET among metastatic cancer patients receiving RT has rarely been attempted, it is unknown how these patients would benefit from ET. The benefits of ET in nonmetastatic patients may largely be owing to its mitigating effects on inflammation, which further improves PROs.

Only 1 other systematic review about ET + RT exists in the current literature. This review10 includes 9 studies (including 802 total participants) and assesses the effects of aerobic exercise, resistance training, yoga, qigong, tai chi, and Pilates on both fatigue and QOL in breast cancer patients undergoing adjuvant RT. The authors report that combined aerobic and resistance training led to significant reductions in fatigue but found no significant effects on QOL. Their findings are comparable to ours; however, our work expands to include other cancer patients (eg, prostate, head/neck, metastatic), evaluates exercise types by the FITT criteria, and assesses the implementation potential of ET regimens in radiation oncology treatments by reporting on acceptability, feasibility, and safety.

Several systematic reviews have been published about the impact of ET on cancer patients who have received chemotherapy and survivors who have completed treatment.5,51,52 The authors of those studies report an improvement in stress, anxiety, fatigue, and QOL. This current work is the only one that analyzes the effects of multiple modalities of ET on a wide variety of outcomes in patients with different subtypes of cancer, specifically during RT. This work may be used to support the use of ET as an adjunctive therapy in the NCCN guidelines, and not just for those who have completed therapy.53–56

Our work has some limitations. First, a majority of patients in the review were diagnosed with localized breast or prostate cancer. The 3 publications referenced for spinal metastases used the same patient population, and only 1 study evaluated head and neck cancer patients, so other cancer subtypes are under-represented. Thus, it is difficult to extrapolate our data on the tolerability of ET for patients with other cancers receiving therapies that cause significant impairments in QOL. More studies are therefore needed to assess the primary effects of ET with RT on other cancers. Nonetheless, there is little evidence to suggest that other cancer patients would be intolerable to ET, and they may even potentially benefit from ET during RT based on the results of our study. Additional studies addressing and comparing the health risks with regard to combination ET + RT would be required to fully elucidate the adverse risks of ET + RT.

Second, the heterogeneity of the included studies’ ET programs complicates this paper’s analysis in terms of providing a definitive recommendation on the suitable exercise parameters for each cancer subtype. As referenced in Table 2, the intervention subtypes are heterogeneous with exercise parameters of various frequency, intensity, length of exercise, sessions per week, and duration. It is unclear if more rigorous exercise (eg, cardio, aerobic, high-intensity interval training) is superior to stretching-type exercises (eg, yoga, Pilates). It also is unclear which exercise parameter is most suitable for individuals having a specific cancer or cancer staging. For example, in accordance with the “Choose Wisely” movement, the American Society for Radiation Oncology recommends shorter fractions of treatment (ie, 10 or fewer fractions versus single-fraction treatments) for palliative cases, such as bone metastases. With most ET ranging from 4 to 24 weeks, a shortened palliative treatment regimen may not fully reap the benefits of ET owing to the timeframe of either treatment. In certain studies, some patients received the majority of their ET after RT, such as in the case of palliative RT, followed by 6 months of ET (mostly nonconcurrent). With the published data, it is unclear if the benefit on QOL is from the concurrent portion of ET versus the nonconcurrent portion of ET. Ongoing clinical trials are necessary to better address how concurrent ET improves QOL. For example, several ongoing clinical trials (NCT clinical trial identifiers NCT03905356 and NCT04556045) incorporate much more overlap in the exercise therapy and radiation therapy portions, with a 1- or 3- month follow-up to assess QOL. Overall, we cannot make a recommendation for any 1 form of ET. On the other hand, we also were not able to assess the durability of the exercise intervention’s benefits nor effectively compare results from multiple treatment arms owing to the heterogeneity of the data. Further studies investigating the benefits of specific exercise parameters for different cancer subgroups and the durability of exercise treatment would be needed.

Furthermore, no studies in the current work assessed the impact of ET on survival. Because survival of breast and prostate cancer patients is generally excellent (>90% at 5–10 years), it is unlikely that a survival benefit would be present for these populations. The ideal population for ET may therefore be in patients with aggressive cancers (eg, pancreas, stomach, liver) in which life expectancy is less than 2 to 4 years,57 or those with metastatic disease receiving RT, in which life expectancy is generally 2 to 24 months.58 Furthermore, the outcome assessments in this study were performed at different times after ET + RT, and the outcomes were measured using different scales, precluding the use of a meta-analysis. Additionally, we do not have sufficient patient-level data and cannot conduct a more detailed analysis using covariates. We also cannot comment on a “plateau” effect of the benefits of ET; presumably, there is a sigmoidal dose-response relationship between the amount of ET (eg, in hours per week) and the benefit in QOL, and we are unable to characterize this relationship.

In the context of clinical practice, we recommend integration of ET + RT in treatment guidelines for select patients. Outside of cancer treatment facilities, ET can also be used in conjunction with primary care practice physicians. In our work, the majority of the studies (14/26, 56.0%) used ET in a home-based setting; thus, many patients may have the option to continue the exercise regimen at their own homes without the need to travel to the actual clinical site. It is critical that practitioners in other fields (eg, family medicine, internal medicine, OB/GYN) are aware of these recommendations to help facilitate exercise therapy.

Conclusion

Combination ET + RT is safe and well tolerated with improvements in PROs and physical function. Integration of ET + RT into treatment guidelines for select patients is warranted. Additional studies are needed in patients with aggressive or metastatic cancers to assess survival and to compare the effectiveness of different exercise regimens. This work supports integration of ET into cancer radiation therapy guidelines.
