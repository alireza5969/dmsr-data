Healthcare professional’s knowledge, attitude, and perception of epilepsy surgery: A systematic review

Debopam Samanta[a][*]

Megan Leigh Hoyt[b]

M. Scott Perry[c]

[a] Neurology Division, Department of Pediatrics, University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences, Little Rock, AR, USA

[b] Division of Neurology, Arkansas Children’s Hospital, USA

[c] Cook Children’s Medical Center, Fort Worth, TX, USA

Author notes:

Correspondence to: [*] Corresponding author at: 1 Children’s Way, Little Rock, AR 72202, USA. dsamanta@uams.edu (D. Samanta).

Abstract

Objective:

The epilepsy surgery treatment gap is well defined and secondary to a broad range of issues, including healthcare professionals’ (HCPs’) knowledge, attitude, and perception (KAP) toward epilepsy surgery. However, no previous systematic reviews investigated this important topic.

Methods:

The systematic review was conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for the Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. We identified a total of 652 articles from multiple databases using database-specific queries and included 65 articles for full-text review after screening the titles and abstracts of the articles. Finally, we selected 11 papers for qualitative analysis. We critically appraised the quality of the studies using the Joanna Briggs critical appraisal tool.

Results:

The qualitative analysis of the content identified several key reasons causing healthcare professional-related barriers to epilepsy surgery: inadequate knowledge and awareness about the role of epilepsy surgery in drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), poor identification and referral of patients with DRE, insufficient selection of candidates for presurgical workup, negative or ambivalent attitudes and perceptions regarding epilepsy surgery, deficient communication practices with patients regarding risk–benefit analysis of epilepsy surgery, and challenging coordination issues with the surgical referral. Neurologists with formal instruction in epilepsy, surgical exposure during training, participation in high volume epilepsy practice, or prior experience in surgical referral may refer more patients for surgical evaluation.

Conclusions:

While significant work has been conducted in a limited number of studies to explore HCPs’ knowledge gap and educational need regarding epilepsy surgery, further research is needed in defining the learning goals, assessing and validating specific learning gaps among providers, defining the learning outcomes, optimizing the educational format, content, and outcome measures, and appraising the achieved results following the educational intervention.

Introduction

1.

Approximately 30% of patients with epilepsy continue to have seizures despite drug therapy [1,2]. These patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) may suffer from neurocognitive and psychological decline, inferior health-related quality of life, and premature mortality [3–5]. Randomized studies have demonstrated superior efficacy of epilepsy surgery in DRE compared to medical management in children and adults [6–8]. Beyond effectiveness to achieve seizure freedom, epilepsy surgery is also safe, cost-effective, and associated with improved quality of life and reduced risk of early mortality. Yet, epilepsy surgery remains one of the most underutilized evidence-based treatment options, with limited numbers of surgeries performed among existing 10 million potential surgical candidates globally [9].

The well-defined, but poorly understood, epilepsy surgery treatment gap is secondary to a broad range of issues, including physician-specific barriers [9]. These barriers can be diverse, as patients with DRE receive care from a wide range of healthcare professionals (HCPs) (primary care providers, emergency room physicians, neurologists, epileptologists, psychologists, psychiatrists, and neurosurgeons) during various stages of the treatment. Knowledge, attitude, and perception (KAP) of epilepsy surgery can vary among these providers. Primary care HCPs may struggle with identifying patients with DRE and referring promptly to a higher level of care. In the secondary setting, neurologists may have inadequate knowledge about selection of appropriate candidates for surgery, discussing various surgical options with risks and benefits, providing individualized counseling, and referring patients to comprehensive epilepsy centers. Lastly, epileptologists in tertiary care centers may fail to do necessary presurgical work-ups in certain patients. No previous systematic reviews investigated the KAP of HCPs toward epilepsy surgery. By summarizing the published literature regarding HCPs’ KAP regarding epilepsy surgery, we seek to generate an in-depth understanding of the existing evidence and portray future needs for educational strategies, change in clinical practice, and research in this area. Our review research questions were:

  • What is known about HCPs’ knowledge regarding epilepsy surgery?

  • What is known about HCPs’ attitudes, perceptions, and referral practices for epilepsy surgery evaluation?

Methods

2.

Eligibility criteria

2.1.

The study inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Table 1. The study population consisted of HCPs with measurement of outcomes regarding KAP of epilepsy surgery.

Search strategy

2.2.

The systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for the Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [10]. We utilized the SPIDER[ (1) Sample, (2) Phenomenon of Interest, (3) Design, (4) Evaluation, and (5) Research type question format (adapted from the PICO tool) to formulate our search strategies [11]. We searched multiple databases (MEDLINE via EBSCO, PsychInfo, Web of Science, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL®) via EBSCO) with database-specific queries, using both controlled and free-text language (Table 2). Two review authors independently searched these databases, and any disagreement about study inclusion was resolved by consensus. We also searched the bibliographies of the selected articles manually to identify further relevant articles. We applied limits to select studies published in English from the inception of the databases until March 2021.

Study selection

2.3.

We identified a total of 652 articles from all searches, with 503 remaining after the removal of duplicates (Fig. 1). We read the titles and abstracts of these articles and included 65 articles for full-text review. We finally selected 11 studies (qualitative or quantitative) to be included in the systematic review that discussed HCPs’ (from primary, secondary, or tertiary settings, such as general practitioners, neurologists, and epileptologists) knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding epilepsy surgery. We excluded studies that focused primarily on healthcare students or trainees, patients, family members, and caregivers’ opinions and views regarding epilepsy surgery. We also excluded non-English articles (Table 1).

Data extraction and quality assessment

2.4.

We extracted the following information from the selected studies: study settings, study design, physician type, total physicians surveyed, the response rate of surveys, questionnaire characteristics, outcome measures, and findings. We critically appraised the quality of the studies using the Joanna Briggs critical appraisal tool [12]. This tool covers nine criteria for study design quality and biases in research to assess prevalence studies’ internal and external validity. The checklist with this tool evaluates if the included study has a representative sample, appropriate sampling strategy, a reasonable response rate, an adequate sample size, appropriate description and reporting of study subjects and setting, good data coverage of the identified sample, reliable and objective measurement of the condition, and proper statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

2.5.

The authors analyzed published results qualitatively, and the results (number and percentage) were reported narratively, focusing on common findings identified across the included studies.

Results

3.

General characteristics of included studies

3.1.

The characteristics of the included studies are displayed in Table 3. The first study that evaluated the KAP of HCPs regarding epilepsy surgery appeared in 2008, and the last included for this review was published in 2020. The majority of the included studies involved neurologists (adult, pediatric, or combined), with one study solely focused on family physicians. Two studies targeted neurologists at the national level, but most involved HCPs at the state or regional level. Only two studies assessed response among epileptologists. All studies were based on structured questionnaires/surveys, with one study using a mixed-method component with a limited number of interviews with specialists. All the studies were done in high-income countries (US-1, Canada-3, UK-1, Australia-1, Saudi Arabia-1, Italy-1, Germany-1, Netherlands-1, and Sweden-1). The sample size of physicians in these studies varied from 9 to 425 (a total of 1280 physicians responses were included in the review). Response rates of HCPs across the surveys ranged from 4.8% to 85%.

Quality assessment

3.2.

Overall, the included studies scored well for the sampling frame of the target population and the use of appropriate statistical analysis for outcomes measurement. (Table 4) All studies explained results appropriately with discussion and conclusion justified by the results. However, most studies failed to address the sample size calculation and utilized survey instruments with inadequate validity and reliability. Many studies had a low survey-response rate (as low as 4.8%) and did not conduct pilot testing before the main study.

Main findings

3.3.

The qualitative analysis of the content identified several key reasons causing HCP-related barriers to epilepsy surgery: inadequate knowledge and awareness about the role of epilepsy surgery in DRE, poor identification and referral of patients with DRE, insufficient selection of candidates for presurgical workup, negative or ambivalent attitudes and perceptions regarding epilepsy surgery, deficient communication practices with patients regarding risk–benefit analysis of epilepsy surgery, and challenging issues around coordination of surgical referral. Some characteristics of HCPs were also noted to be associated with attitudes toward epilepsy surgery (Table 5).

Knowledge and awareness about the role of epilepsy surgery in DRE

3.3.1.

Approximately half to three-quarter of neurologists have some knowledge regarding the role of epilepsy surgery in the management of DRE [13,14]. A study among Canadian family physicians showed only 53.7% felt that surgery should be considered ‘‘in selected cases” [15]. Neurologists have greater awareness (100% vs. 76.3%) about epilepsy surgery than other providers [14]. Distinction between epilepsy surgery and neuromodulation may not be evident among some HCPs. In a Canadian study, some HCPs considered VNS as the only mode of surgical therapy for epilepsy [14]. Another Canadian study reported that 53.2% of HCPs did not know the type of epilepsy that could be surgically treated [15]. In addition, 43.8 and 45.3% of participants were unfamiliar with the risks and potential benefits of epilepsy surgery, respectively [15].

Identification and referral of patients with DRE

3.3.2.

In 2009, the ILAE proposed the definition of DRE as ‘‘failure of adequate trials of two tolerated and appropriately chosen and used AED schedules (whether as monotherapies or in combination) to achieve sustained seizure freedom” [16]. Awareness of DRE is essential as these patients need to be referred to accredited comprehensive epilepsy centers within 3–12 months of unsuccessful treatment for further evaluation [17]. Neurologists have a greater awareness of the ILAE guideline compared to non-neurologists (100% vs. 25.8%) [14]. A study among Saudi Arabian neurologists showed 73% of respondents had familiarity with the overall content of the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) clinical practice guidelines on epilepsy surgery, which specify the importance of referral [13] Two other studies from Michigan, USA, and UK detailed that 46–77% of neurologists considered a need for unsuccessful polytherapy trials and 15% endorsed a trial of VNS therapy before defining patients with medically refractory seizures [18,19]. In the UK study, 59% of pediatric neurologists endorsed failure of ketogenic diet therapy as necessary before consideration for surgery [19]. Various studies showed that only 14–51.4% of HCPs could successfully identify patients with DRE [20,14,18,19]. A study among Australian general practitioners indicated that only 35% were aware of the definition of DRE [21]. In a study among neurologists in Canada, only 43.4% of participants correctly identified the need for referral in DRE with ongoing seizures, and 54.1% recognized the need to refer these patients promptly [20].

Selection of candidates for epilepsy surgery

3.3.3.

Shared decision-making – introducing choice, describing options, and helping patients explore available options before making a decision – is a critical component of quality patient-centered care with better health outcomes. However, the study from Michigan reported that 11% of neurologists did not discuss epilepsy surgery at all with their patients [18]. Additionally, approximately 40% of DRE patients may not be referred for presurgical workup based on neurologists’ opinion (perception of the low burden of seizures, psychiatric comorbidities, and associated psychogenic seizures) without considering patients’viewpoint [22]. Seizure frequency was especially important (68–90% of HCPs’) in the decision to refer for surgery (referral rate according to seizure frequency: 55% would refer patients for surgery if seizures monthly or more often, 27% every 3 months, and 15% every 6 months; only 3% if seizures occurred once a year) and given greater importance than patients’ own wish to pursue surgery [18,23]. The presumed location of an epileptogenic focus was also an essential criterion in the decision to refer patients for surgery (48–83% of neurologists in two studies): 97% would refer patients with hippocampal sclerosis compared to 71% without associated hippocampal sclerosis, 76% with an extratemporal lesion, and only 43% for nonlesional, presumed extratemporal onset epilepsy [18,23]. Approximately, 40% of Swedish neurologists indicate visualizing epileptiform activity on the EEG is important for candidate selection [23]. Duration of epilepsy was considered necessary by 35–57% of neurologists considering referral only after two years of diagnosis [18,23]. Several studies from diverse settings showed that most neurologists considered children younger than 10 years and adults older than 60–65 as challenging surgical candidates (75% and 64–79% of neurologists, respectively) [18,23]. A Swedish national study reported that 56% of neurologists considered associated intellectual impairment as a crucial negative factor for surgical decision-making [23]. Epileptologists may also consider some of the same variables (subjective appraisal of lower seizure frequency, older age, and intellectual impairment) for not referring patients for presurgical workup [24].

Attitudes and perceptions about epilepsy surgery

3.3.4.

The complex decision-making process (concerning epilepsy surgery) needs individual assessment of HCPs’ attitudes and perceptions about epilepsy surgery, as they may not be linearly correlated with the amount of knowledge. A study showed that 3.5% of family physicians felt surgery should never be considered for epilepsy, and 14.9% considered surgery only as a last resort [15]. Only 22.9% of the participants expressed a high degree of potential benefit from epilepsy surgery [15]. Other studies reported that 12–41% of neurologists had come across neurological complications (minor and major) of epilepsy surgery: a perceived rate much higher than the available evidence [19,23]. The study among Canadian family physicians showed that only 8.5% of respondents felt that epilepsy surgery had a low risk, and approximately 50% perceived that surgery constituted a moderate to very high risk [15]. Another study from Sweden showed that 53–61% of neurologists considered the effect of surgery on seizure frequency and the quality of life to be good or very good [23]. An Italian study regarding attitude toward epilepsy surgery showed two-thirds of neurologists scored well below (neutral attitude) compared to a panel of epilepsy experts who had a more positive and unified approach toward epilepsy surgery [25]. A study among neurologists in Saudi Arabia reported that only half of neurologists had a positive attitude toward surgery with a significantly positive correlation between knowledge and attitude scores (p < 0.001) [13]. On the other hand, some studies suggested up to 84% of neurologists felt that surgery was under-recommended and 92% indicated high or very high cost-effectiveness of epilepsy surgery [19,23].

Communication practices with patients with risk–benefit analysis of epilepsy surgery

3.3.5.

Individualized counseling with clear and accurate communication regarding the risks and benefits of epilepsy surgery is vital in surgical decision-making. Significant progress has been made in the understanding of epilepsy surgery outcomes. Despite accumulating evidence of the risks and benefits of epilepsy surgery, physician communication practices remain highly variable. In the Michigan study, 41% of neurologists reported not providing surgical outcome information to patients, more commonly in extratemporal lobe epilepsy [18]. These neurologists predicted seizure-freedom after epilepsy surgery more conservatively, with 42% of HCPs estimated a <70% chance of seizure freedom after temporal lobe surgery. However, they were more comfortable offering opinions about VNS, with 63% reporting >10% chance of seizure-freedom after VNS therapy [18]. Additionally, 70% of the neurologists in this study did not discuss potential complications associated with surgery with their patients. Moreover, 64% of participants who did give their patients information regarding complication rates provided a >5% complication rate of surgery [18].

Coordination issues before and after referral

3.3.6.

Coordinated epilepsy care with comanagement by primary and specialty physicians is vital to improve health outcomes in patients with DRE. One study showed that 56% of HCPs had experienced problems with liaison and information provision with the referral surgical center. Additionally, 16% of participants also experienced a patient not being returned to their care afterward [19]. Another study reported that following referral to the tertiary care from secondary settings, only medication adjustment was performed without considering epilepsy surgery at the time of referral or after further unsuccessful pharmacologic trials [22].

Characteristics of HCPs and attitudes toward epilepsy surgery

3.3.7.

Specific HCP characteristics were associated with a higher number of epilepsy surgery referrals: exposure to a higher volume of patients with epilepsy, previous experience with surgical referral, training in specialized programs with exposure to epilepsy surgery, formal instruction in epilepsy subspeciality, and graduation from medical school after the year 2000 (maybe a reflection of awareness of positive result of the first randomized trial of epilepsy surgery early in the career) [20,14,25]. The clinicians who referred more patients for surgery also considered themselves more knowledgeable about epilepsy surgery. Another study indicated that seniority, type of specialty (adult or child neurology), distance from a neurosurgical center, and availability of a specialized neurosurgeon in the region did not correlate with physicians’ attitudes toward epilepsy surgery [25]. A study among Saudi Arabian neurologists showed 71% of the neurologists who obtained neurology certification from North America had a greater degree of knowledge regarding epilepsy surgery than 52% of the neurologists who received it locally and only 10% of the neurologists who obtained it from other geographic regions [13]. Academic neurologists were also noted to have greater knowledge and expertise regarding epilepsy surgery and referred more patients for presurgical workup [13].

Discussion and the evolving educational landscape

4.

The HCPs’ KAP were systematically assessed in this study to highlight the educational- and practice-gap, one of the significant causes of the underutilization of epilepsy surgery. We searched multiple databases following a deliberately inclusive search strategy during the literature review, but we cannot exclude the possibility of missing relevant papers. As we excluded non-English language studies, it might have resulted in publication bias. The included studies are survey-based (survey instruments mostly lacked reliability and validity), which provided a less nuanced understanding of the KAP of HCPs. One study included mixed-methodology, but the qualitative component was minimal [21]. Detailed qualitative investigations are needed to gain a deeper and refined understanding of HCPs’ attitudes and perceptions about epilepsy surgery. While significant work has been conducted in a limited number of studies to explore HCPs’ knowledge gap and educational need regarding epilepsy surgery, minimal evidence-based educational strategies have emerged to improve provider knowledge and practice patterns.

The first step in addressing the knowledge gap needs is to determine appropriate curriculums to meet the HCPs’ educational needs, including primary care physicians who provide a significant portion of care in patients with DRE due to a shortage of neurologists in most countries [26]. Inaccessibility to specialty care is prevalent even in high-income countries, especially for people from minority groups (racial/ethnic/language minority, economically disadvantaged, and uninsured or insured through public health insurance) [27]. Even after referral to a higher level of care, primary HCPs frequently remain involved in the interdisciplinary collaboration and comanagement of their patients with specialists [28]. However, primary care providers receive limited exposure in epilepsy management during neurology clerkships and residency, and in general, feel less confident managing neurologic conditions [21,29,30]. New innovative curriculums – both in medical school and residency – may be helpful to raise awareness about identification, counseling, and referral of patients with DRE for consideration of epilepsy surgery. The ILAE developed a competency-based primary care educational curriculum (available in Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian, and Spanish), applicable in diverse health care systems and socio-economic settings [31]. The curriculum is proposed to highlight the importance of referral for defined indications, more urgently for specific epilepsy syndromes; it also put forward the significance of learning referral mechanism within the local healthcare system and effectively communicating with other physicians and patients about the necessity of referral [31].

Other than curriculums specific for primary care physicians, combined or specialty-specific curriculums have been developed. The Task Force for Epilepsy Education (EpiEd), founded in 2014, created a three-level (entry, proficiency, and advanced proficiency) competency-based epilepsy curriculum based on a worldwide online survey [32]. The curriculum consists of seven domains, 42 competencies, and 124 learning objectives and is divided into three levels: entry (Level 1), proficiency (Level 2), and advanced proficiency (Level 3) [32]. Education specific to epilepsy surgery includes seven competencies and seven learning objectives, including activities for Level 1 (demonstrating a working knowledge of indications for presurgical evaluation) and Level 2 (describing the importance of early surgical intervention regarding neuro-developmental, cognitive, behavioral, and social integration aspects and demonstrating a working knowledge of etiologies amenable to surgical treatment and their prognoses in all age groups) learners [32]. Besides curriculums focusing on independent practitioners, particular emphasis may need to be given to the residency and fellowship curriculums (neurology, neurosurgery, and epilepsy). In the USA, new subspecialty board certification in epilepsy by the American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology (ABPN) may allow standardized epilepsy education among trainees based on a core curriculum [33].

Effective educational format should be built around the curriculum to reach the intended target physicians and bring desired practice change efficiently. The optimum educational method for physicians is unknown. However, interactive, web-based highquality teaching has emerged as a popular method due to its flexibility, broad resource-sharing capacity, global reach, and cost-effective scalability [34]. Adaptive online training (rather than static slide sets or lectures of video) may promote active learning with instant feedback [35]. A Latin American E-learning course (8 modules course consisting of written didactic material, videos, and interactive discussions in Spanish and Portuguese and include learning opportunity about referral indications for a higher level of care in appropriate patients) for primary HCPs and neurologists was noted to have good retention, approval, and efficacy in improving self-confidence managing patients with epilepsy (21.4% at baseline to 72.6% after completion of the course) among the participants [36]. A virtual campus and teaching academy (www.ilae-academy.org) has built around the EpiEd curriculum, integrating various learning portfolios (tutored e-courses-Virtual Epilepsy Academy; self-paced interactive e-learning courses- ‘‘learn from cases,” ebrain, and Epilepsy Imaging; and online multimedia resources) of the ILAE [37]. These assessment-based certificate programs provide a comprehensive, web-based, patient-centered, goal-oriented, affordable (reduced cost for HCPs from lower and middle-income countries) and outcome-based learning environment for successful adult learning with adequate knowledge and skill gain to change professional behaviors [37]. In a pilot test, the ‘‘Learn From Cases” was noted to have high relevance and usability of scenario-based, step-by-step, and interactive approach among Level 1 learners [37].

National organizations, such as the AAN and American Epilepsy Society, have made a range of educational resources available on the website: on-demand annual meeting contents, lectures, guidelines, self-assessment examinations, short-duration learning videos, digital audio files, webinars, and learning modules to meet the educational demands of busy physicians [38,39]. For example, one relevant learning video regarding epilepsy surgery (combined with neuromodulation) on the AAN website consists of a short video with learning objectives and an evaluation checklist (if the information is helpful to change practice and the depth of the educational content) [40]. Some attempt has been made to disseminate short video summaries of selected presentations using social media.

With the increasing global availability of e-learning, one must consider whether teaching is compatible with the learners’ target cultural and regional settings. Further efforts should be considered to make educational resources freely available, easy to find (open repository), and up-to-date. Another cost-effective alternative to short courses with real-time tutorial interaction may be using massive online open courses with asynchronous feedback. It is currently unknown if physicians are actively seeking out these resources and how effective these resources may be to mediate change in practice [41]. Besides effort from the ILAE and the relevant national organizations to improve awareness of the educational resources, HCPs can be incentivized to use these educational resources, such as through the provision of continuing medical education (CME) credits for the purpose maintaining continuing professional development [21].

In spite of the explosion of online learning platforms since the COVID-19 pandemic, some professionals may receive maximum benefit from other types of educational formats, such as learning from specialists during routine clinical encounters, reading peer-reviewed journals, and clinical guidelines specific to epilepsy surgery. In-person training may still be relevant, such as J. Kiffin Penry Epilepsy Education programs or mini fellowships to allow exposure to epilepsy surgery. Similarly, specialists also can attend epilepsy surgery short courses and workshops to learn practical tips from expert faculties about surgical strategy development and optimum implantation plan.

An increase in knowledge without attitudinal change may not bring a significant difference in the practice patterns [42]. Innovative teaching strategies and practical tools may be particularly relevant to facilitate positive practice patterns. Mastering new skills, such as counseling regarding surgery, conducting streamlined presurgical workup, etc., may be improved with ‘deliberate practice’ methodology [43]. Virtual reality may enable more accessible, deliberate practice targeting a specific skill repeatedly in a safe environment [44]. Additionally, quality improvement methodology can be particularly relevant; a telementoring program in epilepsy (project ECHO-Extension for Community Healthcare Outcome) demonstrated 26.7% improvement in tertiary-center referrals over six months among seven pediatric practices by incorporating QI methodology [45]. Besides new teaching strategies, data-driven decision-making tools can also facilitate physician practice, including online decision-support tools to identify patients with DRE requiring epilepsy center referral. A decision support tool for referral appropriateness has been developed and subsequently evaluated in diverse settings with an excellent concordance with the judgments of epileptologists [46–49]. Several statistical tools (nomogram or scoring algorithms) have also been developed and rigorously assessed to provide individualized counseling and estimate seizure-free outcomes or complications following surgery [50–54]. Additionally, artificial intelligence techniques may be utilized to facilitate the early identification of potential surgical candidates by processing HCP’s clinic notes and predict individualized surgical outcomes by assessing various data sources [55,56]. Besides targeting critical knowledge and practice gap, providers may benefit from directed training in relational strategies to use appropriate skills and tools to develop a positive and trusting relationship with patients and caregivers [42]. These strategies can be particularly beneficial in patients and families with misaligned or unrealistic expectations, significant cultural variations, or negative attitudes (skepticism or denial about epilepsy surgery). Finally, robust quality improvement and research networks (e.g., Epilepsy Learning Healthcare System) need to develop to foster connection among primary HCPs and epilepsy experts, and various epilepsy centers, with the ultimate aim to improve epilepsy outcomes.

Conclusion

5.

The purpose of understanding the KAP of HCPs is to devise strategies to overcome relevant barriers and optimize clinical outcomes. This systematic review demonstrated several key reasons underlying HCP-related barriers to epilepsy surgery that highlight knowledge gap and educational need regarding epilepsy surgery; however, further research is needed in defining the learning goals, assessing and validating specific learning gaps among providers, defining the learning outcomes, optimizing the educational format, content, and outcome measures, and appraising the achieved results following the educational intervention.

Hakimi (2008) Michigan (USA) Cross-sectional Neurologist 84 20% a 10-item survey 77% of respondents stated that patients with DRE had to fail a minimum of two polytherapy combinations and 15% considered failure of VNS a requirement for the diagnosis of DRE. Seizure frequency, epileptogenic focus, MRI abnormalities, epilepsy duration, and age were considered for surgical referral. 41% didn’t provide patients epilepsy surgery outcome statistics. When they did, they provided more conservative numbers (in comparison to the available evidence) for epilepsy surgery than the VNS and quoted higher complication rates than usually expected.
Kumlien (2010) Sweden Cross-sectional Neurologists 100 66% Survey with 12 primary questions The effect of surgery on seizure frequency was considered to be good or very good by 61% of the respondents and 53% evaluated the effect on quality of life to be good or very good and 92% cost-effectiveness of epilepsy surgery as high or very high. Neurological complications were experienced by 41% of respondents. The most important criteria for candidate selection were seizure frequency (68%) and the patient’s own wish (62%). The existences of lesions on MRI were very important to 45% of respondents and epileptiform activity seen on EEG to 40%. Duration of epilepsy was considered as less important to 65.3% of respondents. Age <65 years was very important, 22%, or important to 57% of respondents. Lack of mental retardation was very important to 11% and important to 45%.
Erba (2011) Italy Cross-sectional Adult and child neurologist 183 25–85% 14-item View and attitudes about epilepsy surgery were compared between neurologists versus a group of academic and clinical leaders in the field. The responses from neurologists gave responses characterized had around intermediate scores. Experts had higher and less variable scores favoring surgery. The two groups differed significantly on issues such as how long to pursue pharmacologic treatment and information about indications and outcome of surgery. The years in practice (seniority), type of specialty (adult or child neurology), distance from a neurosurgical center, and availability of a specialized neurosurgeon in the region did not correlate with physicians’ attitudes, whereas number of patients treated for epilepsy, number of patients sent for surgery, region where specialty was attained, and awareness of the randomized trial, reached statistical significance.
Hall-Patch (2012) U.K. Cross-sectional Pediatric Neurologists 38 33% 10-item Low level of knowledge regarding correct diagnosis of DRE. Example, 59% endorsed failure of the ketogenic diet necessary for consideration for surgery. 56% had also experienced problems with liaison and information provision with epilepsy centers. 12% reported patient with a serious unexpected neurological complication following surgery. 84% felt that surgery was under-recommended.
Uijl (2012) Netherlands Cross-sectional Neurologists Unknown 64% Questioning neurologists for the reasons for the nonreferral An expert panel review of the rates and causes of nonreferral by treating neurologists for surgery in potentially eligible patients showed that 74% (66 of the 89) of patients inappropriately excluded from the presurgical work-up due to low burden of seizures (neurologist’s judgement, psychiatric comorbidities, previous referral >5 years ago).
Roberts (2015) National (Canada) Cross-sectional Neurologist 425 53.5% 29-item, pilot-tested questionnaire High failure-rate to recognize patients with DRE and refer patients (and the need for promptness) for a higher-level care. Greater exposure to patients with epilepsy, higher self-believe in epilepsy related knowledge, and graduation from medical school after the year 2000 associated with greater referral for surgery.
Cothros (2016) Ontario, Canada Cross-sectional Family doctors 201 50% 11-item 53.7% felt that surgery should be considered ‘‘in selected cases” for the treatment of epilepsy and 53.2% did not know what type of epilepsy could be surgically treated. 43.8 and 45.3% of doctors were unfamiliar with the risks associated with epilepsy surgery and its potential benefits, respectively. 3.5% reported that surgery should never be considered and 14.9% that surgery should be considered only as a last resort. Only 8.5% of respondents felt that epilepsy surgery carries a low risk, and nearly 50% felt that surgery constituted a moderate to very high risk. Only 22.9% believed that there is a high degree of benefit of epilepsy surgery.
Steinbrenner (2018) Berlin, Germany Cross-sectional Epileptologists 9 NA Structured checklist to select cause of nonreferral for presurgical evaluation in patients with focal intractable epilepsy Clinical variables associated with epileptologists’ nonreferral showed that out of 185 eligible patients, 80 (43%) were recommended presurgical evaluation by their epileptologists. The most frequent reason for nonreferral was subjective appraisal of low seizure, older age of patients, no previous evaluation for epilepsy surgery, the presence of legal guardianship, and ≥11 years of professional experience by the treating epileptologist.

Aljafen (2020) Riyadh, Saudi Arabia Cross-sectional Adult and pediatric neurologists 106 53% 19- item Only 57.5% of the neurologists had a sufficient level of knowledge regarding epilepsy surgery (higher if certification from North America, University staff, had adequate expertise and resources, and previous referral experience for presurgical work-up). Overall, more than half of the neurologists (52.8%) had a positive attitude toward epilepsy surgery. 73% stated that they are familiar with the AAN guideline.
Hutchinson (2020) New South Wales, Australia Cross-sectional General practitioners (20), neurologists (3) and epileptologists (3) 26 NA A structured checklists of 34 questions for generalists and semi-structured interviews with 6 specialists Generalists struggled when dealt with vague presentations of seizures or if have limited expertise in epilepsy. Physicians felt difficultly with prompt and consistent referral due to lack of a national guideline and a standardized local referral process. 88% of generalists reported a need for more information; 60% wanted to use continuing professional development strategy to achieve more epilepsy specific information. Only 35% of generalists were aware of definition of DRE
