Prevalence of medication related osteonecrosis of the jaw in patients treated with sequential antiresorptive drugs: systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Background:

Antiresorptive drugs (ARD) are associated with a serious adverse event, known as medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaws (MRONJ). Transition from one ARD to another has become common clinical practice with the advent of more potent or safer agents; however, the influence of sequential antiresorptive therapy as a risk factor for MRONJ has not been established.

Objectives:

To investigate the prevalence of MRONJ in oncology or osteoporosis patients treated with two or more sequential ARDs as opposed to a single antiresorptive drug.

Material and methods:

Systematic electronic literature searches were conducted for Ovid Medline, Ovid EMBASE and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. Two review authors retrieved studies using pre-determined eligibility criteria and conducted quality assessment and data extraction. Fixed or random-effects meta-analysis models were used to summarize relative estimates for prevalence of MRONJ.

Results:

A total of 483 titles and abstracts were screened, and 18 full texts were retrieved for review. Twelve studies were included in the final qualitative and quantitative synthesis. Random effects meta-analysis models revealed a weighted pooled MRONJ prevalence of 19% (95% CI: 10-27%) for sequential pamidronate-zoledronate therapy, 10% (95% CI: 3-22%) for sequential ibandronate-zoledronate therapy. Pooled weighted prevalence of MRONJ was 13% (95% CI: 3-22%) for sequential bisphosphonate-denosumab therapy while bisphosphonates only was 5% (95% CI: 0-9%) and denosumab only was 4% (95% CI: 3-5%).

Conclusions:

The present systematic review showed an increased prevalence associated with sequential ARD therapy for pamidronate-zoledronate and bisphosphonate-denosumab administration when compared to single ARD therapy.

Introduction

Skeletal-related events (SREs) affect over 50% patients with osteolytic hematologic malignancy, namely multiple myeloma, and bone metastases from other malignancies.1-4 Irreversible complications from SREs, including hypercalcemia, fracture, bone pain, spinal cord compression, and need for radiation or surgery to bone, result in decreased survival, functional performance status, and quality of life, as well as high healthcare costs from increased risk of hospitalization. 1-6 Local extensive bone destruction from intensified osteoclastic activity results from an increased expression of receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANK-L) that are stimulated by growth factors and cytokines released by metastatic bone tumor cells.7

Prevention of skeletal complications by using antiresorptive drugs (ARDs) to inhibit osteoclast mediated resorption, including bisphosphonates and denosumab, have been a central component of treatment for metastatic bone and osteolytic malignancies.1, 8, 9 Currently used nitrogen containing bisphosphonates, such as alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, pamidronate and zoledronate are pyrophosphate analogs that inhibit osteoclast action by binding to hydroxyapatite that are taken up by mature osteoclasts at sites of bone resorption.10 Denosumab is a fully humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to RANK ligand and inhibits osteoclast formation, function and survival.10 Both bisphosphonates and denosumab have shown to significantly lower SREs, and improve patients’ quality of life.1-3, 8, 9 Furthermore, these two drugs in lower doses are also the mainstay of antiresorptive osteoporosis management.11

A specific adverse event associated with ARDs is termed medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ), which by consensus of the American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons (AAOMS) is characterized as exposed bone or probeable bone through an intra- or extra-oral fistula, which is persistent for more than 8 weeks in the maxillofacial region.12 The overall reported prevalence of ARD-associated MRONJ ranges from 0.3-6.7%.12-15 Systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials have reported a similar incidence of MRONJ for bisphosphonates and denosumab ranging from 0.9 to 3.1%.16-19

With introduction of newer more potent bisphosphonates (e.g. zoledronate after pamidronate) and denosumab, several patients likely underwent replacement of one ARD to another.20, 21 It has been hypothesized based on clinical impressions that transition in ARD therapy may have an additional risk for development of MRONJ due to cumulative osteoclast suppression from both drugs. The aim of this systematic review was to systematically analyze the literature, and conduct meta-analyses when possible, to answer the focused review question: “What is the prevalence of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw in oncology or osteoporosis patients treated with two or more sequential antiresorptive drugs compared to patients treated with a single antiresorptive drug?”

Methods

Protocol and eligibility criteria

A protocol was designed and approved by our multidisciplinary group of authors, consisting of subject matter experts. This systematic review was performed following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review) criteria. The criteria for study inclusion were: (a) studies assessing incidence/prevalence of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw following administration of two or more antiresorptive drugs sequentially for cancer or osteoporosis management; (b) clinical trials (phase 1,2,3, non-randomized, randomized) or prospective/retrospective cohort studies; (c) published in English language; and (d) full texts available. Search was limited to human participants (not animals) and studies were excluded if they did not meet all the inclusion criteria and if relevant clinical data was not clearly reported (see data extraction section).

Information sources and literature search

The search strategies were planned considering the following PECO22 statement:

P: Adults diagnosed with cancer or osteoporosis

E: Administration of two or more antiresorptive drugs sequentially

C: Administration of a single antiresorptive drug

O: Prevalence of medication related osteonecrosis of the jaw

We conducted systematic electronic literature searches including Medline (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid) and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) using database-specific search strategies (Appendices 1-3). The search was first conducted on December 10, 2018 and updated on June 5, 2019. We also supplemented the electronic search by manual bibliography screening of previously published reviews and included full text articles.

Study selection and data extraction

Two authors (AS, MC) independently screened titles and abstracts of retrieved references based on the predefined eligibility criteria. Consensus was achieved through discussion in any ambiguous abstract selections or disagreements. Updated search was screened by one author to screen any new reports published while this review was being conducted. Full texts of accepted titles were retrieved and screened for final inclusion for qualitative and quantitative synthesis.

Data was extracted by one author (AS) in a pre-designed excel sheet and reviewed by a second author (GNG) for any discrepancies. Data items extracted include: (a) general study characteristics- authors, year of publication, journal, study aim, (b) study design, (c) patient characteristics (underlying disease, age, gender), (d) definition and diagnosis of MRONJ, (e) follow up period, (f) total sample size and number of MRONJ cases in the sequential ARD and comparator ARD groups, (g) standardized dosage (h) time-to-event, dosage/treatment cycles to event, (i) location of MRONJ lesion, (j) number of dental extractions associated with MRONJ development. Additional details about materials, methods and results were retrieved from earlier reports on the same trial if they were lacking in the included full texts. Studies reporting data from two or more trials were separated by study design, when applicable. Median time of drug exposure-to-event was computed from raw data when summary measures were not reported in the included studies, where applicable.

Quality assessment

Methodological and reporting quality was assessed pertaining to representativeness, and sources of bias in measurement of MRONJ, including misclassification bias, examiner bias and outcome measure assessment validity. Quality grading criteria pertaining to these domains was adapted from methodology previously published and applied by the Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer (MASCC)/International Society of Oral Oncology (ISOO) group for incidence and outcomes evaluation of oral complications from cancer therapies.23 Table 1 outlines the grading criteria. Oral complication assessment validity was evaluated as high quality if standard validated criteria such as American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons (AAOMS) position paper,12, 24 or Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grading. Studies that described diagnostic criteria for MRONJ evaluation other than validated AAOMS/CTCAE grading were assigned quality points for study-specific scale/criteria.

Each full text included in final qualitative and quantitative synthesis was scored using quality points by two authors (AS, MC); disagreements were resolved by discussion till consensus was achieved. These scores were used for qualitative appraisal of published evidence and to ascertain the level of certainty in the evidence used for effect estimation. Quality scores were not applied for exclusion of articles or quantitative data syntheses, to avoid bias from applying quality weights for effect size estimation.25, 26

Summary measures and synthesis of results

Prevalence/proportion of ONJ along with the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were plotted using forest plots. A fixed continuity correction of a count of 0.5 was added to both successes and failures in the case where a study has 0% or 100% success rate.27 We assessed the studies’ heterogeneity using the I 2 statistic described by Higgins et al.28 which measures the percentage of total variation that is due to heterogeneity rather than chance. That is, the I 2 statistic was used to measure whether the studies considered in the meta-analysis were estimating the same effect. I 2 values of less than 25% represent low heterogeneity, whereas I 2 values between 25 and 50% represent moderate heterogeneity. If a statistically significant percentage of the total variation was found to be due to heterogeneity, then the combined proportion from the studies in the meta-analysis was estimated using a random effects model in which each study was weighted equally.

We also estimated potential publication bias (i.e., tendency to publish either positive or negative results) using funnel plots (Appendices 4-6).29 Symmetry in a funnel plot suggests that publication bias is not present. The vertical line in the funnel plot indicates the fixed-effects summary estimate. When performing the meta-analysis, we used the inverse variance-weighted average method.26 The other lines represent the 95% confidence interval (CI) for a given standard error assuming no heterogeneity among studies. If there are small studies, the result display will be more scattered in the graph, while the big studies will show more in the center of the funnel. The possible sources for the asymmetry are selection biases (publication bias or selective outcome reporting), poor methodological quality leading to spuriously inflated effects in smaller studies, true heterogeneity, artifact, and chance.30

Unweighted prevalence of MRONJ by type of malignancy was also computed by combining total number of MRONJ cases and total number of cases reported for each type of malignancy.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 depicts the flow diagram for literature search. Database search identified 710 original studies after de-duplication. Two additional studies were identified from manual bibliography searching. Based on title and abstract screening, 695 records were excluded; primary reason for exclusion of a majority of articles was: 1) study design (case reports/reviews), 2) not related to the topic, or 3) did not include a sequential ARD therapy group. Of the 17 full texts retrieved and assessed for eligibility, 5 studies were excluded; 2 records were conference abstracts with no identifiable full text publications by authors’ names or study details,31, 32 and 3 studies included only a single arm case study from which prevalence of MRONJ could not be assessed.20, 21, 33 A total of 12 studies were included in the qualitative and quantitative syntheses.34-45 Nine studies presented sequential bisphosphonates therapy with pamidronate followed by zoledronate.34-40, 44, 45 Amongst these studies, 3 of them also included sequential therapy with Ibandronate and Zoledronate with reported cases of ONJ.36, 44, 45 Three studies included sequential therapy with various types of bisphosphonates followed by denosumab.41-43

Study characteristics

Tables 1-2 present detailed study characteristics, patient characteristics, and outcome measures including: (a) study design, (b) total study sample size, (c) study duration, (d) study arms, including type of anti-resorptive drug, sequence of therapy, dose, route and frequency of drug administration, (e) number of ONJ cases, location of ONJ lesion, Number of ONJ cases associated with dental extractions, and (f) sample characteristics in terms of gender, age, and type of malignancy. A total of 10,239 participants were studied in the included 12 studies, while sample size of individual studies ranged from 90 to 3994.

Studies on sequential bisphosphonate therapy (n=9):

Five retrospective and 4 prospective cohort studies were included.34-40, 44, 45 No clinical trials were identified. Each of these 9 studies had three study arms for ONJ incidence/prevalence associated with exposure to: (a) pamidronate only, (b) zoledronate only, and (c) sequential therapy with pamidronate and zoledronate. In the sequential pamidronate-zoledronate therapy arm sample sizes ranged from 11 to 526, while sample sizes in pamidronate only arm ranged from 6 to 2288 and in the zoledronate group ranged from 34 to 1180. Five studies reported standardized dose, frequency and route of drug administration.35-37, 40, 44 Pamidronate was consistently administered at a dose of 90mg, but duration of infusion also ranged from 1 hour to 4 hours. Zoledronate dose was 4mg over a 15 min infusion period in all 5 studies. Frequency of administration varied for both drugs from every 3-4 weeks to 4-6 weeks.

Three prospective cohort studies of the 9 included studies had additional study arms for sequential therapy with ibandronate and zoledronate,36, 44, 45 and 2 of these 3 also had a study arm for exposure to ibandronate alone.44, 45 Smaller sample sizes were noted for ibandronate alone (range 2-5) and sequential ibandronate-zoledronate (range 2-15). One of the 3 studies reported that Ibandronate was administered at a dose of 4mg over a 2 hour infusion period every 4-6 weeks.44

Additionally, 1 included study had study arms for sequential therapy with: (a) pamidronate-ibandronate, with 1 ONJ case of 2 total cases, and (b) zoledronate-ibandronate-pamidronate, with 0 ONJ cases of 1 total case.36 Another study reported 3 additional sequential study arms: (a) pamidronate-ibandronate therapy with 0/1 ONJ cases, (b) chlondronate-zoledronate with 0/1 ONJ cases, and (c) residronate-zoledronate with 0/1 ONJ cases.45 These study arms were not included in quantitative synthesis due to small number of studies and sample sizes.

Four of the 9 studies only included individuals with multiple myeloma,34, 35, 37, 45 while 1 study included only females with breast cancer.40 The remaining 4 studies included a combination of study participants with multiple myeloma (n=4 studies), breast cancer (n=4), prostate cancer (n=4), lung cancer (n=2), and other malignancies (n=2) including renal cell, rectal, nasopharynx, urethral, bronchial, lymph node, papillary, and metastatic carcinomas of unknown origin.

Studies on sequential bisphosphonate and denosumab therapy (n=3):

Three studies on sequential bisphosphonate-denosumab therapy that allowed estimation of ONJ prevalence were included.41-43 Two out of the 3 were retrospective cohort studies and included study arms for: (a) bisphosphonate only, (b) and sequential bisphosphonate-denosumab therapy.42, 43 Of these, one study also included a denosumab only study arm.43

One study included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis41 was a prospective open label extension phase of 2 randomized double-blind clinical trials reported elsewhere.46, 47 Only the open label extension phase included a sequential bisphosphonate-denosumab study arm, whereas the randomized studies compared bisphosphonates and denosumab. Published reports of the randomized were retrieved and utilized for extraction of raw data for the bisphosphonate and denosuman only study arms, as the open label extension phase study reported only adjusted data from the randomized trials.46, 47

The sample size ranged from 36 to 452 participants in the sequential bisphosphonate-denosumab therapy arm, 125 to 1958 in the bisphosphonate only group, and 240 to 1963 in the denosumab only group. The bisphosphonate used in 1 retrospective study and the prospective trial was zoledronate, which was administered at a dose of 4mg over a 15 min infusion period every 4 weeks in the clinical trial41 and every 3-4 weeks in the retrospective study.42 In the second retrospective study several bisphosphonates were administered for which individual exposure and outcomes details could not be retrieved; these included: (a) ibandronate only, (b) sequential ibandronate-zoledronate, (c) sequential ibandronate-pamidronate, (d) zoledronate only, (e) sequential zoledronate-pamidronate.

The study population in all 3 studies included a combination of metastatic malignancies including lung, breast, prostate, kidney, colorectal, bladder, melanoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, sarcoma, renal cell, and other malignancies.41-43

Quality assessment

Table 6 and Figure 8 present the methodological and reporting quality assessment based on the modified MASCC/ISOO quality grading strategy for incidence and outcomes evaluation;23 maximum permissible quality points was 6. None of the included studies reported having an examiner blinded to the ARD exposure. One study scored 0 points across all domains,34 while 1 study scored 5 out of 6.41 Nearly 33% studies had a total score of 3,36, 37, 42, 45 while 50% studies scored 1 to 2 quality points.35, 38-40, 43, 44

Only 2 studies included a multicenter, representative sample of the underlying population, while 9 studies included consecutive patients from a single center. Only 2 studies used a standardized validated criteria for ONJ diagnosis; one study used CTCAE grading41 while the other used the AAOMS criteria.42 Four studies did not describe any criteria or methods used for diagnosis of ONJ while the other 6 studies included study-specific criteria commonly consisting of signs and symptoms, clinical and radiographic examinations. Four studies also reported that a biopsy of the bony lesion was performed to exclude metastatic disease when necessary.35, 36, 44, 45 One of these 6 studies included culture of the lesion for microbial analysis.37 Only 1 study with a study-specific ONJ diagnostic criteria defined a time frame (3 months) for persistence of exposed bone to be diagnosed as ONJ.39

Summary of results

Prevalence of MRONJ:

Figures 2-4 show forest plots for prevalence of MRONJ with individual effect estimates, pooled prevalence and weights assigned to each included study. Five retrospective cohort studies34, 37-40 revealed weighted MRONJ prevalence ranging from 2 to 52% for sequential therapy with pamidronate and zoledronate, 0 to 18% for pamidronate only, and 1 to 13% for zoledronate only. Four prospective cohort studies35, 36, 44, 45 also reported a wide variance in weighted prevalence ranging from 9 to 36% for sequential pamidronate and zoledronate therapy, 1 to 50% for pamidronate only, and 7 to 25% for zoledronate only (Figure 2).

Weighted prevalence for sequential Ibandronate and zoledronate therapy ranged from 7 to 50% reported by three prospective cohort studies.36, 44, 45 In the same studies, the weighted prevalence for zoledronate only ranged from 7 to 25% (Figure 3). Only two studies included a group for ibandronate only and revealed 0 MRONJ cases; these were not included in the meta-analysis.44, 45

Weighted MRONJ prevalence for sequential bisphosphonate-denosumab therapy ranged from 15 to 22% for two retrospective cohort studies42, 43 and a much lower 6% for one large prospective study41 that included two phase III trials and an open label extension phase. Weighted prevalence for bisphosphonate administration only was 7% for both retrospective studies and 1% for the prospective study. Denosumab only group was included in one retrospective study43 with a weighted prevalence of 10% and the prospective study41 with a prevalence of 4% (Figure 4).

Unweighted prevalence of MRONJ by type of malignancy, which revealed 8% prevalence amongst patients with multiple myeloma, 3.3% in breast cancer group, 2.9% in prostate cancer group and 0.7% for all other malignancies combined (Table 5).

Time-to-ONJ event:

Due to heterogeneity of data and summary measures reported in included full texts, meta-analysis of the exposure time-to-MRONJ could not be conducted. Table 3 reports median and range for time-to-ONJ reported in included studies. For sequential pamidronte-zoledronate therapy the time-to-ONJ ranged from 9.4 months44 to 127 months37 and for sequential ibandronate-zoledronate therapy it ranged from 7.5 months44 to 89 months.36

Loyson et al. (2018) reported a median time-to-ONJ of 25 months for patient transitioning from bisphosphonates to denosumab. The study also reported that in a matched control group analysis, ONJ occurred more rapidly in the sequential bisphosphonates-denosumab group than in the bisphosphonate only group, with correction for median antiresorptive drug exposure. Majority of the ONJ cases in the sequential bisphosphonates-denosumab group occurred during the first year (65%) of the drug transition, and was much lower in the second (18%) and third year (12%). Furthermore, this study reported that the incidence of ONJ was not significantly higher with a longer exposure to bisphosphonates prior to transitioning to denosumab.43

Sample characteristics and risk factors for MRONJ:

Table 4 reports summaries from individual studies on sample characteristics. A total of 395 ONJ cases were reported in the 12 included studies, irrespective of the drug exposure. Eleven studies reported gender distribution of ONJ but no significant differences were found between males and females; of these studies, 207 out of 360 cases (57.5%) occurred in females.34-37, 39-45 The median age of study participants who were diagnosed with MRONJ was reported in 8 studies and ranged from 60 to 65; within these individual studies no significant difference was noted in the median age of non-ONJ cases.34-37, 39, 42, 44, 45

Location of the ONJ lesion was reported in 8 included studies, and affected the mandible in 58.4% cases, the maxilla in 36.2% and the mandible and maxilla in 5.4% cases.34-39, 44, 45 A total of 54% ONJ cases were associated with a dental extraction.

Several studies evaluated the risks associated with concurrent therapy. Two studies reported that concomitant use of angiogenesis inhibitors was found to be significantly associated with risk of MRONJ.38, 42 However, four studies reported no significant correlation between thalidomide treatment and incidence of MRONJ for multiple myeloma patients.34, 36, 37, 39, 44 One study38 reported a significant association between incidence/prevalence of MRONJ and use of corticosteroids whereas three studies did not report a significant coorelation.38, 39, 42 Estilo et al. (2008) also reported significant association with 5-Fluorouracil, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and methotrexate.38 Hoff et al. (2008) did not find a significant risk for MRONJ associated with administration of anthracyclines, tamoxifen, taxanes, trastuzumab, aromatase inhibitors, or melphalan.39 Furthermore, Boonyapakorn et al. (2008) did not find a significant risk resulting from concomitant conventional chemotherapy, or stem cell transplantation.36

Synthesis of results

Meta-analysis was possible for MRONJ with sequential pamidronate-zoledronate, ibandronate-zoledronate and bisphosphonate-denosumab therapy. In addition, meta-analysis was carried out for pamidronate only, zoledronate only, denosumab only and bisphosphonate only groups.

Our meta-analysis revealed significant heterogeneity among included studies for sequential pamidronate-zoledronate therapy (I2= 91.28%, p=0.00). Pooled weighted prevalence of MRONJ was 19% (95% CI: 10-27%; Figure 2a) using a random effects model. In contrast, pooled weighted prevalence of pamidronate only was 1% (95% CI: 0-3%; I2= 46.44%, p=0.06; Figure 2b) and zoledronate only was 7% (95% CI: 3-10%; I2= 83.40%, p=0.00; Figure 2c).

Included studies for sequential ibandronate-zoledronate therapy had low heterogeneity (I2= 0.89%, p=0.36) and revealed a pooled weighted MRONJ prevalence of 10% (95% CI: 3-22%; Figure 3a). In the same studies, weighted pooled prevalence for zoledronate only was 11% (95% CI: 3-19%; Figure 3b) but had high heterogeneity of data (I2= 77.82%, p=0.01).

Our meta-analysis also revealed significant heterogeneity among included studies for sequential bisphosphonate-denosumab therapy (I2= 91.28%, p=0.00). Pooled weighted prevalence of MRONJ was 13% (95% CI: 3-22%; Figure 4a) using a random effects model. Pooled weighted prevalence of bisphosphonates only was 5% (95% CI: 0-9%; Figure 4b) and denosumab only was 4% (95% CI: 3-5%; Figure 4c).

Discussion

This is the first systematic assessment of the effect of sequentially administered antiresorptive drugs on the prevalence of medication related osteonecrosis of the jaw. Across all included studies, we noted an increased prevalence of MRONJ with sequential pamidronate-zoledronate therapy when compared to only pamidronate or only zoledronate drug administration. Similarly, we noted a higher prevalence of MRONJ in patients who were transitioned from bisphosphonates to denosumab, compared to patients who only received either bisphosphonates or denosumab. We also noted a trend for earlier MRONJ development with sequential bisphonate-denosumab therapy group compared to bisphosphonate only group, with previous similar median exposure to bisphosphonates. However, these results were elaborated only by one study.43 Furthermore, we also noted a higher unweighted prevalence of MRONJ amongst patients with multiple myeloma; this may be attributed to greater cumulative doses or longer period of antiresorptive therapy administered when compared to patients with bone metastasis from other malignancies.39

Meta-analysis was deemed possible even though study designs, sample size and follow-up data varied considerably. However, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution. Due to the various study designs, specifically retrospective and prospective cohorts, included in the analysis of prevalence, a cause-effect relationship between sequential therapy and increased risk for MRONJ cannot be established. Furthermore, in the absence of patient level data on exposure-to-event, cumulative incidence could not be calculated. The overall evidence included in this systematic review is undermined by lack of representativeness and bias in MRONJ measurement due to misclassification and examiner bias with a major limitation being the lack of employing/reporting standardized criteria used for diagnosis of MRONJ.

Findings from the present systematic review suggest an increased prevalence of MRONJ with sequential antiresorptive drugs when compared to a single antiresorptive drug reported within the included studies. Hence, the cumulative effect of these drugs administered sequentially should be further investigated in future studies.
