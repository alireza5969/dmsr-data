The Role of Minimally Invasive Surgery in the Care of Women with Ovarian Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

Abstract

Objective:

To synthesize evidence from studies investigating survival outcomes for patients with ovarian cancer undergoing minimally invasive surgery (traditional or robotic laparoscopy) compared with those for patients with ovarian cancer undergoing laparotomy.

Data Sources:

We searched Ovid MEDLINE and Embase (from inception to December 2019).

Methods of Study Selection:

Observational cohort studies and randomized controlled trials that compared risk of recurrence or death between women undergoing minimally invasive and open procedures for staging (10), interval cytoreduction (4), secondary cytoreduction (2), and evaluation of resectability (1) were included.

Tabulation, Integration, and Results:

Data on the number of participants, number of deaths and recurrences, and results of analyses of overall or progression-free survival were abstracted for all studies. A random-effects meta-analysis was used to pool the results of studies comparing minimally invasive staging and open staging. The surgical approach (minimally invasive versus open) was not significantly associated with hazard of death or recurrence (pooled hazard ratio 0.92; 95% confidence interval, 0.61–1.38) or all-cause mortality (pooled hazard ratio 0.96; 95% confidence interval, 0.49–1.89). One randomized trial demonstrated that diagnostic laparoscopy could triage patients to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and avoid suboptimal primary surgery, without affecting recurrence-free or overall survival. Most studies included in this review were observational and at high risk for bias, and few studies accounted for potential confounding.

Conclusion:

Although existing studies do not demonstrate deleterious survival effects associated with minimally invasive surgery for ovarian cancer, these data must be viewed with caution given the significant methodologic shortcomings in the existing literature.

The role of minimally invasive surgery in gynecologic oncology has expanded in the last several decades and has found broad use in the treatment of endometrial, cervical, and ovarian cancer [1,2]. On the basis of randomized evidence from minimally invasive staging of endometrial cancer [3], gynecologic oncologists increasingly accepted the notion that the surgical approach, whether open or laparoscopic, ought not to be linked to the risk of cancer recurrence or death in other cancer sites [4,5]. This belief has facilitated the acceptance of minimally invasive surgery across many clinical settings in gynecologic oncology [6-8].

The Laparoscopic Approach to Cervical Cancer trial found that randomization to minimally invasive radical hysterectomy led to an increased risk of recurrence and death compared with open radical hysterectomy among patients with early-stage cervical cancer [9], upending the notion that surgical approach does not affect survival outcomes in gynecologic cancers [10,11]. The demonstration of harm related to minimally invasive cancer surgery in a randomized trial is cause for a careful re-evaluation of the evidence supporting the use of these approaches across surgical oncology.

The goal of this systematic review was to synthesize what is known about the associations between minimally invasive surgery and oncologic outcomes among women undergoing ovarian cancer treatment. We queried the literature to identify studies that compare progression-free or overall survival between patients with ovarian cancer who received minimally invasive staging, interval cytoreduction, secondary cytoreduction, or evaluation of upfront resectability and patients who underwent an open approach.

Methods

Literature Search

We searched the Ovid MEDLINE and Embase (inception to December 2019) databases. The searches were not limited by date or study type but were limited to articles in English. The reference lists of the included articles were searched manually. The complete search string is shown in Box 1. Our findings are reported in accordance with the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines [12], and the protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number: CRD42020191183).

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Abstract and title review were performed by A.M. and A.K. to identify studies that compared the risk of death or recurrence between patients with ovarian cancer who underwent minimally invasive surgery (traditional or robot-assisted laparoscopy) and those who underwent laparotomy. Observational cohort studies and randomized controlled trials were included if they met the following criteria: (1) enrolled adult patients with a primary ovarian cancer who were treated with minimally invasive surgery; (2) compared the risk of recurrence or death between women undergoing minimally invasive surgery and those undergoing open procedures using survival analyses; and (3) included patients who underwent surgery for staging, interval cytoreduction after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, secondary cytoreduction of oligometastatic recurrence, or evaluation of the feasibility of optimal cytoreduction. We excluded conference abstracts and proceedings, duplicate reports, and comparisons of minimally invasive surgery and open surgery for other indications such as primary cytoreductive surgery.

For the included studies, the number of participants and events (deaths or recurrences) were extracted for minimally invasive and open surgery groups. When available, adjusted or crude hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were abstracted for progression-free and overall survival. When an HR was not reported, we attempted to estimate it on the basis of available information [13]. In addition, we abstracted the median follow-up time (in each study group or overall) and noted whether any confounder adjustment was performed for all studies. Primary data extraction was performed by A.K. and validated by A.M.

Statistical Analysis

When 5 or more studies were identified in 1 of the prespecified indications for which HRs were reported or could be calculated [13], we pooled the HRs using a random effects model to allow for between-studies variability. We assessed heterogeneity using the I2 statistic, and values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered low, moderate, and high, respectively [14].

Because many studies did not control for potential confounders, we performed a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the potential for unmeasured confounders to influence the findings of the meta-analyses. Using the E-value approach described by VanderWeele and Ding [15,16], we calculated the imbalance in unmeasured factors, and its strength of association with recurrence or death, required to shift the upper limit of the 95% CI of the pooled HR estimates to 2.0 on the basis of the assumption that the inability to rule out a doubling of the hazard represented insufficient evidence of safety.

Meta-analysis was performed using the metan package in Stata/MP version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). E-value calculations used the E-value calculator available at https://mmathur.shinyapps.io/evalue/ [16]. All reported statistical tests were 2-sided, with a significance level of .05. This study was exempt from informed consent and institutional review board approval because it does not constitute human research.

Results

Study Selection

We identified 658 records for title and abstract review, of which 68 qualified for full-text review. Fifty one articles were excluded after review for the following reasons: abstract only (33), no control arm (10), other indication (5), no survival analysis (1), and inclusion of 3 separate cohorts (open vs laparoscopic vs robotic) (2). A total of 17 studies were ultimately included (Fig. 1) and were then categorized as staging (10), interval cytoreduction (4), secondary cytoreduction (2), and evaluation of resectability (1).

Staging Surgery

Ten studies of ovarian cancer staging and their main characteristics are tabulated in Table 1. These studies included 1509 patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery and 1787 patents who underwent laparotomy [17-26]. Among the 8 studies that reported the number of recurrences, there were a total of 52 recurrences in 363 patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery (14%) and 118 recurrences among 641 patients who underwent open surgery (18%). The number of deaths was reported in 6 of the minimally invasive cohorts (81 deaths in 1256 patients [6.4%]) and in 5 of the laparotomy cohorts (90 deaths in 1224 patients [7.4%]).

The results of the meta-analyses of studies reporting progression-free survival and overall survival are shown in Fig. 2. In the studies that evaluated progression-free survival, minimally invasive staging surgery was not associated with hazard of death or recurrence compared with open staging (pooled HR 0.92; 95% CI, 0.61–1.38), with low heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 25.0%). Similarly, among the studies that reported overall survival, there was no association between minimally invasive surgery and all-cause mortality (pooled HR 0.96; 95% CI, 0.49–1.89), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 55.2%).

Only 2 of the 10 included studies adjusted for confounding variables. Melamed et al [23] adjusted for patient and tumor characteristics as well as year of diagnosis and treating facility type, and Wu et al [26] adjusted for tumor characteristics and receipt of chemotherapy.

In a sensitivity analysis evaluating whether the apparent absence of associations between the surgical approach and progression-free survival could be the result of confounding, we found that adjusting for a residual confounder that was associated with both open surgery and risk of death or recurrence by at least 1.9-fold each could shift the 95% CI for the pooled HR to include 2.0. For overall survival, we found that adjusting for an unmeasured confounder that was associated with both risk of death and open surgery by a factor of at least 1.3 could shift the 95% CI for the pooled HR to include 2.0.

Interval Cytoreduction

We identified 4 studies that compared survival between women who underwent minimally invasive interval cytoreductive surgery and those who underwent open interval cytoreductive surgery after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which included a total of 543 subjects in the minimally invasive groups and 2801 patients in the laparotomy cohorts (Table 2) [27-30]. The number of patients who progressed was not reported in any of the studies. In 2 studies [28,29] that reported the number of observed deaths, there were 185 deaths among 460 patients who underwent minimally invasive interval cytoreductive surgery (40%) and 1045 deaths among 2801 patients who underwent open surgery (40%). In a single-institution study of 21 patients, Favero et al [29] found a nonsignificant decrease in cancer-specific survival and a nonsignificant reduction in the chemotherapy-free interval among women undergoing minimally invasive cytoreduction. In another single-institution study, Gueli Alletti et al [30] found that although patients who underwent minimally invasive cytoreductive surgery had significantly better median progression-free survival than those who underwent open surgery (18 months vs 12 months), this difference was not significant in multivariable analysis. In a study of more than 3000 patients in the National Cancer Database, Melamed et al [28] found no association between surgical approach and all-cause mortality (HR 1.09; 95% CI, 0.93–1.28) among patients who underwent interval cytoreductive surgery for advanced ovarian cancer. Brown et al [27] compared both progression-free and overall survival between patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery and open interval cytoreductive surgery at a single institution and found no statistically significant differences, with a median progression-free survival of 27 months and 29 months and median overall survival of 37 months and 35 months between the patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery and those who underwent open surgery, respectively [27]. A meta-analysis was not performed for this subset, given the limited number of studies included and the limitations in extracting HRs in a number of these studies.

We identified important limitations among all included studies. Three of the 4 studies were single-institution cohort studies that were too small to estimate clinically important effect sizes precisely. Furthermore, only 2 studies adjusted for any confounding, despite evidence of substantial difference in likely confounders such as preoperative cancer antigen–125 levels and surgical complexity.

Secondary Cytoreduction

We identified 2 retrospective cohort studies comparing outcomes after minimally invasive secondary cytoreduction and open secondary cytoreduction, which included 50 patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery and 81 who underwent laparotomy (Table 3) [31,32]. Neither study reported the number of deaths or recurrences. In a study of 79 women who underwent secondary cytoreduction at Samsung Medical Center in South Korea, Paik et al [31] found no significant differences between the laparoscopy and laparotomy groups with respect to patient and tumor characteristics. The patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery had shorter operative times, shorter admissions, and less blood loss than those who underwent open surgery. The survival outcomes did not differ significantly, with 5-year postsurgical progression-free survival rates of 37% in the minimally invasive group and 28% in the open group and 5-year overall survival rates of 64% in the minimally invasive group and 58% in the open group. In another small single-institution study—Magrina et al [32]—no statistically significant differences were observed in operating time, complications, complete debulking, and survival between women who underwent minimally invasive secondary cytoreduction and those who underwent open secondary cytoreduction [32]. Importantly, neither study accounted for potential confounders such as the extent and distribution of recurrent disease or treatment-free interval before recurrence.

Evaluation of Resectability

We identified I randomized trial that evaluated whether laparoscopic evaluation of resectability affects survival in patients with ovarian cancer [33]. Rutten et al [33] randomized 201 women with suspected advanced-stage ovarian cancer who qualified for primary cytoreductive surgery to either diagnostic laparoscopy or primary surgery. The investigators found a significant difference in the proportion of patients who underwent primary cytoreduction: among 102 patients randomized to diagnostic laparoscopy, 63 (62%) went on to primary cytoreductive surgery, whereas among 99 patients assigned to undergo immediate laparotomy, 93 (94%) underwent primary cytoreductive surgery. The patients randomized to diagnostic laparoscopy experienced fewer futile laparotomies (10%) than those who underwent immediate laparotomy (39%). However, neither median progression-free survival (13.7 months vs 15.2 months; HR 1.11; 95% CI, 0.99–1.25) nor median overall survival (44.4 months vs 46.3 months; HR 1.33; 95% CI, 0.89–1.98) differed between the patients randomized to diagnostic laparoscopy and those randomized to immediate laparotomy.

Discussion

In this systematic review, we synthesized the available evidence regarding the oncologic impact of minimally invasive surgery in the management of ovarian cancer. Specifically, we reviewed studies that compare minimally invasive surgery and open surgery for staging surgery, interval cytoreduction, secondary cytoreduction, and evaluation of resectability for suspected advanced ovarian cancer. Meta-analysis was performed for the studies of surgical staging only, owing to the small number of studies identified for other indications.

Overall, most of the studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis were observational and at high risk for bias. Few studies accounted for potential confounding, and those that did were unlikely to have included all important confounders. Furthermore, most studies that concluded that survival outcomes did not differ between minimally invasive surgery and open surgery were under-powered to detect clinically important effects.

Our pooled analysis of studies of minimally invasive staging surgery found no association between the surgical approach and overall or progression-free survival. This finding is consistent with randomized data from the endometrial cancer literature [34] but differs from a recent randomized trial in cervical cancer [9]. Importantly, all studies included in this meta-analysis were retrospective cohort studies. The absence of random allocation means that the results of the meta-analysis must be viewed with caution owing to selection bias introduced by surgical decision-making. In addition, confidence regarding the safety of minimally invasive staging surgery for ovarian cancer staging is limited by the failure of most studies to account for confounding. This limitation is highlighted by our sensitivity analyses that suggest that adjustment for plausible imbalances in important prognostic factors, such as tumor grade or substage [35], could shift the results of the meta-analysis such that we cannot exclude a doubling of the hazard of recurrence or death owing to minimally invasive surgery. Furthermore, a recent study that found increased rates of tumor rupture among women who underwent minimally invasive staging for ovarian cancer raises additional concerns about the oncologic safety of this approach to staging [36].

Despite the growing acceptance of interval cytoreductive surgery for ovarian cancer [28,37], there are few studies that support the oncologic safety in this setting. The largest study investigating survival outcomes of minimally invasive interval cytoreduction found no difference in 3-year survival rates between the minimally invasive and open surgery cohorts, even after correcting for patient comorbidities and cancer substage [28]. However, observational studies such as these must be interpreted cautiously because it is likely that the patients selected to undergo laparoscopic interval cytoreduction have responded more robustly to chemotherapy than those who undergo laparotomy—a significant potential confounder that is not adequately addressed in any of the reviewed studies. The Laparoscopic Cytoreduction after Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy (LANCE) trial, an ongoing randomized study, may provide clarity regarding the appropriateness of minimally invasive surgery for this indication [38].

Only 2 small observational studies compared survival outcomes between women who underwent minimally invasive cytoreductive surgery and those who underwent open cytoreductive surgery for recurrent ovarian cancer [32,33]. Although neither study found evidence of inferior survival, these studies lack statistical power and are likely to be confounded by factors such as the distribution and burden of recurrent disease and the treatment-free interval before recurrence. It is difficult to draw firm conclusions from these data.

Finally, we identified I small randomized trial that evaluated the role of diagnostic laparoscopy before primary cytoreductive surgery. Although this study demonstrated that diagnostic laparoscopy can successfully triage patients to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and avoid suboptimal primary surgery, it did not find an impact on recurrence-free or overall survival.

In conclusion, existing studies do not demonstrate any of the deleterious survival effects associated with minimally invasive surgery for ovarian cancer. However, except for a randomized trial assessing the impact of diagnostic laparoscopy before open primary cytoreductive surgery, these data must be viewed with some skepticism in light of significant methodologic shortcomings. Additional high-quality randomized trials are needed to elucidate the role of minimally invasive surgery for staging and cytoreduction.
