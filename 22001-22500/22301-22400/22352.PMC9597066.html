A Systematic Review of the Literature Regarding the Relationship Between Oxidative Stress and Electroconvulsive Therapy

[http://orcid.org/0000-0002-8514-0576] Murat İlhan Atagün[1][{ label needed for corresp[@id='c1-ap-23-2-47'] }]

Abstract

Objective

Brain’s aerobic energy metabolism, abundance of the fatty acids and unsaturated lipids, generation of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) by hormones, physiological roles of transition metals (i.e., iron and copper), and free radicals in the nervous system may cause inclination to oxidative stress in psychiatric disorders. Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) may cause oxidative stress by the electrical field or by the induced seizure. It was aimed to review the literature in terms of the influence of ECT on levels of oxidant and antioxidant compounds.

Methods

The literature search was performed with the keywords that were oxidative stress or "DNA damage" or "RNA damage" or "lipid peroxidase" or "superoxide dismutase" or "catalase" or "glutathione" or "nitrite" or "nitric oxide" and "electroconvulsive therapy" or "electroconvulsive shock" or "electroconvulsive seizure". Twenty of 1480 records were included.

Results

Eleven studies were performed in human subjects, whereas 9 studies were performed in rats. Human studies are conducted with serum, plasma, or urine samples; rat studies include brain tissues from various sites. In rats, four independent studies showed increased levels of lipid oxidation markers, and four independent studies reported increased levels of oxidative stress markers in brain samples. In human studies, studies were performed with circulating blood samples and the results were more inconsistent.

Conclusion

Although some markers like superoxide dismutase or thioredoxin imply that ECT may increase the balance for oxidative stress, this notion is not supported by other markers of ECT. The current literature does not clearly suggest that the ECT is associated with oxidative stress in psychiatric disorders. Further studies with similar methods should be performed in big samples.

MAIN POINTS

  • Inflammatory activation and oxidative stress are the leading common chemical risk factors of underlying metabolic abnormalities of the abovementioned clinical outcomes in psychiatric disorders.

  • The dense aerobic energy metabolism, abundancy of the fatty acids and unsaturated lipids, generation of ROS by hormones, physiological roles of transition metals (i.e., iron and copper), and free radicals may cause inclination to damage by oxidative stress in the central nervous system.

  • Electrical currents, epileptic seizures and other procedures of the electroconvulsive therapy may interfere with the electrical balance between oxidant and antioxidant molecules.

  • It has been consistently shown in experimental models that following electroconvulsive seizures, oxidative stress markers increase in the rat brain.

  • In the circulating blood, oxidative stress markers do not change following electroconvulsive therapy in human studies.

  • Although more studies are needed, it can be speculated that acute and transient changes in oxidative balance do not cause any harm to the brain.

Introduction

Psychiatric disorders are leading causes of disability,1 with high rates of morbidity and premature death.2,3 For example, the risk of oncologic diseases4 or cardiovascular diseases5 increase significantly, even the metabolic risk factors were controlled for. The medical comorbidities may emerge both directly, through biological pathways, and indirectly, through risky health behaviors. Furthermore, alteration in brain volumes, disruption of neuroplasticity, and cell survival may highlight potential abnormalities in cellular longevity and resilience in mood disorders6 and schizophrenia.7 Inflammatory activation and oxidative stress are the leading common chemical risk factors of underlying metabolic abnormalities of the abovementioned clinical outcomes in psychiatric disorders.

Active oxygen species and available antioxidant buffering efficiency are on a balance in physiological conditions. Increased levels of the oxidant compound excess the capacity of the antioxidant systems, and the redox disequilibrium on behalf of reactive oxygen species (ROS) may damage all cellular components including lipids, proteins, nucleic acid, or carbohydrates by altering their chemical structure and function.8 Several antioxidant defense systems include non-enzymatic molecules (vitamins A, C, and E, glutathione, and dietary supplied antioxidants) as well as enzymatic scavengers of ROS, with superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPX) are the most common defense systems.9 Major proportion of the superoxide radical (O2 •−) is generated at the level of the mitochondrial electron transport chain and can be easily converted to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) spontaneously or using SOD. On the other hand, transition metal ions (e.g., iron and copper), H2O2 can generate via Fenton reaction the highly reactive hydroxyl radical (HO•). Reactive species may also be enzymatically produced by xanthine oxidase, uncoupled nitric oxide synthases, and NADPH oxidase.

Oxidative stress is pronounced in psychiatric disorders due to a combination of various characteristics of the nervous system. The dense aerobic energy metabolism, abundancy of the fatty acids and unsaturated lipids, generation of ROS by hormones, physiological roles of transition metals (i.e., iron and copper), and free radicals may cause inclination to damage by oxidative stress in the central nervous system.7 On the other hand, pathophysiological mechanisms, disorganized lifestyle, nutritional deficiencies, metabolic and endocrine alterations, and long-term consequences of the medications may enhance the risk for oxidative stress in psychiatric disorders.10 Consistently, previous studies consistently detected severe oxidative stress in psychiatric disorders and further showed that oxidative stress causes damage to proteins, lipids, and nucleic acid material in the brain, induces telomere shortening, apoptosis, and cell death in the chronic terms of schizophrenia7, bipolar disorder11, and major depressive disorder.12 Clinical (i.e., episodes, disease severity), metabolic and pharmacological factors that may influence the oxidative status.13,14

Intermediate metabolites of the catecholaminergic and dopaminergic catabolic cascades are prone to produce oxidative compounds and irregularities or excess levels of the neurotransmitters may cause oxidative stress in the brain.15 Based on this information, intriguing questions about the effects modulators of the neurotransmitters arise. The literature regarding the effects of antipsychotics on the levels of oxidative compounds is not conclusive.16 However, it was reported that lithium decreased levels of oxidant compounds and oxidized lipid levels in patients with bipolar disorder and the levels of oxidized lipids were associated with clinical response to lithium.17,18 On the other hand, antidepressants use is clearly associated with depleted levels of oxidative markers in patients with major depressive disorder19,20. However, it is still not clear yet, whether the changes in oxidative stress markers are related to remission in patients or the chemical effects of the medications on neurotransmitter metabolism.

The electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) has been used in the treatment of major psychiatric disorders for more than 80 years. The ECT is considered to be a safe treatment modality, even in risky populations.21-23 In the last decades modified version of the ECT application which is under anesthesia conditions is being practiced. The ECT is based on electrical current stimulation (induction phase) which leads to a short seizure activity (seizure phase) ideally lasting between 20 and 50 seconds. The third and last phase is the recovery phase. The induction and seizure phases have the potential to induce chemical effects in the nervous system tissues.22 Electrical currents are based on the flow of electrons and theoretically, electrons may interact with molecules and produce oxidant compounds. Likewise, epilepsy models triggered by chemical agents (i.e., pilocarpine) or electrical stimulus showed similar trends in oxidative stress.24 Electrical stimuli triggered mitochondrial autophagy by inducing oxidative stress in cell cultures.25 It is not clear yet whether levels of oxidative compounds elevate in the patients treated with ECT. Furthermore, anesthetic agents may also influence the chemical balance between oxidant and antioxidant systems. Seizures may provoke oxidative stress that leads to disruption in neurotransmission, ion channel physiology, and DNA break in the hippocampus24 in rat models. Disruption of the neuronal physiology in the hippocampus may cause memory dysfunction. This notion suggests a possible relationship between ECT-related memory dysfunction and oxidative stress. It was aimed to perform a systematic literature review regarding the potential effects of the ECT on oxidative stress markers in patients treated with ECT.

Methods

A quantitative literature review was performed in PubMed database on July 30, 2021 according to the PRISM guidelines.26 The literature search was performed according to The keywords were oxidative stress or "DNA damage" or "RNA damage" or "lipid peroxidase" or "superoxide dismutase" or "catalase" or "glutathione" or "nitrite" or "nitric oxide" and "electroconvulsive therapy" or "electroconvulsive shock" or "electroconvulsive seizure". The English language was the only filter and no date limitation was applied. The literature search retrieved 1480 records. Total 1423 records were eliminated by title and abstract in the first assessment. Fifty-seven records remained in the second phase. Thirty-seven studies were eliminated in the second phase full-text review. Twenty studies remained after the process. Nine studies were preclinical laboratory studies and 11 studies were clinical studies in humans. For details of the review process, please see the flowchart in Figure 1.

Results

Human Studies

Glutathione peroxidase (GSH), SOD, and CAT are antioxidant enzymes that catalyze oxygen radicals and defend the tissues against oxidative compounds. Glutathione peroxidase and CAT levels were measured in 3 studies by independent groups27,28 and the ECT did not alter the levels of these enzymes in schizophrenia27 or BD28 (see Table 1). Superoxide dismutase levels were assessed by 3 different studies by 3 independent groups.28-30 Two studies consistently reported that SOD levels were decreased after ECT treatment in the patient groups. NO is a compound with an unpaired electron and XO generates reactive oxygen compounds. Virit et al30 examined NO and XO levels in patients with MDD before and after ECT. The authors reported that the level of NO and XO did not change after ECT. Kartalci et al27 examined NO levels in schizophrenia patients before and after ECT. Similarly, the authors did not observe any change in NO levels. Atagun et al31 examined NO and its nitrite and nitrate components in depressed patients (n = 23) treated with ECT. The authors reported that NO levels decrease after the 7th ECT session. The authors claim was that the decrease of the NO may contribute to the increased systemic systolic tension.

Total oxidant status (TOS) is an index measure that contains all oxidant molecules and total antioxidant status (TAS) is an index measure that contains all antioxidant measures cumulatively. Total antioxidant status and TOS are in balance normally using the antioxidant defense systems. Total antioxidant status and TOS levels were measured by 3 studies by independent groups.32-34 Kalelioglu et al32 have enrolled 68 patients with acute mania. Twenty-two of the patients had antipsychotic and ECT treatment, while 46 of the patients had only antipsychotic treatment. The groups did not show any difference in TAS and TOS levels. Sahin et al33 enrolled 23 patients with major depressive disorder and 22 healthy controls. Total antioxidant status levels were significantly lower before the treatment in the patient group increased significantly after the ECT treatment. Senyurt et al34 had a mixed sample consisting of major depressive disorder (n = 14), schizophrenia (n = 11), BD (n = 8), and HCs (n = 37). In the first assessment, TOS levels were higher, and TAS levels were lower in the patient group. After ECT treatment, TOS levels decreased and TAS levels increased in the patient group.

Thioredoxin is a protective protein against oxidative stress by neutralizing oxidative compounds. Patients with bipolar disorder in the manic episode (n = 68) were compared with healthy controls (n = 30) in terms of plasma thioredoxin levels, and thioredoxin levels were significantly lower in the patient group. After ECT treatment, thioredoxin levels in the mania group further reduced significantly in this study.35 Albumin is the most abundant protein and its last 5 amino acids of the chain might be modified by chemical stress including oxidative stress. Atagun et al36 evaluated the levels of ischemia modified albumin (IMA) in patients with depression (n = 23) and healthy individuals (n = 21). Ischemia modified albumin levels were significantly higher in the patient group in the first assessment. However, IMA levels did not differ in the patient group before and after ECT. Furthermore, the authors also evaluated thiol/disulfide balance in the same group. Sistine residues and sulfur groups are functional units of amino acids which are also called thiols. Thiols can be reduced into non-reactive disulfides after reacting with reactive oxygen compounds and disulfides can be converted to thiols and thiol-disulfide homeostasis is maintained. Thiol/disulfide levels did not differ between the groups in the first assessment and in the patient group before and after ECT treatment. Another recent study that examined the effects of rTMS on maintenance of the thiol/disulfide homeostasis in patients with treatment-resistant depression reported that thiol levels depleted significantly in the patient group.37 However, there was no relationship between the thiol levels and clinical improvement. Polyunsaturated fatty acids and lipid peroxidation result in malonaldehyde (MDA). Malonaldehyde levels were evaluated in 2 studies.27,30 Malonaldehyde levels were decreased after 9th session of ECT in patients with schizophrenia.27 However, no difference was observed in patients with depression.30

Nucleic acid materials are also prone to oxidative damage and DNA [8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2-deoxyguanosine (8-oxodG)] and RNA [8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanosine (8-oxoGuo)] oxidation markers can be identified in urine. Oxidative stress cause oxidation of the DNA and RNA in psychiatric disorders. A study evaluated the effects of ECT on oxidation markers of DNA and RNA.38 The study included 2 patient groups and a healthy control group (n = 28). One depression group was moderately depressed and medication-free (n = 26) and the other group was severely depressed and treated with ECT (n = 29). RNA oxidation marker (8-oxoGuo) was significantly higher in the severely depressed group and further increased after ECT treatment. However, there was no difference between the groups in terms of DNA oxidation marker (8-oxodG) level and no change was observed after ECT. Telomere length (TL) is believed to be a measure of biological aging, which progressively shortens with the increase of global neurochemical risk.39 Oxidative stress is frequently observed in depressive disorders and cumulative exposure to oxidative stress causes functional and structural brain abnormalities40 as well as telomere erosion and somatic morbidity41 in depressed individuals. A recent study has investigated a potential relationship between TL and cognitive side effects of ECT, by comparing severely depressed patients (n = 100) with healthy controls (n = 80).42 No difference was observed between the groups in terms of TL and TL was not associated with cognitive measures.

Experimental Studies

Human studies are performed in serum or plasma samples. However, experimental studies are performed in brain tissue directly. This is an important advantage for oxidative stress studies since systemic circulation can poorly reflect the changes that occur in the brain (see Table 2). Superoxide dismutase was assessed in 6 studies.43-48 Samples were collected from various tissues such as the frontal cortex, hippocampus, striatum, and cerebellum in these studies. One study found decreased43 and three studies found increased44,46,47 or no alteration45,48 levels of SOD. Catalase levels were evaluated in 3 studies44,45,48 and CAT levels were increased in a study.44 Whereas 2 studies did not report any change in CAT levels after treatment with ECS. Glutathione peroxidase levels were evaluated by 2 studies.43,47 Erakovic et al43 found increased levels of GSH, whereas Zahedi et al47 found decreased levels of GSH. NO was assessed in 3 studies and the studies reported increased levels of nitrite47 and nitrate levels48 and decreased levels of nitrite.31

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) and MDA are markers of lipid peroxidation. Five studies evaluated TBARS levels44-46,48,49, whereas MDA levels were assessed in 2 studies.48,49 Increased45,46,48, decreased44, or unaltered49 levels of lipid peroxidation markers have been detected in various studies. Protein carbonyls are protein peroxidation markers and were assessed in 3 studies44,45,49 and neither of the studies found any change in protein carbonyl levels before and after ECT treatment. DNA (8-oxodG) and RNA (8-oxoGuo) oxidation markers did not differ before and after ECS.50 The authors also detected that glucocorticoid receptor mRNA expression decreased. However, another study found that RNA oxidation marker significantly increased in another study.51

Discussion

An electroconvulsive therapy session has induction of the seizure, seizure, and post-ictal phases. In addition, anesthetic agents may also have the potential to influence oxidative metabolism. However, Propofol is the most preferred agent and is known to reduce oxidative stress.52,53 Prolonged epileptic seizures disturb the balance between oxidative metabolites and antioxidant systems by increasing cellular energy demand and hindering mitochondrial functions.54 The electrical stimulation that induces the seizure may also cause oxidative stress by biophysical effects (flow of the current through the tissue).25 The ECT is capable of inducing electrical dose-dependent neuroplasticity.55,56 Neurogenesis, angiogenesis, synaptogenesis, and gliogenesis might be stimulated by the electrical current.57,58 Different aspects of the application may complicate the picture and the global effect might be influenced by several factors. Electroconvulsive therapy-mediated changes in the brain could be related to the combined effects of seizure activity, cerebral blood flow, and electrical field strength. Cerebral blood flow increases following ECT administration probably due to increased demand.59,60 Therefore, the cumulative effect should be measured to determine the global outcomes of the ECT and the balance between oxidant and antioxidant compounds. The electrical field determines the initial region of the seizure, propagation, and termination processes may determine the outcomes of the ECT-related effects. The discussion regarding the components of the ECT has progressed and lately, the seizure component is claimed to be the unnecessary component of the ECT.61

The included studies measured oxidative stress with SOD, CAT, GSH, NO, XO, TAS, TOS, malonaldehyde, thioredoxine, IMA, and thiol/disulfide balance, 8-oxodG, 8-oxoGuo, and telomere length in patients treated with ECT. Human studies are conducted with serum, plasma, or urine samples. These samples can only reflect a major fluctuation, which cannot be counterbalanced by the systemic antioxidant resources. In human studies, significant differences were found in SOD levels in 2 different studies and a study reported a further decline of thioredoxin levels in manic patients after treatment with ECT. Moreover, the RNA oxidation marker was increased after the successful treatment of severely depressed individuals with ECT. However, telomere length did not differ before and after ECT treatment. Experimental studies are conducted in brain tissues. However, the results are inconsistent between studies.

Oxidative stress and inflammatory activation usually develop together with a self-sustaining loop, particularly in certain disease states and the co-existing entities worsen the disease progress.62 Under normal physiologic conditions, oxidative stress, and activation of the immune system are generally short-lived due to intrinsic negative feedback mechanisms, such as increased production of antioxidant compounds or of anti-inflammatory cytokines. Psychiatric disorders are characterized by oxidative stress and abnormal inflammatory states, which may disrupt feedback mechanisms. Likewise, both systems remain activated and may, indeed, form a positive self-sustaining feedback loop, or a “co-activation” state.63 Over time, such co-activation may lead to a higher risk of disease and to more serious disease.64 Electroconvulsive therapy causes alteration in the immune system65 and the variations in the immune system are associated with cognitive side effects of ECT.66 Oxidative stress may also be triggered by inflammatory activation in the brain.67,68

Electroconvulsive therapy may cause memory disturbances69,70 and it is proposed that neurotoxic materials in the hippocampus are associated with memory problems.66 The literature fails to approve this claim and more studies are needed for clarification. However, the transient dysfunction of the hippocampus might be explained by neurogenerative and neurochemical load. Unilateral ECT administration seems to be advantageous for memory side effects, compared to bilateral ECT application.71 This finding supports the notion that the hippocampus is vulnerable to the effects of ECT. Likewise, unilateral administration of the ECT causes volumetric changes in the stimulated hemisphere.55 These findings suggest that rather than the convulsive seizure, neuroplasticity effects of the ECT depend on electrical fields.

Conclusion

The current literature has several shortcomings and limitations. First, the variable nature of the oxidant and antioxidant compounds might be reflected differentially in psychiatric disorders, and therefore evaluating the effects of ECT might be confusing. Second, although markers of oxidant and antioxidant systems are similar, different designs of the studies may have caused confusing results. Third, the evaluation of serum or plasma does not necessarily reflect the state of brain tissue. This is particularly important since the main event occurs in the brain. Fourth, different components of the ECT may trigger different effects. Fifth, animal models of ECS might be poorly associated with the human ECT. Sixth, our literature review was limited to the PubMed database which may have lower the sensitivity of our scan.

The effects of ECT on oxidant and antioxidant molecules are evaluated in this review. Electroconvulsive therapy has anesthesia, electrical field, and seizure components which may all trigger oxidative stress. Methodological obstacles limit the studies to determine the changes. Studies reported an increase of oxidant compounds following electroconvulsive seizures in rats. Although some markers like SOD or thioredoxin imply that ECT may increase the balance for oxidative stress, this notion is not supported by other markers of ECT. It can be concluded that current literature does not clearly suggest that the ECT is associated with oxidative stress in psychiatric disorders. It is possible to speculate that the ECT may cause an increase of oxidant compounds, however, the buffering capacity of the tissue can neutralize the oxidant compounds. Further studies with similar methods are needed to clarify the effects of ECT on oxidative stress. Mechanism of the effect of neuromodulation therapies may enlighten our understanding of the pathogenesis of psychiatric disorders and developing novel treatments.

## Introduction

Psychiatric disorders are leading causes of disability,1 with high rates of morbidity and premature death.2,3 For example, the risk of oncologic diseases4 or cardiovascular diseases5 increase significantly, even the metabolic risk factors were controlled for. The medical comorbidities may emerge both directly, through biological pathways, and indirectly, through risky health behaviors. Furthermore, alteration in brain volumes, disruption of neuroplasticity, and cell survival may highlight potential abnormalities in cellular longevity and resilience in mood disorders6 and schizophrenia.7 Inflammatory activation and oxidative stress are the leading common chemical risk factors of underlying metabolic abnormalities of the abovementioned clinical outcomes in psychiatric disorders.

Active oxygen species and available antioxidant buffering efficiency are on a balance in physiological conditions. Increased levels of the oxidant compound excess the capacity of the antioxidant systems, and the redox disequilibrium on behalf of reactive oxygen species (ROS) may damage all cellular components including lipids, proteins, nucleic acid, or carbohydrates by altering their chemical structure and function.8 Several antioxidant defense systems include non-enzymatic molecules (vitamins A, C, and E, glutathione, and dietary supplied antioxidants) as well as enzymatic scavengers of ROS, with superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPX) are the most common defense systems.9 Major proportion of the superoxide radical (O2 •−) is generated at the level of the mitochondrial electron transport chain and can be easily converted to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) spontaneously or using SOD. On the other hand, transition metal ions (e.g., iron and copper), H2O2 can generate via Fenton reaction the highly reactive hydroxyl radical (HO•). Reactive species may also be enzymatically produced by xanthine oxidase, uncoupled nitric oxide synthases, and NADPH oxidase.

Oxidative stress is pronounced in psychiatric disorders due to a combination of various characteristics of the nervous system. The dense aerobic energy metabolism, abundancy of the fatty acids and unsaturated lipids, generation of ROS by hormones, physiological roles of transition metals (i.e., iron and copper), and free radicals may cause inclination to damage by oxidative stress in the central nervous system.7 On the other hand, pathophysiological mechanisms, disorganized lifestyle, nutritional deficiencies, metabolic and endocrine alterations, and long-term consequences of the medications may enhance the risk for oxidative stress in psychiatric disorders.10 Consistently, previous studies consistently detected severe oxidative stress in psychiatric disorders and further showed that oxidative stress causes damage to proteins, lipids, and nucleic acid material in the brain, induces telomere shortening, apoptosis, and cell death in the chronic terms of schizophrenia7, bipolar disorder11, and major depressive disorder.12 Clinical (i.e., episodes, disease severity), metabolic and pharmacological factors that may influence the oxidative status.13,14

Intermediate metabolites of the catecholaminergic and dopaminergic catabolic cascades are prone to produce oxidative compounds and irregularities or excess levels of the neurotransmitters may cause oxidative stress in the brain.15 Based on this information, intriguing questions about the effects modulators of the neurotransmitters arise. The literature regarding the effects of antipsychotics on the levels of oxidative compounds is not conclusive.16 However, it was reported that lithium decreased levels of oxidant compounds and oxidized lipid levels in patients with bipolar disorder and the levels of oxidized lipids were associated with clinical response to lithium.17,18 On the other hand, antidepressants use is clearly associated with depleted levels of oxidative markers in patients with major depressive disorder19,20. However, it is still not clear yet, whether the changes in oxidative stress markers are related to remission in patients or the chemical effects of the medications on neurotransmitter metabolism.

The electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) has been used in the treatment of major psychiatric disorders for more than 80 years. The ECT is considered to be a safe treatment modality, even in risky populations.21-23 In the last decades modified version of the ECT application which is under anesthesia conditions is being practiced. The ECT is based on electrical current stimulation (induction phase) which leads to a short seizure activity (seizure phase) ideally lasting between 20 and 50 seconds. The third and last phase is the recovery phase. The induction and seizure phases have the potential to induce chemical effects in the nervous system tissues.22 Electrical currents are based on the flow of electrons and theoretically, electrons may interact with molecules and produce oxidant compounds. Likewise, epilepsy models triggered by chemical agents (i.e., pilocarpine) or electrical stimulus showed similar trends in oxidative stress.24 Electrical stimuli triggered mitochondrial autophagy by inducing oxidative stress in cell cultures.25 It is not clear yet whether levels of oxidative compounds elevate in the patients treated with ECT. Furthermore, anesthetic agents may also influence the chemical balance between oxidant and antioxidant systems. Seizures may provoke oxidative stress that leads to disruption in neurotransmission, ion channel physiology, and DNA break in the hippocampus24 in rat models. Disruption of the neuronal physiology in the hippocampus may cause memory dysfunction. This notion suggests a possible relationship between ECT-related memory dysfunction and oxidative stress. It was aimed to perform a systematic literature review regarding the potential effects of the ECT on oxidative stress markers in patients treated with ECT.
## Methods

A quantitative literature review was performed in PubMed database on July 30, 2021 according to the PRISM guidelines.26 The literature search was performed according to The keywords were oxidative stress or "DNA damage" or "RNA damage" or "lipid peroxidase" or "superoxide dismutase" or "catalase" or "glutathione" or "nitrite" or "nitric oxide" and "electroconvulsive therapy" or "electroconvulsive shock" or "electroconvulsive seizure". The English language was the only filter and no date limitation was applied. The literature search retrieved 1480 records. Total 1423 records were eliminated by title and abstract in the first assessment. Fifty-seven records remained in the second phase. Thirty-seven studies were eliminated in the second phase full-text review. Twenty studies remained after the process. Nine studies were preclinical laboratory studies and 11 studies were clinical studies in humans. For details of the review process, please see the flowchart in Figure 1.
